NEET Chemistry: Chemical Kinetics - Exam-Focused Flashcards

NEETEnglishFlashcards

Card 1

Define the term 'rate of a chemical reaction'.

The rate of a chemical reaction is the change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time. It is expressed as: rate = -Δ[Reactant]/Δt or rate = Δ[Product]/Δt, where Δ[Reactant] and Δ[Product] are the changes in concentration, and Δt is the time interval. The negative sign for reactants indicates their concentration decreases over time.

Card 2

What is the unit of rate of reaction for a gaseous reaction?

For gaseous reactions, the rate of reaction is often expressed in terms of partial pressure. The unit is atmosphere per second (atm s⁻¹) or pascal per second (Pa s⁻¹), as pressure is directly proportional to concentration for gases (ideal gas law: PV = nRT).

Card 3

Explain the difference between average rate and instantaneous rate of a reaction.

The **average rate** is the change in concentration over a finite time interval (Δt), calculated as -Δ[Reactant]/Δt. The **instantaneous rate** is the rate at a specific moment in time, obtained by taking the limit of Δt approaching zero (i.e., the slope of the tangent to the concentration-time curve at that point). Mathematically, it is expressed as -d[Reactant]/dt.

Card 4

State the rate law for a general reaction: aA + bB → cC + dD.

The rate law expresses the rate of reaction in terms of the concentrations of reactants raised to experimentally determined powers. For the reaction aA + bB → cC + dD, the rate law is: rate = k[A]^m[B]^n, where k is the rate constant, and m and n are the **orders of the reaction** with respect to A and B, respectively. Note: m and n are **not** necessarily equal to the stoichiometric coefficients a and b.

Card 5

What is the order of a reaction? How is it determined?

The **order of a reaction** is the sum of the exponents of the concentration terms in the rate law. For example, if rate = k[A]^m[B]^n, the order is m + n. Order is determined **experimentally** by measuring how the rate changes with varying concentrations of reactants. It cannot be predicted from the balanced chemical equation alone.

Card 6

Define zero-order reaction. Give its rate law and an example.

A **zero-order reaction** is one where the rate is independent of the concentration of the reactant(s). The rate law is: rate = k, where k is the rate constant (units: mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹). Example: The decomposition of ammonia on a platinum surface (2NH₃ → N₂ + 3H₂) is zero-order with respect to NH₃ at high pressures.

Card 7

Derive the integrated rate law for a zero-order reaction.

For a zero-order reaction: rate = -d[A]/dt = k. Rearranging and integrating from [A]₀ (initial concentration) to [A] (concentration at time t): ∫[A]₀[A] d[A] = -k ∫₀ᵗ dt. This yields: [A] = [A]₀ - kt. The integrated rate law shows that the concentration of A decreases linearly with time.

Card 8

What is the half-life of a zero-order reaction? Derive its expression.

The **half-life (t₁/₂)** is the time required for the concentration of a reactant to reduce to half its initial value. For a zero-order reaction, using the integrated rate law [A] = [A]₀ - kt, set [A] = [A]₀/2: [A]₀/2 = [A]₀ - kt₁/₂. Solving for t₁/₂: t₁/₂ = [A]₀ / (2k). Note: The half-life of a zero-order reaction **depends on the initial concentration** of the reactant.

Card 9

Define first-order reaction. Give its rate law and an example.

A **first-order reaction** is one where the rate depends on the concentration of a single reactant raised to the power of 1. The rate law is: rate = k[A], where k is the rate constant (units: s⁻¹). Example: The radioactive decay of carbon-14 (¹⁴C → ¹⁴N + β⁻) is a first-order reaction.

Card 10

Derive the integrated rate law for a first-order reaction.

For a first-order reaction: rate = -d[A]/dt = k[A]. Rearranging and integrating from [A]₀ to [A]: ∫[A]₀[A] d[A]/[A] = -k ∫₀ᵗ dt. This yields: ln[A] = ln[A]₀ - kt. The integrated rate law can also be expressed in exponential form: [A] = [A]₀ e⁻ᵏᵗ.

Card 11

What is the half-life of a first-order reaction? Derive its expression.

For a first-order reaction, using the integrated rate law ln[A] = ln[A]₀ - kt, set [A] = [A]₀/2: ln([A]₀/2) = ln[A]₀ - kt₁/₂. Simplifying: ln(1/2) = -kt₁/₂, so t₁/₂ = ln(2)/k. The half-life of a first-order reaction is **independent of the initial concentration** of the reactant.

Card 12

Explain the concept of pseudo-first-order reactions with an example.

A **pseudo-first-order reaction** occurs when a reaction that is actually of higher order (e.g., second-order) is made to behave like a first-order reaction by keeping one reactant in large excess. For example, the hydrolysis of ethyl acetate (CH₃COOC₂H₅ + H₂O → CH₃COOH + C₂H₅OH) is second-order, but if water is in large excess, its concentration remains nearly constant. The rate law simplifies to rate = k'[CH₃COOC₂H₅], where k' = k[H₂O], making it pseudo-first-order.

Card 13

What is the Arrhenius equation? Explain its terms.

The **Arrhenius equation** relates the rate constant (k) of a reaction to the temperature (T): k = A e^(-Eₐ/RT), where: - A = **pre-exponential factor** (frequency factor, units same as k), - Eₐ = **activation energy** (energy barrier for the reaction, in J mol⁻¹ or kJ mol⁻¹), - R = **universal gas constant** (8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹), - T = **temperature** (in Kelvin). The equation shows that the rate constant increases exponentially with temperature.

Card 14

How can the Arrhenius equation be used to determine activation energy experimentally?

The Arrhenius equation can be linearized by taking the natural logarithm of both sides: ln(k) = ln(A) - (Eₐ/R)(1/T). Plotting ln(k) vs. 1/T yields a straight line with: - Slope = -Eₐ/R (used to calculate Eₐ), - Intercept = ln(A). By measuring the rate constant (k) at different temperatures (T), the activation energy (Eₐ) can be determined from the slope of the plot.

Card 15

What is the collision theory of chemical reactions?

The **collision theory** explains the rate of chemical reactions based on the following postulates: 1. **Molecular collisions**: Reactant molecules must collide for a reaction to occur. 2. **Energy requirement**: Colliding molecules must possess a minimum energy called the **activation energy (Eₐ)** to overcome the energy barrier. 3. **Orientation requirement**: Molecules must collide in the correct orientation for the reaction to proceed. The rate of reaction depends on the **frequency of effective collisions** (those meeting both energy and orientation criteria).

Card 16

Explain the concept of activation energy (Eₐ) with the help of a potential energy diagram.

The **activation energy (Eₐ)** is the minimum energy required for reactants to form an **activated complex** (transition state) before converting into products. In a potential energy diagram: - The x-axis represents the **reaction coordinate** (progress of the reaction). - The y-axis represents the **potential energy** of the system. - Reactants start at a certain energy level, and products end at a lower or higher energy level (depending on whether the reaction is exothermic or endothermic). - The peak of the curve represents the **activated complex**, and the energy difference between the reactants and the peak is Eₐ.
