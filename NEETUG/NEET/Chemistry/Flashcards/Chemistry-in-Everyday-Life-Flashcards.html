NEET Chemistry - Chemistry in Everyday Life Flashcards

NEETEnglishFlashcards

Card 1

Define the term 'drug' in the context of medicinal chemistry.

A drug is a chemical substance of low molecular mass (typically 100–500 u) that interacts with macromolecular targets (such as receptors, enzymes, or nucleic acids) to produce a biological response. When the response is therapeutic and useful, the substance is called a medicine. Drugs can be natural, semi-synthetic, or synthetic in origin.

Card 2

What is the primary difference between an agonist and an antagonist drug?

An agonist is a drug that binds to a receptor and activates it, mimicking the action of the endogenous ligand and producing a biological response. An antagonist, on the other hand, binds to the receptor but does not activate it; instead, it blocks the receptor, preventing the endogenous ligand or an agonist from binding and thus inhibiting the biological response.

Card 3

Name the four main types of drug-target interactions and give one example of each.

1. **Enzyme inhibition**: Drugs inhibit enzyme activity (e.g., aspirin inhibits cyclooxygenase, reducing prostaglandin synthesis). 2. **Receptor binding**: Drugs bind to receptors (e.g., morphine binds to opioid receptors for pain relief). 3. **Ion channel modulation**: Drugs alter ion flow (e.g., local anesthetics like lidocaine block sodium channels). 4. **Nucleic acid interaction**: Drugs interfere with DNA/RNA (e.g., cisplatin cross-links DNA in cancer cells).

Card 4

Explain the role of enzymes as drug targets. How do competitive and non-competitive inhibitors differ?

Enzymes catalyze biochemical reactions and are common drug targets. Drugs can inhibit enzyme activity to modulate physiological processes. - **Competitive inhibitors** bind reversibly to the active site of the enzyme, competing with the substrate (e.g., statins inhibit HMG-CoA reductase to lower cholesterol). - **Non-competitive inhibitors** bind to a site other than the active site (allosteric site), altering the enzyme's shape and reducing its activity (e.g., allopurinol inhibits xanthine oxidase to treat gout).

Card 5

What are antacids? Give two examples and explain their mechanism of action.

Antacids are drugs that neutralize excess stomach acid (hydrochloric acid) to relieve symptoms of acidity, heartburn, or gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD). Examples: 1. **Sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO₃)**: Reacts with HCl to form NaCl, CO₂, and water, rapidly neutralizing acid. 2. **Magnesium hydroxide (Mg(OH)₂)**: Reacts with HCl to form MgCl₂ and water, providing longer-lasting relief. Mechanism: Antacids are weak bases that chemically neutralize stomach acid, raising the pH of gastric contents.

Card 6

Differentiate between analgesics and antipyretics. Give one example of each.

- **Analgesics** are drugs that relieve pain without causing loss of consciousness (e.g., aspirin, ibuprofen). - **Antipyretics** are drugs that reduce fever by acting on the hypothalamus (e.g., paracetamol/acetaminophen). Some drugs, like aspirin and ibuprofen, have both analgesic and antipyretic properties, while paracetamol is primarily an antipyretic with mild analgesic effects.

Card 7

What are antimicrobials? Classify them based on their action and give one example for each class.

Antimicrobials are drugs that kill or inhibit the growth of microorganisms (bacteria, viruses, fungi, or protozoa). Classification: 1. **Antibiotics**: Kill or inhibit bacteria (e.g., penicillin). 2. **Antivirals**: Inhibit viral replication (e.g., acyclovir for herpes). 3. **Antifungals**: Kill or inhibit fungi (e.g., fluconazole for yeast infections). 4. **Antiprotozoals**: Kill or inhibit protozoa (e.g., metronidazole for amoebiasis).

Card 8

Explain the mechanism of action of antibiotics with reference to penicillin.

Penicillin is a β-lactam antibiotic that inhibits bacterial cell wall synthesis. It binds to **penicillin-binding proteins (PBPs)**, which are enzymes (transpeptidases) responsible for cross-linking peptidoglycan chains in the bacterial cell wall. By inhibiting PBPs, penicillin weakens the cell wall, leading to osmotic lysis and bacterial death. Penicillin is most effective against **Gram-positive bacteria** (e.g., *Streptococcus*), which have a thick peptidoglycan layer.

Card 9

What are broad-spectrum antibiotics? Give one example and mention their advantages and disadvantages.

Broad-spectrum antibiotics are effective against a wide range of bacteria, including both Gram-positive and Gram-negative species. Example: **Tetracycline**. **Advantages**: Useful when the causative bacteria is unknown or in mixed infections. **Disadvantages**: Can disrupt the body's natural microbiome, leading to secondary infections (e.g., *Clostridium difficile* colitis) and contribute to antibiotic resistance.

Card 10

Define drug resistance. What are the two main mechanisms by which bacteria develop resistance to antibiotics?

Drug resistance is the ability of microorganisms to withstand the effects of a drug that was previously effective. Mechanisms of bacterial antibiotic resistance: 1. **Genetic mutations**: Bacteria acquire mutations in genes encoding drug targets (e.g., altered PBPs in MRSA resist penicillin) or drug-metabolizing enzymes (e.g., β-lactamases that degrade penicillin). 2. **Horizontal gene transfer**: Bacteria acquire resistance genes from other bacteria via plasmids (e.g., genes for β-lactamases or efflux pumps).

Card 11

What are antiseptics and disinfectants? Give one example of each and state their uses.

- **Antiseptics**: Chemicals applied to living tissues (e.g., skin) to kill or inhibit microorganisms and prevent infection. Example: **Povidone-iodine** (used for wound cleaning and surgical scrubs). - **Disinfectants**: Chemicals used on inanimate objects or surfaces to kill microorganisms. Example: **Phenol** (used to disinfect floors, instruments). Note: Some chemicals (e.g., hydrogen peroxide) can be used as both antiseptics and disinfectants at different concentrations.

Card 12

Explain the difference between soaps and synthetic detergents in terms of their chemical structure and cleansing action.

- **Soaps**: Sodium or potassium salts of fatty acids (e.g., sodium stearate, C₁₇H₃₅COO⁻Na⁺). They are biodegradable but form scum with hard water (Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺ ions), reducing their effectiveness. - **Synthetic detergents**: Sodium salts of alkylbenzene sulfonates (e.g., sodium lauryl sulfate, C₁₂H₂₅OSO₃⁻Na⁺). They do not form scum with hard water and are more effective in acidic or saline conditions. **Cleansing action**: Both soaps and detergents have a hydrophobic tail (non-polar) and a hydrophilic head (polar). The tail dissolves grease/oil, while the head interacts with water, forming micelles that emulsify and wash away dirt.

Card 13

What are food preservatives? Give two examples and explain how they work.

Food preservatives are chemicals added to food to prevent spoilage caused by microbial growth, oxidation, or enzymatic reactions. Examples: 1. **Sodium benzoate**: Inhibits the growth of yeast, mold, and some bacteria by lowering intracellular pH and interfering with enzyme activity. Used in acidic foods (e.g., soft drinks, pickles). 2. **Sulfur dioxide (SO₂)**: Acts as an antimicrobial and antioxidant. It inhibits microbial enzymes and prevents browning in dried fruits and wines.

Card 14

Name two artificial sweeteners and state their advantages over natural sugars.

1. **Aspartame**: A dipeptide (200 times sweeter than sucrose) used in diet sodas and sugar-free products. Advantages: Low calorie, does not promote tooth decay, and suitable for diabetics. 2. **Saccharin**: A non-nutritive sweetener (300–400 times sweeter than sucrose) used in tabletop sweeteners. Advantages: Stable at high temperatures (useful in cooking/baking), zero calories, and does not affect blood sugar levels. **Note**: Excessive use of artificial sweeteners may have health concerns (e.g., aspartame is not recommended for individuals with phenylketonuria).

Card 15

What are antioxidants? Give two examples and explain their role in food preservation.

Antioxidants are chemicals that prevent or slow down the oxidation of food, which can cause rancidity, discoloration, or loss of nutritional value. Examples: 1. **Butylated hydroxyanisole (BHA)**: Prevents oxidation of fats and oils by scavenging free radicals. Used in packaged snacks, cereals, and chewing gum. 2. **Ascorbic acid (Vitamin C)**: Inhibits enzymatic browning in fruits and vegetables by acting as a reducing agent. Also used in fruit juices and canned foods. **Role**: Antioxidants donate electrons to free radicals, stabilizing them and preventing chain reactions that lead to spoilage.
