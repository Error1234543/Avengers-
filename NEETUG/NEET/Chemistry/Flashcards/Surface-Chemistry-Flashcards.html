NEET Chemistry - Surface Chemistry Flashcards

NEETEnglishFlashcards

Card 1

Define adsorption and distinguish it from absorption with an example.

Adsorption is the accumulation of molecules of a substance (adsorbate) on the surface of a solid or liquid (adsorbent) due to unbalanced surface forces, e.g., activated charcoal adsorbing toxic gases. Absorption involves the entire volume of the substance, e.g., water absorbed by a sponge. Adsorption is surface-specific, while absorption is bulk-specific.

Card 2

What are the two types of adsorption? Give one example of each.

1. **Physisorption (Physical adsorption)**: Weak van der Waals forces, reversible, e.g., nitrogen gas adsorbed on charcoal at low temperatures. 2. **Chemisorption (Chemical adsorption)**: Strong chemical bonds (covalent/ionic), irreversible, e.g., oxygen adsorbed on tungsten forming a surface oxide layer.

Card 3

Explain the role of surface area in adsorption. How does it affect the process?

Adsorption is a surface phenomenon; thus, greater surface area increases adsorption capacity. Finely divided solids (e.g., activated charcoal, silica gel) or porous materials provide more active sites for adsorbate molecules, enhancing adsorption efficiency. Example: Powdered charcoal adsorbs more gas than a charcoal lump.

Card 4

State Freundlich adsorption isotherm and explain its terms. Under what conditions does it fail?

Freundlich isotherm: **x/m = kP^(1/n)**, where *x* = mass of adsorbate, *m* = mass of adsorbent, *P* = pressure, *k* and *n* = constants (n > 1). It fails at high pressures or low temperatures where multilayer adsorption occurs, as it assumes monolayer adsorption only.

Card 5

What is an adsorption isotherm? Sketch the typical isotherm for physisorption and chemisorption.

An adsorption isotherm plots the amount of adsorbate on the adsorbent (*x/m*) vs. pressure (*P*) at constant temperature. **Physisorption**: Shows a gradual rise and plateau (multilayer formation). **Chemisorption**: Sharp initial rise, then plateau (monolayer saturation). Card 6

Define catalysts and classify them with examples. How do they affect reaction rates?

Catalysts are substances that alter reaction rates without being consumed. **Types**: 1. **Positive catalysts**: Speed up reactions (e.g., MnO₂ in H₂O₂ decomposition). 2. **Negative catalysts (Inhibitors)**: Slow down reactions (e.g., glycerol in H₂O₂ decomposition). 3. **Homogeneous**: Same phase as reactants (e.g., H⁺ in ester hydrolysis). 4. **Heterogeneous**: Different phase (e.g., Fe in Haber process). They lower activation energy by providing alternative pathways.

Card 7

Explain the mechanism of heterogeneous catalysis with an example.

Heterogeneous catalysis involves **adsorption of reactants** on the catalyst surface, **formation of intermediate complexes**, **reaction on the surface**, and **desorption of products**. Example: **Haber process** (N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃) on iron catalyst: 1. N₂ and H₂ adsorb on Fe surface. 2. Bonds weaken, forming Fe-N and Fe-H intermediates. 3. NH₃ forms and desorbs, regenerating Fe.

Card 8

What are promoters and poisons in catalysis? Give examples.

**Promoters**: Substances that enhance catalyst activity (e.g., Mo in Fe catalyst for Haber process). **Poisons**: Substances that deactivate catalysts (e.g., CO or H₂S in Pt catalyst for hydrogenation). Poisons block active sites, while promoters modify electronic/structural properties.

Card 9

Define colloidal solutions. How are they classified based on the physical state of the dispersed phase and dispersion medium?

Colloidal solutions are heterogeneous mixtures with particle sizes (1 nm–1 µm) intermediate between true solutions and suspensions. **Classification**: 1. **Dispersed phase**: Solid (e.g., gold sol), liquid (e.g., milk), gas (e.g., foam). 2. **Dispersion medium**: Solid (e.g., ruby glass), liquid (e.g., starch sol), gas (e.g., smoke).

Card 10

Explain the Tyndall effect and its significance in distinguishing colloids from true solutions.

The Tyndall effect is the scattering of light by colloidal particles, visible as a bright path (e.g., sunlight through mist). **Significance**: True solutions (particle size < 1 nm) do not scatter light, while colloids (1 nm–1 µm) do. Example: Laser beam visible in milk (colloid) but not in sugar solution (true solution).

Card 11

What is Brownian motion? How does it arise in colloidal solutions?

Brownian motion is the random zigzag movement of colloidal particles due to unequal collisions with molecules of the dispersion medium. It arises from thermal energy, preventing particles from settling under gravity. Example: Pollen grains in water observed under a microscope.

Card 12

Describe the electrical properties of colloids. What is the origin of their charge?

Colloidal particles carry charges due to: 1. **Preferential adsorption of ions** (e.g., AgI sol adsorbs Ag⁺ or I⁻). 2. **Ionization of surface groups** (e.g., soap micelles ionize to RCOO⁻). 3. **Frictional electrification** (e.g., rubbing). The charge stabilizes colloids by repelling like-charged particles (electrostatic stabilization).

Card 13

Explain coagulation of colloids. What are the methods to induce coagulation?

Coagulation is the precipitation of colloidal particles by neutralizing their charge. **Methods**: 1. **Electrophoresis**: Migration to oppositely charged electrodes. 2. **Addition of electrolytes**: Higher valency ions (Hardy-Schulze rule) are more effective (e.g., Al³⁺ > Ba²⁺ > Na⁺). 3. **Boiling**: Increases particle collisions. 4. **Mixing oppositely charged colloids**: Mutual neutralization (e.g., Fe(OH)₃ sol + As₂S₃ sol).

Card 14

State Hardy-Schulze rule and explain its significance in coagulation.

Hardy-Schulze rule: The coagulating power of an electrolyte increases with the valency of the active ion (opposite to the colloid’s charge). **Significance**: For negatively charged colloids (e.g., As₂S₃), Al³⁺ (valency 3) is more effective than Ba²⁺ (valency 2) or Na⁺ (valency 1). Example: AlCl₃ coagulates As₂S₃ sol faster than NaCl.

Card 15

What are emulsions? How are they classified? Give one example of each type.

Emulsions are colloidal dispersions of two immiscible liquids. **Types**: 1. **Oil-in-water (O/W)**: Oil droplets in water (e.g., milk, vanishing cream). 2. **Water-in-oil (W/O)**: Water droplets in oil (e.g., butter, cold cream). Emulsifiers (e.g., soaps, proteins) stabilize emulsions by reducing interfacial tension.

Card 16

Explain the role of emulsifiers in stabilizing emulsions. Give an example.

Emulsifiers (surfactants) stabilize emulsions by: 1. **Reducing interfacial tension** between oil and water. 2. **Forming a protective film** around droplets (e.g., soap molecules with hydrophilic heads and hydrophobic tails). Example: **Sodium stearate** in soap stabilizes O/W emulsions by orienting its hydrophilic COO⁻ group toward water and hydrophobic tail toward oil.

Card 17

What is gel? Give two examples and explain its structure.

A gel is a colloidal system where a liquid is dispersed in a solid medium, forming a semi-rigid network. **Examples**: 1. **Silica gel** (water in SiO₂). 2. **Gelatin** (water in protein). **Structure**: Solid particles form a 3D network trapping liquid (e.g., gelatin molecules cross-link to hold water). Gels exhibit **thixotropy** (reversible sol-gel transition).

Card 18

Describe the preparation of colloids by Bredig’s arc method. Which type of colloids is it used for?

Bredig’s arc method prepares **metal sols** (e.g., gold, silver) by striking an electric arc between metal electrodes submerged in a cold dispersion medium (e.g., water). **Process**: 1. Arc vaporizes metal, forming atoms. 2. Atoms condense into colloidal particles stabilized by the medium. **Note**: Used for metals with high melting points.

Card 19

Explain peptization and give an example.

Peptization is the conversion of a precipitate into a colloidal sol by adding a small amount of electrolyte (peptizing agent). **Example**: Freshly precipitated Fe(OH)₃ is converted to a sol by adding FeCl₃ (peptizing agent), which adsorbs Fe³⁺ ions, imparting charge and dispersing particles.
