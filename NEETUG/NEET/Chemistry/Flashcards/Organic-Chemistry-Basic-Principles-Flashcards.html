NEETEnglishFlashcards

Card 1

Define 'organic chemistry' and explain its significance in the context of NEET.

Organic chemistry is the branch of chemistry that studies the structure, properties, composition, reactions, and preparation of carbon-containing compounds, including hydrocarbons and their derivatives. In NEET, it is significant because it forms the basis for understanding biomolecules (carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, nucleic acids), drugs, polymers, and natural products, which are crucial for both Chemistry and Biology sections.

Card 2

What is the concept of 'catenation'? Why is carbon uniquely suited for this property?

Catenation is the ability of an element to form long chains or rings by bonding with atoms of the same element. Carbon is uniquely suited for catenation due to its: (1) small atomic size, which allows strong C-C bonds; (2) tetravalency, enabling it to form four covalent bonds; (3) high bond dissociation energy (348 kJ/mol for C-C bonds), making the bonds stable; and (4) ability to form single, double, and triple bonds with itself and other elements like H, O, N, and halogens.

Card 3

Differentiate between 'homologous series' and 'functional group' with examples.

A **homologous series** is a family of organic compounds with the same general formula, similar chemical properties, and a gradual change in physical properties as the molecular mass increases. Each successive member differs by a -CH₂- unit. Example: Alkanes (CH₄, C₂H₆, C₃H₈). A **functional group** is a specific group of atoms within a molecule that determines its characteristic chemical properties and reactions. Example: -OH (hydroxyl group) in alcohols (CH₃OH, C₂H₅OH). Key difference: Homologous series is a *series* of compounds, while a functional group is a *part* of a compound.

Card 4

Explain the IUPAC nomenclature rules for naming alkanes with an example.

IUPAC nomenclature for alkanes follows these rules: 1. **Longest chain rule**: Identify the longest continuous carbon chain (parent chain). Example: For CH₃-CH₂-CH(CH₃)-CH₃, the longest chain is 4 carbons (butane). 2. **Numbering**: Number the chain from the end nearest to the first substituent. Example: CH₃-CH(CH₃)-CH₂-CH₃ is numbered from left to right (2-methylbutane). 3. **Substituents**: Name substituents (alkyl groups) as prefixes with their position. Example: CH₃-CH(CH₃)-CH₂-CH₃ is 2-methylbutane. 4. **Alphabetical order**: List substituents in alphabetical order (ignore prefixes like di-, tri-). Example: CH₃-CH(Br)-CH(CH₃)-CH₃ is 2-bromo-3-methylbutane. 5. **Suffix**: Use '-ane' for alkanes. Example: C₅H₁₂ is pentane.

Card 5

What are 'structural isomers'? Draw all possible structural isomers for C₅H₁₂ and name them.

Structural isomers are compounds with the same molecular formula but different structural arrangements of atoms (different connectivity). For C₅H₁₂ (pentane), the three structural isomers are: 1. **Pentane**: CH₃-CH₂-CH₂-CH₂-CH₃ (straight-chain). 2. **Isopentane (2-methylbutane)**: CH₃-CH(CH₃)-CH₂-CH₃ (branched). 3. **Neopentane (2,2-dimethylpropane)**: CH₃-C(CH₃)₂-CH₃ (highly branched). Key point: Isomers differ in physical properties (e.g., boiling points) and chemical reactivity.

Card 6

Define 'stereoisomers' and differentiate between 'conformational' and 'configurational' isomers with examples.

**Stereoisomers** are compounds with the same molecular formula and sequence of bonded atoms (constitution) but different 3D orientations. 1. **Conformational isomers**: Interconvert by rotation around single bonds (no bond breaking). Example: Ethane’s staggered vs. eclipsed conformations (energy difference due to torsional strain). 2. **Configurational isomers**: Cannot interconvert without breaking bonds. Subtypes: - **Geometric (cis-trans) isomers**: Due to restricted rotation around C=C bonds. Example: Cis-2-butene (CH₃-CH=CH-CH₃, same side) vs. trans-2-butene (opposite sides). - **Optical isomers (enantiomers)**: Mirror images that are non-superimposable. Example: Lactic acid (CH₃-CH(OH)-COOH) has D- and L-forms.

Card 7

Explain the concept of 'hybridization' in organic compounds. What is the hybridization of carbon in methane (CH₄), ethene (C₂H₄), and ethyne (C₂H₂)?

Hybridization is the mixing of atomic orbitals to form new hybrid orbitals suitable for bonding. It explains the geometry and bonding in organic compounds: 1. **Methane (CH₄)**: Carbon is **sp³ hybridized**. One 2s orbital mixes with three 2p orbitals to form four sp³ hybrid orbitals (tetrahedral geometry, 109.5° bond angles). 2. **Ethene (C₂H₄)**: Carbon is **sp² hybridized**. One 2s orbital mixes with two 2p orbitals to form three sp² hybrid orbitals (trigonal planar geometry, 120° bond angles). The unhybridized 2p orbital forms the π-bond in C=C. 3. **Ethyne (C₂H₂)**: Carbon is **sp hybridized**. One 2s orbital mixes with one 2p orbital to form two sp hybrid orbitals (linear geometry, 180° bond angles). The two unhybridized 2p orbitals form two π-bonds in C≡C.

Card 8

What is 'resonance'? Draw the resonance structures of benzene and explain its stability.

Resonance is the delocalization of π-electrons in molecules with alternating single and double bonds, represented by multiple Lewis structures (resonance structures). The actual structure is a hybrid of these forms. **Benzene (C₆H₆)**: Two equivalent resonance structures (Kekulé structures) show alternating C=C bonds. The true structure is a hybrid with delocalized π-electrons over all six carbons (represented by a circle inside the hexagon). **Stability**: Resonance stabilizes benzene due to: 1. **Delocalization energy**: The energy difference between the hybrid and the most stable resonance structure (~36 kcal/mol). 2. **Equal bond lengths**: All C-C bonds are 1.39 Å (intermediate between single and double bonds). 3. **Aromaticity**: Benzene follows Hückel’s rule (4n+2 π-electrons, n=1) for extra stability.

Card 9

Define 'inductive effect' and differentiate between +I and -I effects with examples.

The **inductive effect** is the permanent polarization of a σ-bond due to the electronegativity difference between bonded atoms, leading to a partial charge separation. It weakens with distance. 1. **+I effect (electron-donating)**: Atoms/groups push electron density toward the carbon chain. Example: Alkyl groups (-CH₃, -C₂H₅) in CH₃-CH₂-Cl increase electron density on Cl, making it less δ⁻. 2. **-I effect (electron-withdrawing)**: Atoms/groups pull electron density away from the carbon chain. Example: Halogens (-F, -Cl), -NO₂, -CN in CH₃-CH₂-Cl make the carbon chain δ⁺. **Key point**: -I effect increases acidity (e.g., chloroacetic acid is stronger than acetic acid), while +I effect decreases acidity.

Card 10

Explain 'electromeric effect' with an example. How does it differ from the inductive effect?

The **electromeric effect** is the temporary polarization of a π-bond in the presence of an attacking reagent, leading to complete electron transfer. It is a **temporary** effect (unlike the permanent inductive effect). **Example**: In ethene (CH₂=CH₂), when a proton (H⁺) approaches, the π-electrons shift toward H⁺, forming a carbocation (CH₃-CH₂⁺). **Differences from inductive effect**: 1. **Temporary vs. permanent**: Electromeric effect occurs only during reactions; inductive effect is always present. 2. **π-bonds vs. σ-bonds**: Electromeric effect involves π-electrons; inductive effect involves σ-bonds. 3. **Complete vs. partial transfer**: Electromeric effect leads to complete electron transfer; inductive effect causes partial polarization.
