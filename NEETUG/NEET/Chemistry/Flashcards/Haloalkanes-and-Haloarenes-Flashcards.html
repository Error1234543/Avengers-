NEET Chemistry: Haloalkanes and Haloarenes - Exam-Focused Flashcards

NEETEnglishFlashcards

Card 1

Define haloalkanes and haloarenes. How are they classified based on the number of halogen atoms?

Haloalkanes (alkyl halides) are organic compounds where one or more hydrogen atoms in an alkane are replaced by halogen atoms (F, Cl, Br, I). Haloarenes (aryl halides) are aromatic compounds where a halogen atom is directly bonded to an aromatic ring (e.g., chlorobenzene). Classification based on the number of halogen atoms: - **Mono-haloalkanes**: One halogen atom (e.g., CH₃Cl, methyl chloride). - **Di-haloalkanes**: Two halogen atoms (e.g., CH₂Cl₂, methylene dichloride). - **Tri-haloalkanes**: Three halogen atoms (e.g., CHCl₃, chloroform). - **Poly-haloalkanes**: More than three halogen atoms (e.g., CCl₄, carbon tetrachloride). Haloarenes are typically mono-substituted (e.g., C₆H₅Cl) due to the stability of the aromatic ring.

Card 2

Explain the nomenclature rules for haloalkanes as per IUPAC. Provide an example.

IUPAC nomenclature for haloalkanes follows these rules: 1. Identify the longest carbon chain containing the halogen. 2. Number the chain to give the halogen the lowest possible locant. 3. Name the halogen as a prefix (fluoro-, chloro-, bromo-, iodo-) with its position number. 4. If multiple halogens are present, list them in alphabetical order with their locants. 5. Use suffixes like -di, -tri for identical halogens. Example: CH₃-CH(Br)-CH₂-CH₃ is named **2-bromobutane** (not 3-bromobutane, as the chain is numbered from the end nearest the halogen). For haloarenes: The halogen is treated as a substituent on the benzene ring (e.g., C₆H₅Br is **bromobenzene**).

Card 3

Describe the nature of the C-X bond in haloalkanes. How does bond polarity affect reactivity?

The C-X bond in haloalkanes is **polar covalent** due to the electronegativity difference between carbon (2.5) and halogens (F: 4.0, Cl: 3.0, Br: 2.8, I: 2.5). This creates a partial positive charge (δ⁺) on carbon and a partial negative charge (δ⁻) on the halogen. **Effects of bond polarity on reactivity:** - **Nucleophilic substitution (Sₙ1/Sₙ2)**: The δ⁺ carbon is susceptible to attack by nucleophiles (e.g., OH⁻, CN⁻). - **Elimination reactions (E1/E2)**: The polar bond weakens adjacent C-H bonds, facilitating elimination. - **Bond strength**: C-X bond strength decreases down the group (C-F > C-Cl > C-Br > C-I), making iodoalkanes the most reactive in substitution/elimination. - **Leaving group ability**: Weaker C-X bonds (e.g., C-I) make better leaving groups (I⁻ > Br⁻ > Cl⁻ > F⁻).

Card 4

Compare Sₙ1 and Sₙ2 mechanisms for nucleophilic substitution in haloalkanes. Include rate laws, stereochemistry, and substrate preference.

**Sₙ2 (Bimolecular Nucleophilic Substitution):** - **Mechanism**: Concerted one-step process where the nucleophile attacks from the backside, displacing the leaving group. - **Rate Law**: Rate = k[Substrate][Nucleophile] (second-order kinetics). - **Stereochemistry**: Inversion of configuration (Walden inversion). - **Substrate Preference**: Primary > Secondary (tertiary halides do not undergo Sₙ2 due to steric hindrance). - **Solvent**: Polar aprotic solvents (e.g., DMSO, acetone) favor Sₙ2 by not solvating the nucleophile. **Sₙ1 (Unimolecular Nucleophilic Substitution):** - **Mechanism**: Two-step process: (1) Ionization of C-X bond to form a carbocation intermediate, (2) Nucleophilic attack. - **Rate Law**: Rate = k[Substrate] (first-order kinetics). - **Stereochemistry**: Racemization (planar carbocation allows attack from both sides). - **Substrate Preference**: Tertiary > Secondary (primary halides rarely undergo Sₙ1 due to unstable carbocations). - **Solvent**: Polar protic solvents (e.g., water, alcohols) stabilize the carbocation intermediate.

Card 5

What are the key differences between elimination reactions E1 and E2 in haloalkanes?

**E2 (Bimolecular Elimination):** - **Mechanism**: Concerted one-step process where a base removes a β-hydrogen, and the leaving group departs simultaneously, forming a double bond. - **Rate Law**: Rate = k[Substrate][Base] (second-order kinetics). - **Stereochemistry**: Anti-periplanar arrangement of H and X is required (e.g., in cyclohexane, H and X must be trans-diaxial). - **Substrate Preference**: Primary > Secondary > Tertiary (though tertiary halides can undergo E2 if the base is strong). - **Base**: Strong, bulky bases (e.g., t-butoxide) favor E2 over Sₙ2. - **Product**: Follows Zaitsev’s rule (more substituted alkene is major product). **E1 (Unimolecular Elimination):** - **Mechanism**: Two-step process: (1) Ionization of C-X bond to form a carbocation, (2) Base removes a β-hydrogen to form a double bond. - **Rate Law**: Rate = k[Substrate] (first-order kinetics). - **Stereochemistry**: No specific requirement (carbocation is planar). - **Substrate Preference**: Tertiary > Secondary (primary halides rarely undergo E1). - **Base**: Weak bases (e.g., water, alcohols) favor E1. - **Product**: Follows Zaitsev’s rule, but rearrangements are possible due to carbocation intermediates.

Card 6

Explain the preparation of haloalkanes from alcohols. Include reagents and conditions for different halogens.

Haloalkanes can be prepared from alcohols using hydrogen halides (HX) or phosphorus halides (PX₃/PX₅) or thionyl chloride (SOCl₂). **1. Using Hydrogen Halides (HX):** - **Reagents**: HCl, HBr, HI (HF is rarely used due to poor reactivity). - **Mechanism**: Sₙ1 or Sₙ2 depending on the alcohol (primary: Sₙ2; tertiary: Sₙ1). - **Conditions**: Heat and a catalyst (e.g., ZnCl₂ for HCl, known as Lucas reagent). - **Example**: CH₃CH₂OH + HCl → CH₃CH₂Cl + H₂O (requires ZnCl₂). - **Order of Reactivity**: HI > HBr > HCl (due to bond strength). **2. Using Phosphorus Halides:** - **Reagents**: PCl₃, PCl₅, PBr₃, PI₃. - **Mechanism**: Sₙ2 for primary/secondary alcohols. - **Conditions**: Room temperature or mild heating. - **Example**: 3CH₃CH₂OH + PCl₃ → 3CH₃CH₂Cl + H₃PO₃. - **Advantage**: No carbocation rearrangements. **3. Using Thionyl Chloride (SOCl₂):** - **Reagents**: SOCl₂ in the presence of pyridine. - **Mechanism**: Sₙ2 with retention of configuration (due to internal nucleophilic substitution). - **Conditions**: Room temperature. - **Example**: CH₃CH₂OH + SOCl₂ → CH₃CH₂Cl + SO₂ + HCl. - **Advantage**: Gaseous byproducts (SO₂, HCl) drive the reaction forward.
