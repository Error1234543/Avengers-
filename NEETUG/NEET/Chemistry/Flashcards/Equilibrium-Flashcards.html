NEET Chemistry - Equilibrium Flashcards (Exam-Focused)

NEETEnglishFlashcards

Card 1

Define chemical equilibrium.

Chemical equilibrium is the state in a reversible reaction where the rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the reverse reaction, resulting in constant concentrations of reactants and products over time. This is a dynamic equilibrium, meaning both reactions continue to occur, but there is no net change in concentrations.

Card 2

State the law of mass action and write its mathematical expression for a general reaction: aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD.

The law of mass action states that the rate of a chemical reaction is directly proportional to the product of the active masses of the reactants, each raised to the power of their stoichiometric coefficients. For the reaction aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD, the equilibrium constant expression is: Kc = [C]^c [D]^d / [A]^a [B]^b, where [ ] denotes equilibrium concentrations in mol/L.

Card 3

What is the difference between Kc and Kp? Write the relationship between them.

Kc is the equilibrium constant expressed in terms of molar concentrations, while Kp is expressed in terms of partial pressures (for gaseous reactions). The relationship between Kp and Kc is: Kp = Kc (RT)^Δn, where R is the gas constant (0.0821 L·atm·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹), T is temperature in Kelvin, and Δn is the change in moles of gas (moles of products - moles of reactants).

Card 4

For the reaction N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g), if Kc = 0.5 at 400 K, what is the value of Kp at the same temperature? (R = 0.0821 L·atm·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹)

Δn = 2 - (1 + 3) = -2. Using Kp = Kc (RT)^Δn: Kp = 0.5 × (0.0821 × 400)^(-2) = 0.5 × (32.84)^(-2) = 0.5 × 9.23 × 10⁻⁴ = 4.615 × 10⁻⁴.

Card 5

What is the reaction quotient (Q)? How does it determine the direction of a reaction?

The reaction quotient (Q) is the ratio of the product of the concentrations (or partial pressures) of the products to the reactants, each raised to their stoichiometric coefficients, at any point during the reaction. If Q < K, the reaction proceeds forward to reach equilibrium. If Q > K, the reaction proceeds in reverse. If Q = K, the reaction is at equilibrium.

Card 6

State Le Chatelier’s principle and explain its application to concentration changes.

Le Chatelier’s principle states that if a dynamic equilibrium is disturbed by changing the conditions (concentration, pressure, temperature), the system adjusts to counteract the change and restore equilibrium. For concentration: Increasing reactant concentration shifts equilibrium to the right (toward products). Increasing product concentration shifts equilibrium to the left (toward reactants).

Card 7

How does a change in pressure affect the equilibrium of the reaction: N₂(g) + 3H₂(g) ⇌ 2NH₃(g)?

The reaction involves 4 moles of gas on the left and 2 moles on the right. Increasing pressure shifts equilibrium toward the side with fewer moles of gas (right, toward NH₃) to reduce pressure. Decreasing pressure shifts equilibrium toward the side with more moles of gas (left, toward N₂ and H₂).

Card 8

Explain the effect of temperature on the equilibrium of an exothermic reaction.

For an exothermic reaction (ΔH < 0), heat is a product. Increasing temperature shifts equilibrium to the left (toward reactants) to absorb the added heat. Decreasing temperature shifts equilibrium to the right (toward products) to release heat and restore equilibrium.

Card 9

What is the common ion effect? Provide an example.

The common ion effect is the suppression of the dissociation of a weak electrolyte (e.g., weak acid or base) when a strong electrolyte containing a common ion is added. Example: Adding sodium acetate (CH₃COONa) to acetic acid (CH₃COOH) solution decreases the dissociation of CH₃COOH, shifting equilibrium left: CH₃COOH ⇌ CH₃COO⁻ + H⁺.

Card 10

Define solubility product (Ksp) and write its expression for AgCl(s) ⇌ Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq).

The solubility product (Ksp) is the product of the concentrations of the ions of a sparingly soluble salt in a saturated solution, each raised to the power of their stoichiometric coefficients. For AgCl: Ksp = [Ag⁺][Cl⁻].

Card 11

For Ag₂CrO₄, Ksp = 1.1 × 10⁻¹² at 25°C. Calculate its solubility in mol/L.

Ag₂CrO₄ ⇌ 2Ag⁺ + CrO₄²⁻. Let solubility = s mol/L. Then [Ag⁺] = 2s, [CrO₄²⁻] = s. Ksp = (2s)²(s) = 4s³ = 1.1 × 10⁻¹². Solving: s³ = 2.75 × 10⁻¹³, s = (2.75 × 10⁻¹³)^(1/3) ≈ 6.5 × 10⁻⁵ mol/L.

Card 12

What is the relationship between solubility (s) and Ksp for a salt of the type AB₂?

For AB₂ ⇌ A²⁺ + 2B⁻, Ksp = [A²⁺][B⁻]² = s × (2s)² = 4s³. Thus, s = (Ksp / 4)^(1/3).

Card 13

Define pH and pOH. Write their relationship with [H⁺] and [OH⁻].

pH = -log[H⁺], where [H⁺] is the hydrogen ion concentration. pOH = -log[OH⁻], where [OH⁻] is the hydroxide ion concentration. At 25°C, pH + pOH = 14, due to Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻] = 1 × 10⁻¹⁴.

Card 14

Calculate the pH of a 0.01 M HCl solution.

HCl is a strong acid and fully dissociates: [H⁺] = 0.01 M. pH = -log(0.01) = 2.

Card 15

What is the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation? Write it for a weak acid HA.

The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation relates pH to the pKa of a weak acid and the ratio of its conjugate base to acid: pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA]), where [A⁻] is the concentration of the conjugate base and [HA] is the concentration of the weak acid.

Card 16

For a buffer solution containing 0.1 M CH₃COOH (pKa = 4.74) and 0.2 M CH₃COONa, calculate the pH.

Using Henderson-Hasselbalch: pH = pKa + log([CH₃COO⁻]/[CH₃COOH]) = 4.74 + log(0.2/0.1) = 4.74 + log(2) = 4.74 + 0.301 = 5.041.

Card 17

What is the degree of dissociation (α) for a weak electrolyte? How is it related to the dissociation constant (Ka)?

The degree of dissociation (α) is the fraction of the total molecules that dissociate into ions. For a weak acid HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻, Ka = [H⁺][A⁻]/[HA] = (Cα)(Cα)/(C(1-α)) = Cα²/(1-α), where C is the initial concentration. For very small α, Ka ≈ Cα².

Card 18

Explain the concept of buffer capacity.

Buffer capacity is the measure of a buffer solution’s ability to resist changes in pH when small amounts of acid or base are added. It depends on the concentrations of the weak acid and its conjugate base: higher concentrations yield higher buffer capacity. Buffer capacity is maximum when [acid] = [conjugate base], i.e., pH = pKa.
