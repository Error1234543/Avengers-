Card 1

Define the term 'intermolecular forces'. How do they differ from intramolecular forces?

Intermolecular forces are the attractive or repulsive forces that exist **between** molecules, influencing physical properties like boiling point, melting point, and solubility. Intramolecular forces, on the other hand, are the **chemical bonds** (e.g., covalent, ionic) that hold atoms **within** a molecule together. Intermolecular forces are generally weaker than intramolecular forces but are critical in determining the state of matter (solid, liquid, gas).

Card 2

List the three primary types of intermolecular forces in order of increasing strength. Provide an example for each.

1. **London dispersion forces (weakest)**: Present in all molecules, including non-polar ones (e.g., noble gases like He, or non-polar molecules like CH₄). 2. **Dipole-dipole interactions**: Occur between polar molecules (e.g., HCl, where the H end is partially positive and the Cl end is partially negative). 3. **Hydrogen bonding (strongest)**: A special dipole-dipole interaction between hydrogen and highly electronegative atoms (N, O, F) in adjacent molecules (e.g., H₂O, NH₃).

Card 3

Explain the concept of 'ideal gas' and state the conditions under which a real gas behaves ideally.

An **ideal gas** is a hypothetical gas that perfectly follows the **ideal gas law (PV = nRT)** under all conditions. It assumes: 1. Gas molecules have **negligible volume** compared to the container. 2. **No intermolecular forces** exist between molecules. 3. Collisions are **perfectly elastic** (no energy loss). Real gases behave ideally at: - **Low pressures** (molecules are far apart, minimizing intermolecular forces). - **High temperatures** (kinetic energy dominates, reducing the effect of intermolecular forces).

Card 4

Derive the ideal gas equation from Boyle’s Law, Charles’s Law, and Avogadro’s Law.

1. **Boyle’s Law**: At constant temperature, **PV = constant** (P ∝ 1/V). 2. **Charles’s Law**: At constant pressure, **V ∝ T** (V = kT). 3. **Avogadro’s Law**: At constant P and T, **V ∝ n** (V = kn). Combining these: V ∝ (1/P) × T × n → V = (nRT)/P → **PV = nRT** (where R is the universal gas constant, 0.0821 L·atm·K⁻¹·mol⁻¹).

Card 5

What is the **compressibility factor (Z)**? How does it indicate deviations from ideal behavior?

The compressibility factor (Z) is defined as **Z = PV/nRT**. For an ideal gas, Z = 1. Deviations from Z = 1 indicate non-ideal behavior: - **Z > 1**: Repulsive forces dominate (e.g., at high pressures, gas molecules occupy significant volume). - **Z < 1**: Attractive forces dominate (e.g., at low temperatures, intermolecular forces reduce pressure). Z is used to quantify how real gases deviate from ideality.

Card 6

State **van der Waals equation** and explain the significance of its correction terms.

Van der Waals equation: **[P + (an²/V²)] × (V - nb) = nRT** Correction terms: 1. **Pressure correction (an²/V²)**: Accounts for **intermolecular attractions** (reduces observed pressure). Here, *a* is a measure of the attraction between molecules. 2. **Volume correction (nb)**: Accounts for the **finite volume of gas molecules** (reduces available space). Here, *b* is the excluded volume per mole of gas. This equation improves the ideal gas law for real gases at high pressures and low temperatures.

Card 7

Define **critical temperature (Tₚ)** and **critical pressure (Pₚ)**. Why is Tₚ important for liquefaction of gases?

**Critical temperature (Tₚ)**: The highest temperature at which a gas can be liquefied by applying pressure. Above Tₚ, no amount of pressure can liquefy the gas. **Critical pressure (Pₚ)**: The pressure required to liquefy a gas at its critical temperature. **Importance of Tₚ**: Gases must be **cooled below Tₚ** before pressure can liquefy them. For example, CO₂ (Tₚ = 31.1°C) can be liquefied at room temperature, but O₂ (Tₚ = -118.6°C) requires cooling below -118.6°C.

Card 8

Explain **surface tension** and **viscosity** in liquids. How do temperature and intermolecular forces affect them?

**Surface tension**: The energy required to increase the surface area of a liquid due to **cohesive forces** between molecules. It causes liquids to minimize surface area (e.g., water droplets forming spheres). - **Effect of temperature**: Decreases with increasing temperature (kinetic energy overcomes cohesive forces). - **Effect of intermolecular forces**: Stronger forces (e.g., hydrogen bonding in water) increase surface tension. **Viscosity**: A measure of a liquid’s resistance to flow (e.g., honey is more viscous than water). - **Effect of temperature**: Decreases with increasing temperature (molecules move faster, reducing resistance). - **Effect of intermolecular forces**: Stronger forces increase viscosity (e.g., glycerol has high viscosity due to hydrogen bonding).

Card 9

What is **vapor pressure**? How does it relate to boiling point and intermolecular forces?

**Vapor pressure**: The pressure exerted by a vapor in equilibrium with its liquid at a given temperature. It is a measure of a liquid’s **tendency to evaporate**. **Relation to boiling point**: A liquid boils when its vapor pressure equals the **external pressure** (e.g., 1 atm at sea level). **Effect of intermolecular forces**: Stronger forces (e.g., hydrogen bonding) **lower vapor pressure** (fewer molecules escape the liquid) and **increase boiling point** (more energy needed to overcome forces). For example, water (H-bonding) has lower vapor pressure and higher boiling point than ethanol (weaker forces).

Card 10

Describe the **phase diagram** of a substance. Label the triple point and critical point.

A **phase diagram** plots pressure (y-axis) vs. temperature (x-axis) and shows the conditions under which a substance exists as solid, liquid, or gas. Key features: 1. **Triple point**: The unique P-T combination where **all three phases (solid, liquid, gas) coexist in equilibrium**. For water, this is at 0.01°C and 4.58 mmHg. 2. **Critical point**: The endpoint of the liquid-gas curve, beyond which the substance becomes a **supercritical fluid** (no distinction between liquid and gas). 3. **Phase boundaries**: Lines separating phases: - **Solid-liquid**: Melting/freezing curve. - **Liquid-gas**: Vaporization/condensation curve. - **Solid-gas**: Sublimation/deposition curve. For water, the solid-liquid boundary has a **negative slope** (ice is less dense than water).

Card 11

What is **Henry’s Law**? State its mathematical form and give a practical example.

**Henry’s Law**: The solubility of a gas in a liquid is **directly proportional** to the partial pressure of the gas above the liquid at constant temperature. Mathematical form: **S = kₕ × P** - *S*: Solubility of the gas (mol/L or M). - *kₕ*: Henry’s law constant (varies with gas, solvent, and temperature). - *P*: Partial pressure of the gas. **Example**: Carbonated drinks are bottled under high CO₂ pressure. When opened, the pressure drops, reducing CO₂ solubility (Henry’s Law), causing bubbles to form.

Card 12

Explain **Dalton’s Law of Partial Pressures** and its application in gas mixtures.

**Dalton’s Law**: In a mixture of non-reacting gases, the **total pressure** is the sum of the **partial pressures** of each gas. Mathematical form: **P_total = P₁ + P₂ + P₃ + ...** - *P₁, P₂, ...*: Partial pressure of each gas (P₁ = χ₁ × P_total, where χ₁ is the mole fraction of gas 1). **Application**: Used to calculate the pressure of individual gases in a mixture (e.g., air: 78% N₂, 21% O₂, 1% other gases). If total pressure is 1 atm, P_N₂ = 0.78 atm, P_O₂ = 0.21 atm.
