NEET Chemistry: d and f Block Elements - Exam-Focused Flashcards

NEETEnglishFlashcards

Card 1

What are d-block elements, and which groups of the periodic table do they occupy?

d-block elements are transition metals characterized by the filling of the (n-1)d orbitals. They occupy Groups 3 to 12 of the periodic table, spanning the 3d, 4d, 5d, and 6d series. These elements exhibit variable oxidation states, form colored compounds, and often act as catalysts due to their partially filled d-orbitals.

Card 2

Why do transition metals exhibit variable oxidation states?

Transition metals exhibit variable oxidation states because the energy difference between the (n-1)d and ns orbitals is small. This allows electrons from both the d and s orbitals to participate in bonding. For example, manganese (Mn) shows oxidation states from +2 to +7 due to the involvement of 3d and 4s electrons.

Card 3

Explain the trend in atomic radii across the 3d series (Sc to Zn).

Across the 3d series, atomic radii initially decrease from Sc to Mn due to increasing nuclear charge, which pulls electrons closer. However, from Fe to Zn, the radii remain nearly constant because the added electrons enter inner (n-1)d orbitals, which shield the outer electrons from increased nuclear charge, counteracting the contraction effect.

Card 4

What causes the color in transition metal compounds?

The color in transition metal compounds arises from d-d electronic transitions. When visible light is absorbed, electrons in partially filled d-orbitals are excited from lower to higher energy levels. The wavelength of absorbed light corresponds to the energy gap between these levels, and the complementary color is observed. For example, Cu²⁺ compounds appear blue due to absorption of red light.

Card 5

Define lanthanoid contraction and explain its consequences.

Lanthanoid contraction refers to the gradual decrease in atomic and ionic radii of the lanthanoid elements (Ce to Lu) due to poor shielding of the 4f electrons. This results in: (1) Similar radii for second and third transition series elements (e.g., Zr and Hf), (2) Increased density and hardness in later lanthanoids, and (3) Difficulty in separation of lanthanoids due to their similar chemical properties.

Card 6

Why are Zn, Cd, and Hg not considered transition metals despite being in the d-block?

Zn, Cd, and Hg are not considered transition metals because they have completely filled (n-1)d orbitals in their elemental and common oxidation states (+2). Transition metals are defined by the presence of partially filled d-orbitals, which these elements lack in their stable forms.

Card 7

What is the coordination number and geometry of [Co(NH₃)₆]³⁺?

The coordination number of [Co(NH₃)₆]³⁺ is 6, and its geometry is octahedral. This is due to the six ammonia (NH₃) ligands bonding to the central Co³⁺ ion, arranging themselves symmetrically around it to minimize repulsion.

Card 8

Explain the magnetic properties of d-block elements.

d-block elements exhibit paramagnetism when they have unpaired electrons in their d-orbitals, as the magnetic moments of these electrons do not cancel out. Diamagnetism occurs when all electrons are paired (e.g., Zn²⁺). The magnetic moment (μ) is calculated using the spin-only formula: μ = √[n(n+2)] BM, where n is the number of unpaired electrons.

Card 9

What are the common oxidation states of chromium (Cr) and manganese (Mn)?

Chromium (Cr) commonly exhibits oxidation states of +2, +3, and +6. Manganese (Mn) shows a wider range: +2, +3, +4, +6, and +7. The stability of these states varies; for example, Cr³⁺ is stable in aqueous solutions, while Mn²⁺ is the most stable state for manganese.

Card 10

Why do transition metals form interstitial compounds?

Transition metals form interstitial compounds because their lattices have voids or interstices that can accommodate small non-metal atoms like hydrogen, boron, carbon, or nitrogen. These compounds are hard, have high melting points, and retain metallic conductivity due to the preservation of the metal lattice structure.

Card 11

What is the role of K₂Cr₂O₇ in redox titrations?

Potassium dichromate (K₂Cr₂O₇) acts as a strong oxidizing agent in acidic medium. It is used in redox titrations because it undergoes a color change from orange (Cr₂O₇²⁻) to green (Cr³⁺) upon reduction, serving as a self-indicator. The half-reaction is: Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 14H⁺ + 6e⁻ → 2Cr³⁺ + 7H₂O.

Card 12

Explain the stability of Cu²⁺ over Cu⁺ in aqueous solutions.

Cu²⁺ is more stable than Cu⁺ in aqueous solutions due to the higher hydration energy of Cu²⁺, which compensates for its higher second ionization energy. Additionally, Cu⁺ disproportionates in water: 2Cu⁺ → Cu²⁺ + Cu, further favoring the +2 oxidation state.

Card 13

What are the characteristics of actinoid elements?

Actinoid elements (Ac to Lr) are radioactive, exhibit variable oxidation states (though +3 is common), and show actinoid contraction similar to lanthanoid contraction. They have high densities, form colored compounds, and their chemistry is dominated by the filling of the 5f orbitals. Many actinoids are synthetic and have applications in nuclear energy and weapons.

Card 14

Why is Sc³⁺ colorless while Ti³⁺ is colored?

Sc³⁺ is colorless because it has no d-electrons (d⁰ configuration), so no d-d transitions are possible. Ti³⁺, with a d¹ configuration, absorbs visible light to promote its single d-electron to a higher energy level, resulting in the observed color (typically purple or violet).

Card 15

What is the difference between inner transition and outer transition elements?

Inner transition elements (lanthanoids and actinoids) involve the filling of (n-2)f orbitals and are placed separately in the periodic table. Outer transition elements (d-block) involve the filling of (n-1)d orbitals and occupy Groups 3 to 12. Inner transition elements exhibit more similar chemical properties due to the shielding effect of f-orbitals.

Card 16

Explain the catalytic properties of transition metals.

Transition metals act as catalysts due to their ability to adopt variable oxidation states and form intermediate complexes with reactants. This lowers the activation energy of reactions. For example, iron (Fe) catalyzes the Haber process for ammonia synthesis, and nickel (Ni) is used in hydrogenation reactions.

Card 17

What is the electronic configuration of Cr and Cu, and why do they deviate from the Aufbau principle?

Chromium (Cr) has the configuration [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹, and copper (Cu) has [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹. These deviations occur because half-filled (d⁵) and fully filled (d¹⁰) d-orbitals are more stable due to symmetry and exchange energy. The energy difference between 3d and 4s orbitals is small, making these configurations favorable.

Card 18

What are the uses of lanthanoids and actinoids?

Lanthanoids are used in: (1) Misch metal (alloys for lighter flints), (2) Glass polishing (CeO₂), (3) Phosphors in TV screens (Eu, Tb), and (4) Strong magnets (Nd₂Fe₁₄B). Actinoids are used in: (1) Nuclear reactors (U, Pu), (2) Radiotherapy (Co-60), and (3) Smoke detectors (Am-241).

Card 19

Why do transition metal ions form complexes easily?

Transition metal ions form complexes easily due to: (1) Small size and high charge density, which attract ligands, (2) Availability of empty d-orbitals to accept electron pairs from ligands, and (3) Variable oxidation states that allow flexibility in bonding. This results in a wide variety of coordination compounds.

Card 20

What is the difference between ferromagnetism and paramagnetism?

Paramagnetism arises from unpaired electrons and results in weak attraction to a magnetic field, disappearing when the field is removed. Ferromagnetism occurs in metals like Fe, Co, and Ni, where unpaired electrons align parallel to each other in domains, resulting in strong, permanent magnetization even after the field is removed.
