NEET Chemistry: Chemical Bonding and Molecular Structure - Exam-Focused Flashcards

NEETEnglishFlashcards

Card 1

Define chemical bond. What are the primary forces involved in chemical bonding?

A chemical bond is a lasting attraction between atoms, ions, or molecules that enables the formation of chemical compounds. The primary forces involved are electrostatic forces (attraction between oppositely charged ions in ionic bonds) and the sharing of electron pairs (in covalent bonds). Metallic bonds involve a 'sea of electrons' shared among metal atoms.

Card 2

What is the key difference between ionic and covalent bonds? Provide one example of each.

Ionic bonds involve the complete transfer of electrons from one atom to another, resulting in oppositely charged ions (e.g., NaCl). Covalent bonds involve the sharing of electron pairs between atoms (e.g., H₂O). Ionic bonds typically form between metals and non-metals, while covalent bonds form between non-metals.

Card 3

Explain the concept of electronegativity. How does it influence bond polarity?

Electronegativity is the ability of an atom to attract shared electrons in a covalent bond. If two atoms have a significant difference in electronegativity (>0.4 on the Pauling scale), the bond is polar, with the more electronegative atom pulling electron density toward itself, creating a dipole moment (e.g., H-Cl). If the difference is negligible, the bond is non-polar (e.g., H-H).

Card 4

What is a coordinate covalent bond? Give an example of a molecule where it occurs.

A coordinate covalent bond (or dative bond) is a type of covalent bond where both electrons in the shared pair come from the same atom. Example: In the ammonium ion (NH₄⁺), the nitrogen atom donates a lone pair to a proton (H⁺), forming a coordinate bond.

Card 5

State the Octet Rule. What are its limitations?

The Octet Rule states that atoms tend to gain, lose, or share electrons to achieve a stable configuration of 8 electrons in their valence shell (like noble gases). Limitations: (1) Incomplete octets (e.g., BF₃, BeCl₂), (2) Expanded octets (e.g., PCl₅, SF₆), (3) Odd-electron molecules (e.g., NO), and (4) Transition metals, which often do not follow the octet rule.

Card 6

Describe the Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion (VSEPR) theory. How does it predict molecular geometry?

VSEPR theory predicts molecular geometry based on the repulsion between electron pairs (bonding and lone pairs) in the valence shell of the central atom. Electron pairs arrange themselves to minimize repulsion, leading to specific shapes: linear (2 pairs), trigonal planar (3 pairs), tetrahedral (4 pairs), trigonal bipyramidal (5 pairs), and octahedral (6 pairs). Lone pairs occupy more space than bonding pairs, distorting ideal geometries (e.g., H₂O is bent, not linear).

Card 7

What is hybridization? Explain sp³ hybridization with an example.

Hybridization is the mixing of atomic orbitals to form new hybrid orbitals suitable for bonding. In sp³ hybridization, one s orbital and three p orbitals mix to form four sp³ hybrid orbitals, arranged tetrahedrally (109.5° bond angles). Example: Methane (CH₄), where carbon’s 2s and 2p orbitals hybridize to form four equivalent sp³ orbitals, each bonding with a hydrogen atom.

Card 8

Differentiate between sigma (σ) and pi (π) bonds. Which is stronger and why?

Sigma (σ) bonds are formed by head-on overlap of orbitals (e.g., s-s, s-p, or p-p overlap) and are stronger due to greater orbital overlap. Pi (π) bonds are formed by side-by-side overlap of p orbitals and are weaker because the overlap is less effective. Single bonds are σ bonds; double bonds consist of one σ and one π bond; triple bonds consist of one σ and two π bonds.

Card 9

What is bond order? How is it calculated for diatomic molecules using Molecular Orbital Theory (MOT)?

Bond order is the number of chemical bonds between a pair of atoms. In MOT, it is calculated as: Bond Order = (Number of bonding electrons - Number of antibonding electrons) / 2. Higher bond order indicates stronger and shorter bonds. Example: O₂ has a bond order of 2 (8 bonding electrons - 4 antibonding electrons = 4; 4/2 = 2).

Card 10

Explain the concept of resonance with an example. Why is resonance important?

Resonance occurs when a molecule cannot be represented by a single Lewis structure but is a hybrid of multiple structures (resonance structures). Example: Ozone (O₃) has two equivalent resonance structures with one single and one double bond. Resonance stabilizes the molecule by delocalizing electrons, lowering its overall energy. It explains bond lengths (e.g., in benzene, all C-C bonds are equal and intermediate between single and double bonds).

Card 11

What are hydrogen bonds? How do they differ from covalent bonds?

Hydrogen bonds are weak electrostatic attractions between a hydrogen atom covalently bonded to a highly electronegative atom (N, O, or F) and another electronegative atom. They are weaker than covalent bonds but stronger than van der Waals forces. Example: Hydrogen bonding in water (H₂O) leads to high boiling points and surface tension. Unlike covalent bonds, hydrogen bonds are intermolecular and do not involve electron sharing.

Card 12

Describe the molecular geometry and bond angle of ammonia (NH₃) using VSEPR theory.

Ammonia (NH₃) has a trigonal pyramidal geometry with a bond angle of ~107°. The central nitrogen atom has 4 electron pairs (3 bonding pairs with H and 1 lone pair). The lone pair repels bonding pairs more strongly, compressing the H-N-H bond angle from the ideal tetrahedral angle (109.5°) to 107°.

Card 13

What is dipole moment? How is it calculated for a diatomic molecule?

Dipole moment (μ) is a measure of the separation of positive and negative charges in a molecule. For a diatomic molecule, it is calculated as: μ = q × r, where q is the magnitude of charge and r is the distance between charges. It is a vector quantity, measured in Debye (D). Example: HCl has a dipole moment because Cl is more electronegative than H.

Card 14

Explain the formation of a metallic bond. What properties of metals does it explain?

A metallic bond is the electrostatic attraction between a lattice of positive metal ions and a 'sea of delocalized electrons.' This model explains properties of metals: (1) Electrical conductivity (delocalized electrons move freely), (2) Malleability and ductility (layers of ions can slide without breaking bonds), and (3) High melting/boiling points (strong electrostatic forces).

Card 15

What are van der Waals forces? Differentiate between London dispersion forces and dipole-dipole interactions.

Van der Waals forces are weak intermolecular forces. (1) London dispersion forces: Temporary attractive forces due to instantaneous dipoles in non-polar molecules (e.g., noble gases, H₂). Strength increases with molecular size/weight. (2) Dipole-dipole interactions: Attractive forces between permanent dipoles in polar molecules (e.g., HCl). Dipole-dipole forces are stronger than London forces for molecules of similar size.

Card 16

How does Molecular Orbital Theory (MOT) explain the paramagnetism of oxygen (O₂)?

MOT explains O₂’s paramagnetism by showing it has two unpaired electrons in its molecular orbitals. The molecular orbital configuration of O₂ is: (σ1s)² (σ*1s)² (σ2s)² (σ*2s)² (σ2p_z)² (π2p_x)² (π2p_y)² (π*2p_x)¹ (π*2p_y)¹. The two unpaired electrons in the π*2p orbitals make O₂ paramagnetic, as observed experimentally.

Card 17

What is the Fajans' rule? How does it predict the covalent character in ionic bonds?

Fajans' rules predict the extent of covalent character in ionic bonds based on: (1) Small cation size: Increases polarization of the anion, leading to covalent character (e.g., AlCl₃ is more covalent than NaCl). (2) Large anion size: Easier to polarize, increasing covalent character (e.g., LiI is more covalent than LiF). (3) High charge on cation/anion: Increases polarization (e.g., MgCl₂ is more covalent than NaCl).
