NEET Chemistry - Solid State Flashcards (Exam-Focused)

NEETEnglishFlashcards

Card 1

Define a crystalline solid and give two examples.

A crystalline solid is a solid material whose constituent particles (atoms, ions, or molecules) are arranged in a highly ordered, repeating three-dimensional pattern called a crystal lattice. This ordered structure extends over long ranges, giving crystalline solids sharp melting points and anisotropic properties. Examples: Sodium chloride (NaCl), Diamond.

Card 2

What is the coordination number in a crystal lattice? State the coordination number for a body-centred cubic (BCC) structure.

The coordination number in a crystal lattice is the number of nearest neighbour particles surrounding a given particle in the lattice. In a body-centred cubic (BCC) structure, each atom is surrounded by 8 nearest neighbours, so the coordination number is 8.

Card 3

Differentiate between hexagonal close-packed (HCP) and cubic close-packed (CCP) structures in terms of layer arrangement.

In hexagonal close-packed (HCP), the layers of spheres are stacked in an ABAB pattern, where the third layer aligns directly above the first layer. In cubic close-packed (CCP), also known as face-centred cubic (FCC), the layers are stacked in an ABCABC pattern, where the third layer is placed over the octahedral voids of the second layer, not aligning with the first.

Card 4

Calculate the number of atoms per unit cell in a face-centred cubic (FCC) structure.

In a face-centred cubic (FCC) unit cell: 8 corner atoms contribute 1/8 each (8 × 1/8 = 1 atom), and 6 face-centred atoms contribute 1/2 each (6 × 1/2 = 3 atoms). Total atoms per unit cell = 1 + 3 = 4 atoms.

Card 5

What is a Schottky defect? How does it affect the density of a crystal?

A Schottky defect is a type of point defect in ionic crystals where an equal number of cations and anions are missing from their lattice sites, maintaining electrical neutrality. This defect decreases the density of the crystal because the mass of the crystal decreases while the volume remains nearly unchanged.

Card 6

Explain Frenkel defect with an example. Does it change the density of the crystal?

A Frenkel defect occurs when an ion is displaced from its regular lattice site to an interstitial site within the crystal, creating a vacancy at its original site and an interstitial defect. This defect does not change the density of the crystal because the mass and volume remain the same. Example: Silver chloride (AgCl), where Ag⁺ ions occupy interstitial sites.

Card 7

What is the relationship between edge length (a) and atomic radius (r) in a body-centred cubic (BCC) structure?

In a body-centred cubic (BCC) structure, the atoms along the body diagonal touch each other. The body diagonal length is 4r (where r is the atomic radius), and it is also equal to a√3 (where a is the edge length). Therefore, the relationship is: a√3 = 4r ⇒ a = (4r)/√3.

Card 8

Define packing efficiency. Calculate the packing efficiency of a simple cubic (SC) structure.

Packing efficiency is the percentage of the total volume of a unit cell occupied by the constituent particles (atoms, ions, or molecules). For a simple cubic (SC) structure: Volume of atoms = (4/3)πr³ × 1 (1 atom per unit cell), Volume of unit cell = a³ = (2r)³ = 8r³. Packing efficiency = [(4/3)πr³ / 8r³] × 100 = (π/6) × 100 ≈ 52.4%.

Card 9

What are the differences between n-type and p-type semiconductors in terms of doping and charge carriers?

n-type semiconductors are doped with pentavalent impurities (e.g., phosphorus in silicon), which introduce extra electrons as majority charge carriers. p-type semiconductors are doped with trivalent impurities (e.g., boron in silicon), which create holes (electron deficiencies) as majority charge carriers. In n-type, electrons are majority carriers; in p-type, holes are majority carriers.

Card 10

Explain the concept of Bragg's Law in X-ray diffraction. Write its equation.

Bragg's Law describes the conditions for constructive interference of X-rays scattered by the planes of atoms in a crystal. It states that constructive interference occurs when the path difference between waves scattered from adjacent planes is an integer multiple of the wavelength. The equation is: nλ = 2d sinθ, where n is an integer (order of diffraction), λ is the wavelength of X-rays, d is the interplanar spacing, and θ is the angle of incidence (Bragg angle).

Card 11

What is the difference between ferromagnetic and antiferromagnetic materials?

Ferromagnetic materials (e.g., iron, cobalt, nickel) have permanent magnetic moments that align parallel to each other in domains, resulting in strong magnetization even in the absence of an external magnetic field. Antiferromagnetic materials (e.g., manganese oxide, MnO) have magnetic moments of adjacent atoms or ions that align in opposite directions, cancelling each other out and resulting in zero net magnetization.

Card 12

How does the presence of impurities affect the electrical conductivity of semiconductors?

The presence of impurities in semiconductors (doping) significantly increases their electrical conductivity. In intrinsic (pure) semiconductors, conductivity is low due to limited charge carriers. Doping introduces additional charge carriers: electrons in n-type semiconductors or holes in p-type semiconductors, thereby enhancing conductivity.

Card 13

What is a unit cell? Name the seven crystal systems based on unit cell geometry.

A unit cell is the smallest repeating unit in a crystal lattice that, when repeated in three dimensions, reproduces the entire lattice. The seven crystal systems, based on unit cell geometry, are: Cubic, Tetragonal, Orthorhombic, Hexagonal, Rhombohedral (Trigonal), Monoclinic, and Triclinic.

Card 14

Explain the term 'lattice point' in the context of a crystal lattice.

A lattice point is a specific position in a crystal lattice that represents the location of a constituent particle (atom, ion, or molecule). All lattice points are identical in their surroundings, and the arrangement of lattice points defines the geometry of the crystal structure. The entire lattice is generated by repeating these points in three-dimensional space.

Card 15

What is the significance of voids in close-packed structures? Name the two types of voids.

Voids in close-packed structures are the empty spaces between the constituent particles (atoms, ions, or molecules) where smaller particles can reside. They are significant because they determine the coordination number and the stability of ionic compounds. The two types of voids are: Tetrahedral voids (coordination number 4) and Octahedral voids (coordination number 6).

Card 16

Calculate the density of a crystal given the following: Edge length (a) = 400 pm, Molar mass (M) = 60 g/mol, Number of atoms per unit cell (Z) = 2. (Use N_A = 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹)

Density (ρ) of a crystal is given by: ρ = (Z × M) / (N_A × a³). Convert edge length to cm: a = 400 pm = 400 × 10⁻¹² m = 4 × 10⁻⁸ cm. a³ = (4 × 10⁻⁸ cm)³ = 64 × 10⁻²⁴ cm³. ρ = (2 × 60 g/mol) / (6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹ × 64 × 10⁻²⁴ cm³) = 120 / (38.5408) ≈ 3.11 g/cm³.

Card 17

What is anisotropy in crystalline solids? Give an example.

Anisotropy is the property of crystalline solids where physical properties (e.g., electrical conductivity, refractive index, thermal expansion) vary depending on the direction in which they are measured. This occurs due to the ordered arrangement of particles in different directions. Example: Graphite conducts electricity along the layers but is a poor conductor perpendicular to the layers.

Card 18

Explain the term 'point defect' in crystals. Name the two main categories of point defects.

A point defect is a type of crystal defect where the irregularity or deviation from the ideal arrangement occurs at a single lattice point or a very small cluster of points. Point defects disrupt the perfect periodicity of the crystal lattice. The two main categories are: Stoichiometric defects (e.g., Schottky and Frenkel defects) and Non-stoichiometric defects (e.g., metal excess and metal deficiency defects).
