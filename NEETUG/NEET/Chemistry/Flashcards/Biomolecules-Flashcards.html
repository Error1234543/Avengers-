Card 1

Define biomolecules and classify them based on their chemical nature.

Biomolecules are organic molecules that are essential components of living organisms and play critical roles in biological processes. Based on their chemical nature, they are classified into four major types: 1. **Carbohydrates**: Polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones (e.g., glucose, starch). 2. **Proteins**: Polymers of amino acids linked by peptide bonds (e.g., enzymes, hormones). 3. **Lipids**: Esters of fatty acids and alcohols (e.g., triglycerides, phospholipids). 4. **Nucleic Acids**: Polymers of nucleotides (e.g., DNA, RNA). **Exam Tip**: Focus on the functional groups and bonding patterns unique to each class.

Card 2

What is the difference between a monosaccharide, disaccharide, and polysaccharide? Provide one example of each.

**Monosaccharide**: Simplest carbohydrate; cannot be hydrolyzed further. Example: Glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆). **Disaccharide**: Formed by the condensation of two monosaccharides with the loss of a water molecule. Example: Sucrose (glucose + fructose). **Polysaccharide**: Long-chain polymers of monosaccharides. Example: Starch (storage form in plants) or glycogen (storage form in animals). **Key Point**: Monosaccharides are reducing sugars (e.g., glucose), while disaccharides like sucrose are non-reducing unless hydrolyzed.

Card 3

Explain the structure of glucose. What is the difference between its open-chain and cyclic forms?

**Open-Chain Form**: Glucose is a hexose sugar with the formula C₆H₁₂O₆. It contains an aldehyde group (–CHO) at C1 and hydroxyl groups (–OH) at the remaining carbons (C2–C6). **Cyclic Form**: In solution, glucose predominantly exists as a six-membered ring (pyranose) due to intramolecular nucleophilic addition of the –OH group at C5 to the aldehyde group at C1. This forms a hemiacetal, creating two anomers: - **α-D-glucose**: –OH group at C1 is below the plane of the ring. - **β-D-glucose**: –OH group at C1 is above the plane of the ring. **Exam Focus**: Cyclization is critical for understanding glycosidic bond formation in disaccharides and polysaccharides.

Card 4

What is a glycosidic bond? How is it formed in maltose?

A **glycosidic bond** is a covalent bond formed between the anomeric carbon of one monosaccharide and a hydroxyl group of another monosaccharide (or another molecule) via a condensation reaction (loss of water). **Formation in Maltose**: Maltose is a disaccharide formed by the linkage of two α-D-glucose molecules. The glycosidic bond is formed between the **C1 of the first glucose (anomeric carbon)** and the **C4 hydroxyl group of the second glucose**, resulting in an **α(1→4) glycosidic bond**. This bond is hydrolyzed by the enzyme maltase. **Key Detail**: The anomeric carbon involved in the bond determines whether the sugar is reducing (free anomeric carbon) or non-reducing (no free anomeric carbon).

Card 5

Describe the structure and biological significance of starch and cellulose.

**Starch**: - **Structure**: A homopolysaccharide of glucose with two components: 1. **Amylose**: Linear chain of α-D-glucose units linked by α(1→4) glycosidic bonds. 2. **Amylopectin**: Branched chain with α(1→4) linkages in the main chain and α(1→6) linkages at branch points (every 24–30 glucose units). - **Biological Significance**: Primary storage polysaccharide in plants; hydrolyzed by amylase to glucose for energy. **Cellulose**: - **Structure**: Linear chain of β-D-glucose units linked by β(1→4) glycosidic bonds. The β-linkage allows hydrogen bonding between adjacent chains, forming strong microfibrils. - **Biological Significance**: Structural component of plant cell walls; indigestible by humans due to lack of cellulase enzyme. **Exam Tip**: Contrast the **digestibility** (starch is digestible; cellulose is not) and **linkage types** (α vs. β) for high-yield questions.

Card 6

What are essential and non-essential amino acids? List two examples of each.

**Essential Amino Acids**: Cannot be synthesized by the human body and must be obtained through diet. Example: **Valine**, **Leucine**. **Non-Essential Amino Acids**: Can be synthesized by the body. Example: **Glycine**, **Alanine**. **Key Point**: There are **9 essential amino acids** for humans: Histidine, Isoleucine, Leucine, Lysine, Methionine, Phenylalanine, Threonine, Tryptophan, and Valine. **Clinical Relevance**: Deficiency of essential amino acids leads to malnutrition disorders (e.g., kwashiorkor).

Card 7

Explain the primary, secondary, tertiary, and quaternary structures of proteins.

**Primary Structure**: Linear sequence of amino acids linked by peptide bonds. Determined by the genetic code (e.g., insulin’s amino acid sequence). **Secondary Structure**: Local folding patterns stabilized by hydrogen bonds: - **α-Helix**: Right-handed coil with hydrogen bonds between –NH of one amino acid and –C=O of the fourth amino acid ahead. - **β-Pleated Sheet**: Extended strands connected by hydrogen bonds; can be parallel or antiparallel. **Tertiary Structure**: 3D folding of a single polypeptide chain due to interactions between R-groups: - **Hydrophobic interactions** (non-polar groups cluster inward). - **Hydrogen bonds** (between polar R-groups). - **Ionic bonds** (between charged R-groups). - **Disulfide bridges** (covalent bonds between cysteine residues). **Quaternary Structure**: Association of two or more polypeptide chains (subunits) to form a functional protein. Example: Hemoglobin (4 subunits: 2 α and 2 β chains). **Exam Focus**: Denaturation disrupts secondary/tertiary/quaternary structures but not primary structure.

Card 8

What is denaturation of proteins? List three agents that cause denaturation and explain their effects.

**Denaturation**: Loss of the native conformation (secondary, tertiary, or quaternary structure) of a protein due to disruption of non-covalent interactions, leading to loss of biological activity. **Agents and Effects**: 1. **Heat**: Breaks hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions (e.g., cooking egg white coagulates albumin). 2. **Strong Acids/Bases**: Disrupt ionic bonds and hydrogen bonds by altering the charge on R-groups (e.g., curdling of milk by lactic acid). 3. **Organic Solvents (e.g., alcohol)**: Disrupt hydrophobic interactions by dissolving non-polar R-groups (e.g., ethanol denatures proteins in bacteria, acting as a disinfectant). **Key Point**: Denaturation is often irreversible (e.g., boiled egg cannot revert to raw egg).

Card 9

Describe the structure of a nucleotide. How do DNA and RNA nucleotides differ?

**Nucleotide Structure**: Composed of three components: 1. **Nitrogenous Base**: Purines (adenine, guanine) or pyrimidines (cytosine, thymine, uracil). 2. **Pentose Sugar**: Ribose (in RNA) or deoxyribose (in DNA; lacks –OH at C2). 3. **Phosphate Group**: Attached to the 5’ carbon of the sugar. **DNA vs. RNA Nucleotides**: - **Sugar**: DNA has deoxyribose; RNA has ribose. - **Bases**: DNA contains adenine (A), thymine (T), cytosine (C), and guanine (G). RNA replaces thymine with uracil (U). - **Structure**: DNA is double-stranded; RNA is typically single-stranded. **Exam Tip**: Remember the base pairing rules: **A-T (DNA)** or **A-U (RNA)** via 2 hydrogen bonds; **C-G** via 3 hydrogen bonds.
