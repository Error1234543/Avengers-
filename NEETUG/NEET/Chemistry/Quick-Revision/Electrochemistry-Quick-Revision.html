  • {'term': 'Redox Reaction', 'definition': 'A chemical reaction involving the transfer of electrons between species, where oxidation (loss of electrons) and reduction (gain of electrons) occur simultaneously. It is the foundation of electrochemical processes.'}
  • {'term': 'Electrochemical Cell', 'definition': 'A device that converts chemical energy into electrical energy (galvanic cell) or uses electrical energy to drive a non-spontaneous chemical reaction (electrolytic cell).   • {'term': 'Electrode Potential (E)', 'definition': 'The potential difference developed between an electrode and its solution in a half-cell, measured under standard conditions (1 M concentration, 1 atm pressure, 298 K). It indicates the tendency of an electrode to lose or gain electrons.'}

  • {'term': 'Nernst Equation', 'definition': 'An equation that relates the electrode potential of a half-cell to the standard electrode potential, temperature, and concentrations of the species involved: E = E° - (RT/nF) ln(Q), where E° is the standard electrode potential, R is the gas constant, T is the temperature, n is the number of electrons transferred, F is the Faraday constant, and Q is the reaction quotient.'}
  • {'term': 'Faraday’s Laws of Electrolysis', 'definition': 'Two laws describing the quantitative aspects of electrolysis: (1) The amount of substance deposited or liberated at an electrode is directly proportional to the quantity of electricity passed. Key concepts

  • {'concept': 'Types of Electrochemical Cells', 'content': 'Electrochemical cells are classified into two main types: (1) **Galvanic (Voltaic) Cells**: These cells convert chemical energy into electrical energy through spontaneous redox reactions. Examples include Daniell cells and dry cells. The anode is the site of oxidation (negative electrode), and the cathode is the site of reduction (positive electrode). (2) **Electrolytic Cells**: These cells use electrical energy to drive non-spontaneous chemical reactions, such as electrolysis of water or molten salts. The anode is positive, and the cathode is negative, with external voltage applied to force the reaction.'}
  • {'concept': 'Standard Electrode Potential (E°)', 'content': 'Standard electrode potential is a measure of the tendency of an electrode to gain or lose electrons under standard conditions. A more positive E° indicates a stronger oxidizing agent, while a more negative E° indicates a stronger reducing agent. The standard cell potential (E°cell) is calculated as E°cell = E°cathode - E°anode. If E°cell is positive, the reaction is spontaneous; if negative, it is non-spontaneous.'}
  • {'concept': 'Nernst Equation and Its Applications', 'content': 'The Nernst equation is crucial for calculating the electrode potential under non-standard conditions. It is given by: E = E° - (0.0591/n) log(Q) at 298 K, where Q is the reaction quotient. Applications include determining the cell potential at varying concentrations, calculating equilibrium constants (K) using the relation E°cell = (0.0591/n) log(K), and predicting the direction of redox reactions. For example, in a Daniell cell, increasing the concentration of Zn²⁺ ions decreases the cell potential.'}
  • {'concept': 'Electrolysis and Faraday’s Laws', 'content': 'Electrolysis involves the decomposition of an electrolyte using an external electric current. Faraday’s laws quantify the relationship between the amount of substance deposited and the electricity passed: (1) **First Law**: w = Z × I × t, where w is the mass deposited, Z is the electrochemical equivalent, I is the current, and t is the time. (2) **Second Law**: The masses of different substances deposited by the same quantity of electricity are proportional to their equivalent weights. For example, passing 96,500 C (1 Faraday) of electricity deposits 1 mole of Ag (108 g) or 0.5 moles of Cu (31.75 g).'}
  • {'concept': 'Conductance in Electrolytic Solutions', 'content': 'Conductance in electrolytic solutions depends on the nature of the electrolyte, concentration, temperature, and the solvent. **Strong electrolytes** (e.g., NaCl, HCl) dissociate completely and show high conductivity, while **weak electrolytes** (e.g., CH₃COOH) dissociate partially and show lower conductivity. Conductivity (κ) increases with temperature due to increased ion mobility. Molar conductivity (Λm) increases with dilution for strong electrolytes but shows a sharp increase for weak electrolytes due to increased dissociation.'}
  • {'concept': 'Kohlrausch’s Law of Independent Migration of Ions', 'content': 'Kohlrausch’s law states that the molar conductivity of an electrolyte at infinite dilution (Λ°m) is the sum of the contributions of its individual ions. Mathematically, Λ°m = ν₊λ°₊ + ν₋λ°₋, where ν₊ and ν₋ are the number of cations and anions per formula unit, and λ°₊ and λ°₋ are their limiting ionic conductivities. This law is used to calculate the molar conductivity of weak electrolytes at infinite dilution, which cannot be measured directly due to incomplete dissociation.'}
  • {'concept': 'Applications of Electrochemistry', 'content': 'Electrochemistry has wide-ranging applications: (1) **Batteries and Fuel Cells**: Galvanic cells like lead-acid batteries and lithium-ion batteries power devices, while fuel cells convert chemical energy from fuels (e.g., H₂) into electricity. (2) **Electroplating**: Used to coat metals with a thin layer of another metal (e.g., gold plating) for corrosion resistance or aesthetics. (3) **Corrosion Prevention**: Techniques like cathodic protection and sacrificial anodes (e.g., Zn on iron) prevent rusting. (4) **Industrial Processes**: Electrolysis is used in the extraction of metals (e.g., Al from Al₂O₃) and the production of chemicals (e.g., Cl₂ and NaOH via chlor-alkali process).'}

Important facts

  • The standard electrode potential of the hydrogen electrode (SHE) is arbitrarily set to 0.00 V for reference purposes.
  • In a galvanic cell, the anode is negative, and the cathode is positive, while in an electrolytic cell, the anode is positive, and the cathode is negative.
  • The electrochemical series is a list of electrodes arranged in order of their standard electrode potentials. It helps predict the feasibility of redox reactions and the direction of electron flow in a cell.
  • The Faraday constant (F) is the charge carried by 1 mole of electrons, equal to 96,485 C/mol (approximately 96,500 C/mol for calculations).
  • The molar conductivity of a strong electrolyte increases slightly with dilution due to reduced interionic attractions, while that of a weak electrolyte increases sharply due to increased dissociation.
  • The conductivity of an electrolyte solution decreases with dilution because the number of ions per unit volume decreases, even though their mobility increases.
  • The Nernst equation can be used to calculate the equilibrium constant (K) of a reaction using the relation: E°cell = (0.0591/n) log(K) at 298 K.
  • In electrolysis, the product formed at the cathode depends on the nature of the electrolyte: (1) For aqueous solutions, metals below hydrogen in the electrochemical series (e.g., Cu²⁺) are deposited, while metals above hydrogen (e.g., Na⁺) liberate H₂ gas. (2) For molten salts, the metal cation is always deposited at the cathode.
  • The efficiency of an electrochemical cell is determined by its cell potential (E°cell) and the resistance of the circuit. Higher E°cell and lower resistance lead to better performance.
  • Common errors in electrochemistry problems include confusing the signs of electrodes in galvanic vs. electrolytic cells, misapplying the Nernst equation (e.g., using natural log instead of base-10 log), and neglecting the effect of concentration on cell potential.
