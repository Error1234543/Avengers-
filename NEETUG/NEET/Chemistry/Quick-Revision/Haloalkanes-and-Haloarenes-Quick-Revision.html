Definitions

  • {'term': 'Haloalkanes (Alkyl Halides)', 'definition': 'Organic compounds derived from alkanes by replacing one or more hydrogen atoms with halogen atoms (fluorine, chlorine, bromine, or iodine). General formula: R-X, where R is an alkyl group and X is a halogen.'}
  • {'term': 'Haloarenes (Aryl Halides)', 'definition': 'Organic compounds derived from aromatic hydrocarbons (arenes) by replacing one or more hydrogen atoms with halogen atoms. General formula: Ar-X, where Ar is an aryl group and X is a halogen.'}
  • {'term': 'Nucleophilic Substitution Reaction (SN1 and SN2)', 'definition': 'A reaction in which a nucleophile replaces a leaving group (typically a halogen) in a haloalkane. SN2 is a one-step, bimolecular reaction with inversion of configuration, while SN1 is a two-step, unimolecular reaction with racemization.'}
  • {'term': 'Elimination Reaction (E1 and E2)', 'definition': 'A reaction in which a haloalkane loses a hydrogen halide (HX) to form an alkene. E2 is a one-step, bimolecular reaction favored by strong bases, while E1 is a two-step, unimolecular reaction favored by weak bases and polar protic solvents.'}
  • {'term': 'Grignard Reagent', 'definition': 'An organomagnesium compound with the formula R-Mg-X, where R is an alkyl or aryl group and X is a halogen. Highly reactive and used in the synthesis of alcohols, carboxylic acids, and other organic compounds.'}

Key concepts

  • {'concept': 'Classification of Haloalkanes', 'content': 'Haloalkanes are classified based on the carbon atom bonded to the halogen: primary (1°), secondary (2°), or tertiary (3°). This classification determines their reactivity in substitution and elimination reactions. For example, tertiary haloalkanes favor SN1 and E1 mechanisms due to the stability of the carbocation intermediate, while primary haloalkanes favor SN2 and E2 mechanisms.'}
  • {'concept': 'Nomenclature of Haloalkanes and Haloarenes', 'content': "Follow IUPAC rules: (1) Identify the longest carbon chain containing the halogen. (2) Number the chain to give the halogen the lowest possible locant. (3) Name the halogen as a prefix (fluoro-, chloro-, bromo-, iodo-) with its position. For haloarenes, the halogen is treated as a substituent on the benzene ring (e.g., chlorobenzene). Common names like 'vinyl chloride' (chloroethene) are also used."}
  • {'concept': 'Preparation of Haloalkanes', 'content': 'Key methods include: (1) **Free Radical Halogenation**: Alkanes react with halogens (Cl₂ or Br₂) in the presence of UV light or heat to form haloalkanes. (2) **From Alcohols**: Reaction of alcohols with HX (Lucas test for tertiary alcohols), PCl₅, PCl₃, or SOCl₂. (3) **Halide Exchange**: Finkelstein reaction (R-Cl + NaI → R-I + NaCl) or Swarts reaction (R-Cl + AgF → R-F + AgCl). (4) **Electrophilic Addition**: Alkenes react with HX or X₂ to form haloalkanes (Markovnikov’s rule applies).'}
  • {'concept': 'Preparation of Haloarenes', 'content': 'Key methods include: (1) **Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution**: Benzene reacts with Cl₂ or Br₂ in the presence of a Lewis acid catalyst (FeCl₃ or AlCl₃) to form chlorobenzene or bromobenzene. (2) **Sandmeyer Reaction**: Diazonium salts (ArN₂⁺Cl⁻) react with CuCl, CuBr, or CuCN to form aryl halides. (3) **Gattermann Reaction**: Similar to Sandmeyer but uses Cu powder and HCl/HBr instead of CuCl/CuBr.'}
  • {'concept': 'Reactivity of Haloalkanes vs. Haloarenes', 'content': 'Haloalkanes are more reactive than haloarenes due to the sp³ hybridization of the carbon-halogen bond in haloalkanes, which is weaker and more polar than the sp² hybridized C-X bond in haloarenes. Haloarenes undergo nucleophilic substitution only under harsh conditions (e.g., high temperature, pressure, or strong nucleophiles) due to resonance stabilization of the benzene ring, which makes the C-X bond stronger.'}
  • {'concept': 'Nucleophilic Substitution (SN1 and SN2) Mechanisms', 'content': '**SN2 Mechanism**: (1) One-step, concerted process. (2) Nucleophile attacks the carbon from the side opposite the leaving group, causing inversion of configuration (Walden inversion). (3) Rate = k[R-X][Nu⁻]; depends on both substrate and nucleophile concentrations. (4) Favored by primary haloalkanes, strong nucleophiles, and polar aprotic solvents (e.g., DMSO, acetone).\n\n**SN1 Mechanism**: (1) Two-step process: (a) Formation of a carbocation intermediate (rate-determining step). (b) Nucleophile attacks the carbocation. (2) Rate = k[R-X]; depends only on the substrate concentration. (3) Racemization occurs due to planar carbocation intermediate. (4) Favored by tertiary haloalkanes, weak nucleophiles, and polar protic solvents (e.g., water, alcohols).'}
  • {'concept': 'Elimination Reactions (E1 and E2)', 'content': '**E2 Mechanism**: (1) One-step, concerted process. (2) Base abstracts a β-hydrogen, and the leaving group departs simultaneously, forming an alkene. (3) Rate = k[R-X][Base]; depends on both substrate and base concentrations. (4) Favored by strong bases (e.g., OH⁻, RO⁻), high temperatures, and anti-periplanar geometry of H and X. (5) Follows Zaitsev’s rule (major product is the more substituted alkene).\n\n**E1 Mechanism**: (1) Two-step process: (a) Formation of a carbocation intermediate (rate-determining step). (b) Base abstracts a β-hydrogen to form an alkene. (2) Rate = k[R-X]; depends only on the substrate concentration. (3) Favored by tertiary haloalkanes, weak bases, and polar protic solvents. (4) Also follows Zaitsev’s rule.'}
  • {'concept': 'Reactions of Haloalkanes', 'content': 'Key reactions include: (1) **Nucleophilic Substitution**: With OH⁻ (forms alcohols), CN⁻ (forms nitriles), NH₃ (forms amines), RCOO⁻ (forms esters), and OR⁻ (forms ethers). (2) **Elimination**: With alcoholic KOH (forms alkenes) or NaNH₂ (forms alkynes). (3) **Wurtz Reaction**: Two alkyl halides react with sodium metal to form alkanes (R-X + 2Na + X-R → R-R + 2NaX). (4) **Grignard Reagent Formation**: R-X + Mg → R-Mg-X (in dry ether). (5) **Reduction**: With LiAlH₄ or H₂/Pd to form alkanes.'}
  • {'concept': 'Reactions of Haloarenes', 'content': 'Key reactions include: (1) **Nucleophilic Substitution**: Under harsh conditions (e.g., high temperature, pressure, or strong nucleophiles like NaOH at 300°C). (2) **Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution**: Halogens are ortho/para-directing deactivators (e.g., nitration, sulfonation). (3) **Fittig Reaction**: Two aryl halides react with sodium metal to form biaryls (Ar-X + 2Na + X-Ar → Ar-Ar + 2NaX). (4) **Wurtz-Fittig Reaction**: Aryl halide reacts with alkyl halide and sodium to form alkylbenzenes (Ar-X + 2Na + X-R → Ar-R + 2NaX). (5) **Grignard Reagent Formation**: Ar-X + Mg → Ar-Mg-X (in dry ether).'}
  • {'concept': 'Polyhalogen Compounds', 'content': 'Important polyhalogen compounds include: (1) **Chloroform (CHCl₃)**: Used as a solvent and anesthetic; oxidizes in air to form phosgene (COCl₂), a poisonous gas. (2) **Carbon Tetrachloride (CCl₄)**: Used as a solvent and fire extinguisher; toxic and carcinogenic. (3) **Freons (CFCs)**: Chlorofluorocarbons used as refrigerants; deplete the ozone layer. (4) **DDT (Dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane)**: Insecticide; banned due to environmental persistence and toxicity. (5) **Iodoform (CHI₃)**: Yellow solid with antiseptic properties; formed in the iodoform test for methyl ketones or alcohols with a CH₃-CH(OH)- group.'}

Important facts

  • The C-X bond length increases in the order: C-F < C-Cl < C-Br < C-I, while bond strength decreases in the same order.
