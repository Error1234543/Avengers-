NEET Chemistry Quick Revision: Coordination Compounds

NEETEnglishQuick Revision

Definitions

  • Coordination Compound: A compound consisting of a central metal atom or ion bonded to a group of surrounding molecules or ions (ligands) through coordinate covalent bonds.
  • Ligand: An ion or molecule that donates a pair of electrons to the central metal atom/ion to form a coordinate bond. Ligands can be monodentate (single donor atom) or polydentate (multiple donor atoms).
  • Coordination Number: The number of ligand donor atoms directly bonded to the central metal atom/ion in a coordination compound.
  • Chelate: A complex formed when a polydentate ligand binds to a central metal atom/ion, creating a ring structure. Chelates are more stable than similar complexes with monodentate ligands.
  • Crystal Field Theory (CFT): A model explaining the bonding, structure, and color of coordination compounds by considering the electrostatic interactions between the central metal ion and ligands.
  • Spectrochemical Series: A ranking of ligands based on their ability to split the d-orbital energies of the central metal ion in an octahedral complex: I⁻ < Br⁻ < Cl⁻ < F⁻ < OH⁻ < H₂O < NH₃ < en < NO₂⁻ < CN⁻ < CO.

Important facts

  • The coordination number of a metal ion is not fixed and depends on the size of the metal ion and ligands. Common coordination numbers: 2 (linear), 4 (tetrahedral or square planar), 6 (octahedral).
  • Chelating ligands (e.g., ethylenediamine, oxalate) form more stable complexes than monodentate ligands due to the chelate effect, which is primarily entropic in origin.
  • Square planar complexes (e.g., [Pt(NH₃)₂Cl₂]) exhibit geometric isomerism (cis and trans forms), while tetrahedral complexes do not due to their symmetry.
  • The color of coordination compounds is due to d-d transitions, which are forbidden by Laporte’s rule but become partially allowed due to vibronic coupling or symmetry distortions.
  • High-spin complexes have maximum unpaired electrons (weak field ligands, small Δ₀), while low-spin complexes have minimum unpaired electrons (strong field ligands, large Δ₀).
  • The spectrochemical series helps predict the magnitude of Δ₀: weak field ligands (e.g., I⁻, Br⁻) → small Δ₀ → high-spin complexes; strong field ligands (e.g., CN⁻, CO) → large Δ₀ → low-spin complexes.
  • Coordination compounds with ambidentate ligands (e.g., NO₂⁻, SCN⁻) can exhibit linkage isomerism, where the ligand binds through different donor atoms.
  • The stability of a chelate complex increases with the number of rings formed (e.g., EDTA, a hexadentate ligand, forms very stable complexes).

Memory aids

  • Nomenclature: 'Ligands first, metal last; alphabetical order, no prefix fuss; oxidation state in Roman, anion ends with -ate.'
  • Isomerism types: 'Structural: connectivity change (ionization, coordination, linkage). Stereo: spatial change (geometric, optical).'
  • Spectrochemical series: 'I Brought Clothes From Home, Not Expensive Ones; Cyanide and Carbonyl are Strong.' (I⁻ < Br⁻ < Cl⁻ < F⁻ < OH⁻ < H₂O < NH₃ < en < NO₂⁻ < CN⁻ < CO).
  • Crystal Field Splitting: 'Octahedral: t₂g down, eg up; Tetrahedral: e down, t₂ up (smaller Δₜ).'
  • Magnetic properties: 'High-spin: weak field, max unpaired; Low-spin: strong field, min unpaired.'
  • Chelate effect: 'More rings, more stability; entropy drives the party.'

Common traps

  • Confusing coordination number with oxidation state: Coordination number is the number of ligand donor atoms bonded to the metal, while oxidation state is the charge on the metal ion.
  • Misapplying nomenclature rules: Forgetting to name ligands alphabetically (ignoring prefixes) or incorrectly assigning the metal’s oxidation state.
  • Assuming all tetrahedral complexes are high-spin: While most are, exceptions exist (e.g., [Ni(CN)₄]²⁻ is square planar and diamagnetic).
  • Overlooking linkage isomerism: Not all ambidentate ligands exhibit linkage isomerism in every complex (e.g., NO₂⁻ in [Co(NH₃)₅(NO₂)]²⁺ can bind as nitro or nitrito, but SCN⁻ may prefer one mode depending on the metal).
  • Ignoring the chelate effect: Assuming monodentate ligands form more stable complexes than polydentate ligands (the opposite is true due to entropy).
  • Misinterpreting color: Assuming color arises from charge transfer transitions (common in some complexes) rather than d-d transitions (primary cause in most coordination compounds).
  • Confusing geometric and optical isomerism: Geometric isomers (cis-trans) are not optically active unless they lack a plane of symmetry (e.g., cis-[Co(en)₂Cl₂]⁺ has optical isomers, but trans does not).
