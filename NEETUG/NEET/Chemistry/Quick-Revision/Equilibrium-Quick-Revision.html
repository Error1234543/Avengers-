Definitions

  • {'term': 'Chemical Equilibrium', 'definition': 'A state in a reversible chemical reaction where the rate of the forward reaction equals the rate of the reverse reaction, resulting in constant concentrations of reactants and products over time. This is a dynamic equilibrium, not a static one, as both reactions continue to occur at equal rates.'}
  • {'term': 'Equilibrium Constant (K)', 'definition': 'A numerical value that expresses the relationship between the concentrations of products and reactants at equilibrium for a reversible reaction at a given temperature. For a general reaction aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD, the equilibrium constant expression is K = [C]^c [D]^d / [A]^a [B]^b, where [ ] denotes concentration in mol/L.'}
  • {'term': 'Le Chatelier’s Principle', 'definition': 'If a dynamic equilibrium is disturbed by changing the conditions (concentration, pressure, temperature), the system adjusts to counteract the change and restore equilibrium. This principle predicts the direction in which the equilibrium will shift.'}
  • {'term': 'Ionic Equilibrium', 'definition': 'The equilibrium established between the unionized molecules and the ions in a solution of weak electrolytes. It is fundamental to understanding acid-base behavior, solubility, and buffer systems.'}
  • {'term': 'Common Ion Effect', 'definition': 'The suppression of the dissociation of a weak electrolyte (e.g., weak acid or base) by the addition of a strong electrolyte that provides an ion common to the equilibrium. This shifts the equilibrium toward the undissociated form, reducing the concentration of the common ion.'}

Key concepts

  • {'concept': 'Equilibrium Constant Expression and Units', 'content': 'The equilibrium constant (K) is dimensionless for reactions where the number of moles of gaseous reactants and products are equal. For reactions with unequal moles, K has units of (mol/L)^Δn, where Δn = (moles of gaseous products) - (moles of gaseous reactants). For heterogeneous equilibria, pure solids and liquids are omitted from the expression as their concentrations remain constant.'}
  • {'concept': 'Relationship Between Kc and Kp', 'content': 'For gaseous reactions, the equilibrium constant can be expressed in terms of partial pressures (Kp) or concentrations (Kc). The two are related by the equation Kp = Kc (RT)^Δn, where R is the gas constant (0.0821 L·atm·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹), T is the temperature in Kelvin, and Δn is the change in moles of gas. This relationship is critical for converting between pressure-based and concentration-based equilibrium data.'}
  • {'concept': 'Factors Affecting Equilibrium (Le Chatelier’s Principle)', 'content': '1. **Concentration**: Increasing reactant concentration shifts equilibrium toward products; increasing product concentration shifts it toward reactants. 2. **Pressure**: For gaseous reactions, increasing pressure shifts equilibrium toward the side with fewer moles of gas. 3. **Temperature**: For exothermic reactions, increasing temperature shifts equilibrium toward reactants; for endothermic reactions, it shifts toward products. Catalysts do not affect equilibrium position but speed up the attainment of equilibrium.'}
  • {'concept': 'Ionization of Weak Acids and Bases', 'content': 'Weak acids (HA) and weak bases (B) partially ionize in water: HA ⇌ H⁺ + A⁻ and B + H₂O ⇌ BH⁺ + OH⁻. The ionization constant (Ka for acids, Kb for bases) quantifies the extent of ionization: Ka = [H⁺][A⁻]/[HA] and Kb = [BH⁺][OH⁻]/[B]. The smaller the Ka or Kb, the weaker the acid or base. For conjugate acid-base pairs, Ka × Kb = Kw (ionization constant of water, 1 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 25°C).'}
  • {'concept': 'Buffer Solutions', 'content': 'A buffer solution resists changes in pH when small amounts of acid or base are added. It consists of a weak acid and its conjugate base (or weak base and its conjugate acid) in comparable concentrations. The pH of a buffer is calculated using the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation: pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA]). Buffers are essential in biological systems (e.g., blood) and laboratory applications.'}
  • {'concept': 'Solubility Product (Ksp)', 'content': 'For a sparingly soluble salt (e.g., AgCl), the equilibrium between the solid and its ions in solution is described by the solubility product: AgCl(s) ⇌ Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq), Ksp = [Ag⁺][Cl⁻]. The solubility (s) of the salt is related to Ksp. For salts with unequal ion ratios (e.g., PbI₂), Ksp = [Pb²⁺][I⁻]² = 4s³. A higher Ksp indicates greater solubility. The common ion effect reduces solubility by shifting equilibrium toward the solid.'}
  • {'concept': 'Acid-Base Indicators', 'content': 'Indicators are weak acids or bases that exhibit different colors in their ionized and unionized forms. The color change occurs over a pH range (typically pKa ± 1) and is used to determine the endpoint of titrations. For example, phenolphthalein is colorless in acidic solutions and pink in basic solutions (pH 8.3–10.0). The choice of indicator depends on the pH range of the titration’s equivalence point.'}

Important facts

  • The equilibrium constant (K) is temperature-dependent. Changing the temperature alters K, while changes in concentration or pressure shift the equilibrium position but do not change K.
  • For a reaction quotient (Q) calculated at any point during the reaction: Q < K indicates the reaction proceeds forward to reach equilibrium; Q > K indicates the reaction proceeds backward; Q = K indicates the system is at equilibrium.
  • The degree of ionization (α) of a weak electrolyte is the fraction of total molecules that ionize. For a weak acid HA, α = [H⁺]/[HA]₀, where [HA]₀ is the initial concentration. α increases with dilution (Ostwald’s dilution law).
  • The pH scale is logarithmic: pH = -log[H⁺]. A solution with pH 3 is 10 times more acidic than a solution with pH 4. The pH of pure water is 7 at 25°C, but it decreases with increasing temperature due to the autoionization of water (Kw increases).
  • A buffer’s capacity is highest when the concentrations of the weak acid and its conjugate base are equal (pH = pKa). Buffer capacity also increases with higher concentrations of the buffer components.
  • The solubility of a salt decreases in the presence of a common ion (common ion effect) but may increase in the presence of a complexing agent or due to the formation of ion pairs.
  • For polyprotic acids (e.g., H₂CO₃, H₃PO₄), each ionization step has its own Ka (Ka₁ > Ka₂ > Ka₃). The first proton is the easiest to remove, and subsequent ionizations contribute negligibly to [H⁺] in most cases.

Memory aids

  • Le Chatelier’s Principle: Think of equilibrium as a seesaw. Adding weight (concentration) to one side tips it, so the system shifts to the other side to balance it again.
  • Kp vs. Kc: Remember the formula Kp = Kc (RT)^Δn. If Δn = 0, Kp = Kc. If Δn ≠ 0, use the formula to convert between them.
  • Henderson-Hasselbalch Equation: "pH = pKa + log(salt/acid)" can be remembered as "pH is pKa plus the log of the ratio of salt to acid."
  • Common Ion Effect: "Common ion, less soluble." Adding a common ion reduces the solubility of the salt.
  • Buffer Action: "Weak acid + its salt = buffer." For example, acetic acid (CH₃COOH) + sodium acetate (CH₃COONa) forms a buffer that resists pH changes.

Common traps

  • Confusing equilibrium constant (K) with reaction quotient (Q). K is fixed at a given temperature, while Q changes as the reaction proceeds and is used to predict the direction of the reaction.
  • Assuming all reactions reach equilibrium. Some reactions (e.g., combustion) are irreversible and go to completion, while others (e.g., weak acid ionization) establish equilibrium.
  • Ignoring the units of K. While K is often treated as dimensionless, it technically has units for reactions with Δn ≠ 0. Always check the context of the question.
  • Misapplying Le Chatelier’s Principle to catalysts. Catalysts speed up both forward and reverse reactions equally, so they do not shift the equilibrium position.
  • Forgetting that pure solids and liquids are omitted from equilibrium expressions. For example, in CaCO₃(s) ⇌ CaO(s) + CO₂(g), Kp = P_CO₂, not [CaO][CO₂]/[CaCO₃].
  • Assuming that a higher Ksp always means higher solubility. This is true only for salts with the same ion ratio (e.g., comparing AgCl and AgBr). For salts like Ag₂CrO₄ and AgCl, the relationship between Ksp and solubility is not direct due to different stoichiometries.
