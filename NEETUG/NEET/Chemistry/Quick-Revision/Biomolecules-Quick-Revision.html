NEET Chemistry Quick Revision: Biomolecules

NEETEnglishQuick Revision

Definitions

  • Biomolecules: Organic compounds that are essential constituents of living organisms, including carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, nucleic acids, and vitamins.
  • Carbohydrates: Polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones, or substances that yield such compounds on hydrolysis. Classified as monosaccharides, oligosaccharides, and polysaccharides based on the number of sugar units.
  • Proteins: Polymers of amino acids linked by peptide bonds. They perform structural, catalytic, transport, and regulatory functions in living systems.
  • Lipids: Heterogeneous group of compounds insoluble in water but soluble in organic solvents. Include fats, oils, phospholipids, steroids, and waxes.
  • Nucleic Acids: Polynucleotides that store and transmit genetic information. DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) and RNA (ribonucleic acid) are the two main types.
  • Enzymes: Biological catalysts, mostly proteins, that speed up biochemical reactions without being consumed. They exhibit specificity and are sensitive to temperature and pH.
  • Vitamins: Organic compounds required in small amounts for normal metabolic functions. Classified as fat-soluble (A, D, E, K) or water-soluble (B-complex, C).

Important facts

  • Glucose is the primary energy source for cells and is stored as glycogen in animals and starch in plants.
  • Cellulose, a structural polysaccharide in plants, cannot be digested by humans due to the absence of cellulase enzyme.
  • Essential amino acids cannot be synthesized by the body and must be obtained from the diet. There are 9 essential amino acids for humans.
  • Hemoglobin is a conjugated protein with a quaternary structure, consisting of four polypeptide chains (2 α and 2 β) and four heme groups.
  • Phospholipids spontaneously form bilayers in aqueous environments due to their amphipathic nature, which is critical for cell membrane structure.
  • DNA replication is semi-conservative, meaning each new DNA molecule consists of one original strand and one newly synthesized strand.
  • The genetic code is degenerate, meaning multiple codons can code for the same amino acid (e.g., UUU and UUC both code for phenylalanine).
  • Enzymes like pepsin (stomach) and trypsin (pancreas) are proteases that break down proteins into smaller peptides or amino acids.
  • Lactose intolerance arises from the deficiency of lactase enzyme, leading to the inability to digest lactose (milk sugar).
  • Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is essential for collagen synthesis and acts as an antioxidant. Its deficiency causes scurvy.

Memory aids

  • To remember the essential amino acids: **PVT TIM HALL** (Phenylalanine, Valine, Threonine, Tryptophan, Isoleucine, Methionine, Histidine, Arginine, Leucine, Lysine).
  • For the bases in DNA: **AT GC** (Adenine pairs with Thymine, Guanine pairs with Cytosine). In RNA, Thymine is replaced by Uracil (AU GC).
  • To recall fat-soluble vitamins: **ADEK** (Vitamins A, D, E, K).
  • For the classification of carbohydrates: **Mono- (1), Oligo- (few), Poly- (many)** sugar units.
  • To remember the structure of proteins: **Primary (sequence), Secondary (helix/sheet), Tertiary (3D), Quaternary (complex)**.

Exam tips

  • Focus on **structural differences** between biomolecules, such as the types of bonds (e.g., glycosidic in carbohydrates, peptide in proteins, phosphodiester in nucleic acids).
  • Memorize **key examples** of each biomolecule class (e.g., glucose for monosaccharides, hemoglobin for proteins, cholesterol for steroids).
  • Understand **biological functions** of biomolecules, such as energy storage (glycogen, starch), structural support (cellulose, keratin), and genetic information (DNA, RNA).
  • Practice **numerical problems** related to biomolecules, such as calculating the number of peptide bonds in a polypeptide or the energy yield from glucose metabolism.
  • Review **enzyme kinetics** concepts like Michaelis-Menten constant (Km) and how inhibitors affect enzyme activity. NEET often tests these graphically.
  • Pay attention to **vitamin deficiencies** and their associated diseases (e.g., night blindness for Vitamin A, rickets for Vitamin D, beriberi for Vitamin B1).
  • For nucleic acids, focus on **replication, transcription, and translation** processes, including the roles of mRNA, tRNA, and rRNA.
  • In diagrams, label **functional groups** (e.g., aldehyde in glucose, carboxyl in amino acids) and **structural features** (e.g., α-helix, β-pleated sheet in proteins).
  • Avoid mixing up **isomers** of biomolecules, such as glucose (aldohexose) and fructose (ketohexose), or α-glucose and β-glucose (anomers).
  • For quick revision, use **flashcards** to memorize definitions, structures, and functions of key biomolecules. Focus on high-yield topics like enzymes, DNA structure, and amino acids.

Common traps

  • Confusing **starch** (storage polysaccharide in plants) with **glycogen** (storage polysaccharide in animals). Both are polymers of glucose but differ in branching.
  • Assuming all **lipids are fats**. Lipids include fats, oils, phospholipids, steroids, and waxes, each with distinct structures and functions.
  • Misidentifying **peptide bonds** as the only bonds in protein structure. While peptide bonds link amino acids, disulfide bridges, hydrogen bonds, and hydrophobic interactions stabilize higher-order structures.
  • Overlooking the **difference between DNA and RNA** beyond the sugar (deoxyribose vs. ribose). RNA is typically single-stranded and contains uracil instead of thymine.
  • Assuming **all enzymes are proteins**. While most enzymes are proteins, some RNA molecules (ribozymes) also exhibit catalytic activity.
  • Forgetting that **vitamin D can be synthesized by the body** upon exposure to sunlight, unlike other vitamins that must be obtained from the diet.
  • Confusing **competitive inhibition** (inhibitor binds to active site) with **non-competitive inhibition** (inhibitor binds elsewhere, altering enzyme shape).
