Definitions

  • {'term': 'Thermodynamics', 'definition': 'The branch of physical chemistry that deals with the study of energy changes, particularly heat and work, during chemical reactions and physical transformations. It focuses on the macroscopic properties of systems and does not concern itself with the molecular nature of matter.'}
  • {'term': 'System', 'definition': 'A specific portion of matter or space under thermodynamic study, separated from the surroundings by a real or imaginary boundary. Systems can be open (exchange of matter and energy), closed (exchange of energy only), or isolated (no exchange of matter or energy).'}
  • {'term': 'Surroundings', 'definition': 'Everything outside the thermodynamic system that can interact with it, typically through the exchange of heat or work.'}
  • {'term': 'State Function', 'definition': 'A property of a system that depends only on the current state of the system and not on the path taken to reach that state. Examples include internal energy (U), enthalpy (H), entropy (S), and Gibbs free energy (G).'}
  • {'term': 'Path Function', 'definition': 'A property that depends on the path taken to reach a particular state, such as work (w) and heat (q).'}
  • {'term': 'Internal Energy (U)', 'definition': 'The total energy contained within a system, including kinetic and potential energy at the molecular level. It is a state function and cannot be measured absolutely, but changes in internal energy (ΔU) can be determined.'}
  • {'term': 'Enthalpy (H)', 'definition': 'A thermodynamic property defined as H = U + PV, where U is internal energy, P is pressure, and V is volume. Enthalpy is a state function and is particularly useful for studying processes at constant pressure, such as most chemical reactions.'}
  • {'term': 'Entropy (S)', 'definition': "A measure of the disorder or randomness of a system. It is a state function and quantifies the number of microscopic configurations (microstates) that correspond to a thermodynamic system's macroscopic state."}
  • {'term': 'Gibbs Free Energy (G)', 'definition': 'A thermodynamic potential defined as G = H - TS, where H is enthalpy, T is temperature, and S is entropy. It is used to predict the spontaneity of a process at constant temperature and pressure: a negative ΔG indicates a spontaneous process.'}

Key concepts

  • {'concept': 'First Law of Thermodynamics', 'content': 'The first law of thermodynamics is the law of conservation of energy, stated as: ΔU = q + w, where ΔU is the change in internal energy of the system, q is the heat added to the system, and w is the work done on the system. For a process at constant volume, ΔU = q_v (heat at constant volume). For a process at constant pressure, q_p = ΔH (change in enthalpy). Work done by the system (expansion) is considered negative, while work done on the system (compression) is positive.'}
  • {'concept': 'Second Law of Thermodynamics', 'content': 'The second law introduces the concept of entropy and states that for any spontaneous process, the total entropy of the universe (system + surroundings) always increases. Mathematically, ΔS_universe = ΔS_system + ΔS_surroundings > 0 for a spontaneous process. This law explains the direction of natural processes and the irreversibility of certain transformations.'}
  • {'concept': 'Third Law of Thermodynamics', 'content': 'The third law states that the entropy of a perfect crystalline substance approaches zero as the temperature approaches absolute zero (0 K). This provides a reference point for the determination of absolute entropies of substances.'}
  • {'concept': 'Spontaneity and Gibbs Free Energy', 'content': 'The spontaneity of a process at constant temperature and pressure is determined by the change in Gibbs free energy (ΔG). The criteria are: ΔG < 0 (spontaneous), ΔG = 0 (equilibrium), ΔG > 0 (non-spontaneous). ΔG can be calculated using ΔG = ΔH - TΔS, where ΔH is the enthalpy change, T is the temperature in Kelvin, and ΔS is the entropy change. This equation highlights the competition between enthalpy and entropy in determining spontaneity.'}
  • {'concept': 'Standard Enthalpy Changes', 'content': 'Standard enthalpy changes refer to enthalpy changes measured under standard conditions (1 bar pressure, 298 K temperature, and 1 M concentration for solutions). Key types include: (1) Standard Enthalpy of Formation (ΔH_f°): Enthalpy change when 1 mole of a compound is formed from its constituent elements in their standard states. (2) Standard Enthalpy of Combustion (ΔH_c°): Enthalpy change when 1 mole of a substance is completely burned in oxygen. (3) Standard Enthalpy of Reaction (ΔH_r°): Enthalpy change for a reaction as written, calculated using ΔH_r° = Σ ΔH_f°(products) - Σ ΔH_f°(reactants).'}
  • {'concept': "Hess's Law", 'content': "Hess's Law states that the total enthalpy change for a reaction is the same regardless of the pathway taken or the number of steps involved. This is a consequence of enthalpy being a state function. Hess's Law is used to calculate enthalpy changes for reactions that are difficult to measure directly by combining known enthalpy changes of other reactions."}
  • {'concept': 'Entropy Changes in Reactions', 'content': 'The entropy change for a reaction (ΔS_r°) can be calculated using standard molar entropies (S°) of the reactants and products: ΔS_r° = Σ S°(products) - Σ S°(reactants). Entropy increases with: (1) phase changes from solid to liquid to gas, (2) an increase in the number of moles of gas, (3) an increase in temperature, and (4) dissolution of solids or liquids in solvents.'}
  • {'concept': 'Work in Thermodynamics', 'content': 'Work in thermodynamics is often associated with the expansion or compression of gases. For a reversible isothermal expansion of an ideal gas, work done by the system is given by w = -nRT ln(V_f/V_i), where n is the number of moles, R is the gas constant, T is temperature, and V_f and V_i are final and initial volumes, respectively. For irreversible processes, work done is less than the reversible work.'}

Important facts

  • The standard state of an element is its most stable form at 1 bar pressure and 298 K (e.g., O₂(g), C(graphite), H₂(g)). The standard enthalpy of formation of an element in its standard state is zero.
  • For an ideal gas, internal energy (U) depends only on temperature. Thus, ΔU = 0 for an isothermal process involving an ideal gas.
  • The enthalpy change for a reaction (ΔH) is equal in magnitude but opposite in sign for the reverse reaction.
  • A process with ΔH > 0 (endothermic) can still be spontaneous if ΔS > 0 and the temperature is sufficiently high, as ΔG = ΔH - TΔS will become negative.
  • The entropy of the universe is constantly increasing, which is why certain processes (e.g., heat flow from hot to cold) are irreversible.
  • For a reaction to be spontaneous at all temperatures, both ΔH must be negative (exothermic) and ΔS must be positive (increase in disorder).
  • The Gibbs free energy change (ΔG) for a reaction can also be calculated using the standard Gibbs free energy of formation (ΔG_f°): ΔG_r° = Σ ΔG_f°(products) - Σ ΔG_f°(reactants).
  • The relationship between the equilibrium constant (K) and the standard Gibbs free energy change is given by ΔG° = -RT ln K, where R is the gas constant and T is the temperature in Kelvin.
