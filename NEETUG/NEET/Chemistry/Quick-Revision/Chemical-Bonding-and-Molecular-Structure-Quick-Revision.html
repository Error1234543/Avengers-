NEET Quick Revision: Chemical Bonding and Molecular Structure

NEETEnglishQuick Revision

Definitions

  • Chemical Bond: A force of attraction between atoms or ions that holds them together in a molecule or crystal.
  • Ionic Bond: Electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions formed by complete transfer of electrons from one atom to another.
  • Covalent Bond: Bond formed by the sharing of electron pairs between atoms.
  • Coordinate Bond: A type of covalent bond where both electrons in the shared pair come from the same atom.
  • Electronegativity: The ability of an atom to attract shared electrons in a covalent bond.
  • Bond Length: The equilibrium distance between the nuclei of two bonded atoms.
  • Bond Angle: The angle between two bonds originating from the same atom in a molecule.
  • Bond Order: The number of chemical bonds between a pair of atoms (single bond = 1, double bond = 2, triple bond = 3).
  • Hybridisation: The concept of mixing atomic orbitals to form new hybrid orbitals suitable for the pairing of electrons to form chemical bonds.
  • Molecular Orbital: A region in space where electrons are likely to be found in a molecule, formed by the combination of atomic orbitals.

Important facts

  • Ionic compounds have high melting/boiling points due to strong electrostatic forces; covalent compounds have lower melting/boiling points (except giant covalent structures like diamond).
  • Bond length decreases with increasing bond order (single > double > triple).
  • Bond energy increases with bond order (triple > double > single).
  • Lone pairs occupy more space than bonding pairs, reducing bond angles (e.g., H₂O bond angle is 104.5°, less than tetrahedral 109.5°).
  • Molecules with odd number of electrons (e.g., NO) are paramagnetic; MOT explains this via unpaired electrons in molecular orbitals.
  • Hydrogen bonding is strongest when H is bonded to F, weaker with O, and weakest with N.
  • Resonance energy is the difference in energy between the actual molecule and the most stable resonance structure.
  • Dipole moment is zero for homonuclear diatomic molecules (e.g., H₂, O₂) and symmetrical polyatomic molecules (e.g., CO₂, BF₃).
  • Hybridisation does not occur in isolated atoms; it is a concept applied to bonded atoms in molecules.
  • Molecular orbital diagrams differ for diatomic molecules of elements before and after O₂ (e.g., B₂, C₂ vs. N₂, O₂, F₂) due to s-p mixing.

Memory aids

  • VSEPR Shapes: "2 Linear, 3 Trigonal, 4 Tetra, 5 Bipyramid, 6 Octa" (for 2 to 6 electron pairs).
  • Hybridisation: "sp (2), sp² (3), sp³ (4), sp³d (5), sp³d² (6)" — count the number of electron pairs around the central atom.
  • Electronegativity Trend: "FONClBrISCH" (F > O > N > Cl > Br > I > S > C > H) — helps predict bond polarity.
  • Molecular Orbital Order: "σ1s < σ*1s < σ2s < σ*2s < π2p (for B₂, C₂) < σ2p < π*2p < σ*2p" — remember s-p mixing for B₂, C₂.
  • Hydrogen Bonding: "H with FON" — Hydrogen bonding occurs when H is bonded to F, O, or N.

Common traps

  • Confusing bond order with number of bonds: Bond order can be fractional (e.g., O₂⁺ has bond order 2.5), not just integer values.
  • Assuming all molecules with polar bonds are polar: Symmetrical molecules (e.g., CO₂, BF₃) have zero dipole moment despite polar bonds.
  • Misapplying VSEPR: Lone pairs occupy more space than bonding pairs, so they reduce bond angles (e.g., H₂O bond angle is less than NH₃).
  • Incorrect hybridisation: Hybridisation is determined by the number of electron pairs (bonding + lone pairs), not just the number of atoms bonded.
  • Forgetting s-p mixing in MOT: For B₂ and C₂, the σ2p orbital is higher in energy than π2p orbitals, unlike O₂ and F₂.
  • Assuming all odd-electron molecules are paramagnetic: While most are, some (e.g., NO) have paired electrons in MOT and are diamagnetic (though NO is actually paramagnetic due to one unpaired electron).
  • Overlooking resonance: Not considering resonance can lead to incorrect bond lengths or bond orders (e.g., O₃ has bond order 1.5, not 1 or 2).
