NEET Quick Revision: Hydrogen and s-Block Elements

NEETEnglishQuick Revision

Definitions

  • Hydrogen (H): The first element in the periodic table with atomic number 1, existing as diatomic H₂ gas under standard conditions. It exhibits unique properties due to its single proton and electron, making it distinct from both alkali metals and halogens.
  • s-Block Elements: Elements in Groups 1 (alkali metals) and 2 (alkaline earth metals) of the periodic table, characterized by the filling of their outermost s-orbital. They are highly reactive, electropositive, and form basic oxides/hydroxides.
  • Isotopes of Hydrogen: Protium (¹H, no neutron), Deuterium (²H or D, one neutron), and Tritium (³H or T, two neutrons). Deuterium oxide (D₂O) is known as heavy water, used in nuclear reactors.
  • Hydrides: Binary compounds of hydrogen with other elements. Classified into ionic (saline, e.g., NaH), covalent (molecular, e.g., CH₄), and metallic (interstitial, e.g., PdH).
  • Diagonal Relationship: Similarities between certain elements of the second period and the third period diagonally adjacent (e.g., Li-Mg, Be-Al). Explained by comparable charge/radius ratios and electronegativities.

Important facts

  • Hydrogen is the most abundant element in the universe (~75% by mass) but ranks 9th in Earth's crust (~0.14% by mass).
  • Heavy water (D₂O) has higher boiling point (101.4°C) and density (1.107 g/mL) than H₂O due to stronger hydrogen bonding.
  • Lithium is the lightest metal (density: 0.53 g/cm³) and is used in rechargeable batteries (Li-ion cells).
  • Sodium and potassium are stored under kerosene to prevent reaction with atmospheric oxygen/moisture.
  • Beryllium and its compounds are toxic (e.g., berylliosis, a lung disease).
  • Radium (Ra) is radioactive and was historically used in luminous paints (e.g., watch dials).
  • The Solvay process for Na₂CO₃ production involves the reaction: 2NH₃ + H₂O + CO₂ + NaCl → NaHCO₃ + NH₄Cl, followed by thermal decomposition of NaHCO₃.
  • Hard water contains Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺ ions, causing scaling in pipes and reduced soap lathering. Temporary hardness (due to bicarbonates) is removed by boiling; permanent hardness (due to sulfates/chlorides) requires ion-exchange resins or washing soda.

Exam tips

  • Focus on trends: Ionization enthalpy, atomic/ionic radii, and reactivity trends (increase down Group 1, decrease down Group 2 for hydration enthalpy).
  • Memorize key reactions: e.g., 2Na + O₂ → Na₂O₂ (peroxide), 2K + O₂ → KO₂ (superoxide), Ca + 2H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ + H₂ (vigorous reaction).
  • Prioritize biological roles: Na⁺/K⁺ pump, Ca²⁺ in bones, Mg²⁺ in chlorophyll. NEET often tests these in biology-chemistry overlap questions.
  • Practice numericals: e.g., calculating hardness of water (ppm of CaCO₃), percentage of deuterium in heavy water, or yield in Solvay process.
  • Diagrams: Be prepared to label the Solvay process flow chart or the electrolysis of brine (NaCl) to produce NaOH, Cl₂, and H₂.

Common traps

  • Confusing hydrogen’s position in the periodic table: It is not an alkali metal or halogen, despite similarities. Its unique properties place it separately.
  • Assuming all alkali metals react with nitrogen: Only lithium forms Li₃N; others do not react directly with N₂.
  • Misidentifying flame test colors: Sodium’s intense yellow often masks other colors (use cobalt glass to filter it out).
  • Overlooking beryllium’s amphoteric nature: BeO reacts with both acids (BeO + 2HCl → BeCl₂ + H₂O) and bases (BeO + 2NaOH → Na₂BeO₂ + H₂O).
  • Forgetting that alkaline earth metals form peroxides/superoxides: Ba and Sr form peroxides (e.g., BaO₂) on heating in oxygen, unlike Mg/Ca.
  • Incorrectly recalling the Solvay process: It does not directly produce Na₂CO₃ from NaCl and CO₂; intermediate NaHCO₃ is formed first.
  • Assuming all hydrides are ionic: Only Group 1/2 metals form ionic hydrides (e.g., NaH); others form covalent (e.g., CH₄) or metallic hydrides (e.g., PdH).
