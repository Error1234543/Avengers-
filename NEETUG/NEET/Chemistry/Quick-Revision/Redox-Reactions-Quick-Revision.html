Important facts

  • The oxidation number of fluorine is always -1 in all its compounds, as it is the most electronegative element.
  • In a redox reaction, the total number of electrons lost in oxidation must equal the total number of electrons gained in reduction.
  • The oxidizing power of halogens decreases down the group: F₂ > Cl₂ > Br₂ > I₂, while their reducing power increases in the same order.
  • Potassium permanganate (KMnO₄) acts as a strong oxidizing agent in acidic, neutral, and basic media, but its reduction product varies: Mn²⁺ (acidic), MnO₂ (neutral), and MnO₄²⁻ (basic).
  • In the reaction between KMnO₄ and oxalic acid (H₂C₂O₄), Mn²⁺ acts as an autocatalyst, speeding up the reaction as it proceeds.
  • The Nernst equation relates the reduction potential of a half-cell to the standard electrode potential, temperature, and concentrations of species involved: E = E° - (RT/nF) ln(Q).
  • A substance with a higher (more positive) reduction potential will oxidize a substance with a lower reduction potential.
  • The electrochemical series ranks metals based on their standard electrode potentials, predicting their reactivity and displacement tendencies.

Exam tips

  • For numerical problems, always write the balanced redox equation first. This ensures you account for stoichiometric coefficients in calculations (e.g., molarity, equivalents).
  • In titration problems involving KMnO₄ or K₂Cr₂O₇, remember that the equivalent weight depends on the medium (acidic/basic) and the change in oxidation number.
  • When asked to identify oxidizing/reducing agents, look for changes in oxidation numbers. The substance whose oxidation number increases is the reducing agent, and the one whose oxidation number decreases is the oxidizing agent.

Common traps

  • Confusing oxidation number with valency. Oxidation number is a hypothetical charge, while valency is the combining capacity of an element. For example, carbon has a valency of 4 but can have oxidation numbers ranging from -4 to +4.
  • Assuming all reactions involving oxygen are redox reactions. For example, the reaction CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ is not a redox reaction, as oxidation numbers remain unchanged.
  • Forgetting to balance charges in half-reactions. Always ensure the total charge on both sides of a half-reaction is equal after adding electrons.
  • Misidentifying disproportionation reactions. Not all reactions where a single substance reacts are disproportionation; the substance must undergo both oxidation and reduction.
  • Incorrectly assigning oxidation numbers in organic compounds. For example, in ethanol (C₂H₅OH), the oxidation number of carbon is not uniform: one carbon is -3, and the other is -1.
  • Overlooking the role of the medium (acidic/basic) when balancing redox reactions. The products and balancing steps differ significantly between acidic and basic media.
  • Assuming all metals can displace hydrogen from acids. Only metals above hydrogen in the electrochemical series (e.g., Zn, Fe) can do so; metals like Cu and Ag cannot.
