Important facts

  • Carbon forms strong covalent bonds with other carbon atoms and elements like hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, and halogens, leading to a vast number of organic compounds.
  • The presence of functional groups in organic molecules largely determines their chemical reactivity and physical properties.
  • In the IUPAC nomenclature, the suffix '-ane' is used for alkanes, '-ene' for alkenes, '-yne' for alkynes, '-ol' for alcohols, '-al' for aldehydes, '-one' for ketones, and '-oic acid' for carboxylic acids.
  • Geometrical isomerism is possible in alkenes due to restricted rotation around the carbon-carbon double bond, leading to cis and trans forms.
  • Optical isomerism occurs in compounds containing at least one chiral carbon (a carbon atom bonded to four different groups), resulting in enantiomers that rotate plane-polarized light in opposite directions.
  • The inductive effect decreases rapidly with distance from the substituent and is a permanent effect, while the electromeric effect is temporary and occurs only in the presence of a reagent.
  • Resonance structures contribute to the stability of molecules like benzene, where delocalized pi electrons are spread over the entire ring.
  • Carbocations are stabilized by electron-donating groups (e.g., alkyl groups) through the inductive effect and hyperconjugation, while carbanions are stabilized by electron-withdrawing groups.

Exam tips

  • Focus on the IUPAC nomenclature rules, as questions often test your ability to name complex organic compounds or identify the correct structure from a given name.
  • Understand the conditions for geometrical and optical isomerism, as NEET frequently tests these concepts with specific examples.
  • Memorize the priority order of functional groups for nomenclature, as this is a common source of errors in naming questions.
  • Practice drawing resonance structures for molecules like benzene, carboxylate ions, and allylic systems, as resonance stability is a recurring theme in reaction mechanisms.
  • Be thorough with the stability order of carbocations, carbanions, and free radicals, as this determines the major products in many organic reactions.
  • Pay attention to the electronic displacement effects (inductive, resonance, electromeric, hyperconjugation) as they explain the reactivity and orientation in organic reactions.

Common traps

  • Confusing structural isomers with stereoisomers: Structural isomers differ in the connectivity of atoms, while stereoisomers have the same connectivity but differ in spatial arrangement.
  • Misidentifying the parent chain in IUPAC nomenclature: Always select the longest continuous carbon chain, even if it is not drawn in a straight line.
  • Incorrectly assigning priority in naming compounds with multiple functional groups: The functional group with the highest priority determines the suffix, while others are treated as prefixes.
  • Assuming all alkenes exhibit geometrical isomerism: Geometrical isomerism is only possible if each carbon of the double bond is attached to two different groups.
  • Overlooking the stability of reaction intermediates: For example, tertiary carbocations are more stable than primary carbocations, which influences the major product in reactions like SN1.
  • Misapplying electronic effects: The inductive effect is permanent and decreases with distance, while the electromeric effect is temporary and occurs only during a reaction.
  • Confusing enantiomers with diastereomers: Enantiomers are mirror images, while diastereomers are not mirror images and include geometrical isomers.
