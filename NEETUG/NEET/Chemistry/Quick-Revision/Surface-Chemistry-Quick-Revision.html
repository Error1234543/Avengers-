NEET Chemistry Quick Revision: Surface Chemistry

NEETEnglishQuick Revision

Definitions

  • Surface Chemistry: The branch of chemistry that deals with phenomena occurring at the surfaces or interfaces of substances, including adsorption, catalysis, colloids, and emulsions.
  • Adsorption: The accumulation of molecules of a substance (adsorbate) on the surface of a solid or liquid (adsorbent) due to unbalanced forces at the surface.
  • Absorption: The process in which a substance is uniformly distributed throughout the bulk of another substance, unlike adsorption which is a surface phenomenon.
  • Catalysis: The acceleration of a chemical reaction by a catalyst, which remains chemically unchanged at the end of the reaction. Catalysts provide an alternative pathway with lower activation energy.
  • Colloids: Heterogeneous systems in which one substance (dispersed phase) is dispersed as particles (1 nm to 1 µm) in another substance (dispersion medium). They exhibit properties like the Tyndall effect and Brownian motion.
  • Emulsion: A colloidal system in which both the dispersed phase and dispersion medium are liquids (e.g., oil in water or water in oil).

Important facts

  • Hardy-Schulze Rule: The coagulating power of an electrolyte increases with the valency of the active ion (e.g., Al³⁺ > Ba²⁺ > Na⁺ for negatively charged colloids).
  • Gold Number: The minimum mass (in mg) of a protective colloid (e.g., gelatin) required to prevent the coagulation of 10 mL of a standard gold sol when 1 mL of 10% NaCl solution is added. Lower gold number = higher protective power.
  • Zeta Potential: The potential difference between the dispersion medium and the stationary layer of fluid attached to the dispersed particle. Determines colloidal stability; higher zeta potential = more stable colloid.
  • Catalyst Poisoning: Deactivation of a catalyst by impurities (e.g., arsenic in the Haber process). Temporary poisoning can be reversed; permanent poisoning requires catalyst replacement.
  • Promoters: Substances that enhance the activity of a catalyst (e.g., Mo in Fe catalyst for the Haber process).
  • Shape-Selective Catalysis: Catalysis by zeolites, where the reaction depends on the pore size and shape of the catalyst (e.g., ZSM-5 in petroleum refining).
  • Applications of Adsorption: (1) Gas masks (activated charcoal adsorbs toxic gases). (2) Chromatography (separation based on adsorption affinity). (3) Heterogeneous catalysis (e.g., catalytic converters in vehicles).

Memory aids

  • Adsorption vs. Absorption: **Adsorption** = **A**t the **S**urface (like a sticker on a wall); **Absorption** = **A**ll **B**ulk (like a sponge soaking water).
  • Freundlich Isotherm: Remember the linear form **log(x/m) = log k + (1/n)log P** as "Log X over M equals Log K plus one over N Log P."
  • Hardy-Schulze Rule: **H**igher **V**alency = **H**arder **C**oagulation (e.g., Al³⁺ > Na⁺).
  • Tyndall Effect: Think of a **T**orchlight beam visible in a **D**usty room (colloidal particles scatter light).
  • Gold Number: **G**elatin **P**rotects **G**old sol (lower gold number = better protector).

Common traps

  • Confusing physisorption and chemisorption: Physisorption is reversible and multilayered; chemisorption is irreversible and monolayered. NEET often tests this distinction in assertion-reason questions.
  • Misapplying adsorption isotherms: Freundlich isotherm is empirical and fails at high pressures; Langmuir assumes monolayer adsorption. Do not use Freundlich for chemisorption.
  • Ignoring units in adsorption calculations: Ensure x/m (mass of adsorbate per unit mass of adsorbent) is in consistent units (e.g., g/g or mol/g).
  • Overlooking the role of temperature: Physisorption decreases with temperature, but chemisorption may increase initially. NEET questions often ask about temperature effects.
  • Misidentifying colloid types: Aerosols (liquid/solid in gas) are often confused with emulsions (liquid in liquid). Remember: aerosols involve a gas dispersion medium.
  • Forgetting the Hardy-Schulze rule: For negatively charged colloids, Al³⁺ is a stronger coagulant than Na⁺, not the other way around.
