NEET Chemistry Quick Revision: p-Block Elements (Group 13 & 14)

NEETEnglishQuick Revision

Definitions

  • p-Block Elements: Elements in which the last electron enters the p-orbital of the outermost energy level. Groups 13 to 18 of the periodic table constitute the p-block.
  • Inert Pair Effect: The tendency of the two electrons in the outermost s-orbital to remain unshared in compounds of post-transition metals, leading to a lower oxidation state than the group valence.
  • Catenation: The ability of an element to form chains or rings by bonding with itself, prominently observed in carbon (Group 14).
  • Allotropy: The existence of an element in two or more different forms in the same physical state, differing in physical properties but having similar chemical properties (e.g., diamond, graphite, and fullerenes for carbon).

Important facts

  • Boron does not occur in free state in nature; it is found as borax (Na₂B₄O₇·10H₂O) or kernite (Na₂B₄O₇·4H₂O).
  • Aluminium is the most abundant metal in the Earth's crust and is extracted from bauxite (Al₂O₃·2H₂O) via the Hall-Héroult process.
  • Carbon dioxide (CO₂) is a linear molecule with sp hybridization, while silicon dioxide (SiO₂) forms a three-dimensional network solid.
  • Tin exists in two allotropic forms: grey tin (α-tin, cubic structure) and white tin (β-tin, tetragonal structure). Grey tin is stable below 13.2°C and is a semiconductor.
  • Lead is highly toxic and accumulates in the body, causing damage to the nervous system and kidneys. Its use in paints and gasoline has been phased out.
  • The diagonal relationship between boron (Group 13) and silicon (Group 14) is due to similar charge-to-radius ratios, leading to comparable properties (e.g., formation of covalent hydrides and oxides).
  • Carbon monoxide (CO) is a neutral oxide and a strong reducing agent, used in metallurgy to extract iron from its ore.

Memory aids

  • BAT for Group 13: Boron (B), Aluminium (Al), Thallium (Tl) — remember the inert pair effect makes Tl prefer +1 over +3.
  • C Si Ge Sn Pb for Group 14: 'Can Silly Germans Snatch Lead?' — helps recall the order of elements.
  • Diamond vs. Graphite: 'Diamond is forever' (hard, insulator), 'Graphite writes' (soft, conductor).
  • Inert Pair Effect Mnemonic: 'The heavier the metal, the lazier the s-electrons' — explains stability of lower oxidation states in Tl and Pb.

Exam tips

  • Focus on the anomalous properties of boron and carbon, as these are frequently tested in NEET.
  • Memorize the oxidation states of Group 13 and 14 elements, especially the stability of +1 and +2 states in heavier elements.
  • Understand the structure and properties of carbon allotropes (diamond, graphite, fullerenes) and their applications.
  • Practice questions on the acidic/basic/amphoteric nature of oxides and halides, as these are common in NEET.
  • Review the diagonal relationship between boron and silicon, and be prepared to compare their properties.
  • Pay attention to the inert pair effect and its impact on the stability of oxidation states in thallium and lead.
  • For numerical questions, focus on stoichiometry involving borax, aluminium extraction, and carbon/silicon compounds.

Common traps

  • Confusing the oxidation states of thallium and lead: Thallium commonly exhibits +1 (not +3), and lead commonly exhibits +2 (not +4) due to the inert pair effect.
  • Assuming all Group 13 elements form ionic compounds: Boron forms covalent compounds, while aluminium can form both ionic and covalent compounds depending on the context.
  • Misidentifying the nature of oxides: Al₂O₃ is amphoteric, not basic; CO is neutral, not acidic.
  • Overlooking the diagonal relationship: Boron and silicon share similarities, but this does not extend to other Group 13 and 14 elements.
  • Assuming all Group 14 hydrides are stable: Silanes and germanes are less stable and more reactive than hydrocarbons.
