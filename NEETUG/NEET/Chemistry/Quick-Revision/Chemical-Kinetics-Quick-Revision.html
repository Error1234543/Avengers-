NEET Chemistry Quick Revision: Chemical Kinetics

NEETEnglishQuick Revision

Definitions

  • Chemical Kinetics: The branch of physical chemistry that studies the rate of chemical reactions, the factors affecting these rates, and the mechanisms by which reactions proceed.
  • Rate of Reaction: The change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time. It is expressed as -d[R]/dt or +d[P]/dt, where [R] and [P] are the concentrations of reactant and product, respectively.
  • Rate Law: An expression that relates the rate of a reaction to the concentrations of the reactants, each raised to a power determined experimentally. For a reaction aA + bB → products, rate = k[A]^m[B]^n, where m and n are the orders of the reaction with respect to A and B, and k is the rate constant.
  • Order of Reaction: The sum of the exponents of the concentration terms in the rate law. It can be zero, first, second, or fractional, and is determined experimentally.
  • Rate Constant (k): A proportionality constant in the rate law that is characteristic of the reaction at a given temperature. Its units depend on the overall order of the reaction.
  • Half-life (t₁/₂): The time required for the concentration of a reactant to decrease to half of its initial value. It is a constant for first-order reactions and varies with initial concentration for other orders.
  • Activation Energy (Eₐ): The minimum energy required for reactants to form an activated complex and proceed to products. It is a key factor in determining the rate of a reaction.
  • Collision Theory: A theory that explains reaction rates in terms of the frequency, energy, and orientation of collisions between reactant molecules.
  • Arrhenius Equation: k = A e^(-Eₐ/RT), where k is the rate constant, A is the pre-exponential factor (frequency factor), Eₐ is the activation energy, R is the gas constant, and T is the temperature in Kelvin.

The **rate-determining step** is the slowest step in the mechanism and determines the overall rate law. For example, if the mechanism is: (1) A → B (slow), (2) B + C → D (fast), the rate law is rate = k[A], as the first step is rate-determining. Intermediates are species formed in one step and consumed in a later step and do not appear in the overall rate law.'}

Important facts

  • The units of the rate constant (k) depend on the overall order of the reaction: zero-order (mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹), first-order (s⁻¹), second-order (L mol⁻¹ s⁻¹), and third-order (L² mol⁻² s⁻¹).
  • For a first-order reaction, the half-life is independent of the initial concentration of the reactant.
  • A reaction with a high activation energy will have a rate that is more sensitive to temperature changes than a reaction with a low activation energy.
  • The molecularity of an elementary reaction is the number of reactant molecules involved in that step (e.g., unimolecular, bimolecular). It is always a whole number and cannot be fractional.
  • The rate law for an elementary reaction can be written directly from its stoichiometry, but this is not true for overall reactions, which may involve multiple steps.
  • A catalyst lowers the activation energy for both the forward and reverse reactions equally, thus speeding up both reactions without affecting the equilibrium constant.
  • The rate of a reaction can be expressed in terms of the disappearance of reactants or the appearance of products, but the sign depends on whether the concentration is increasing or decreasing (e.g., rate = -d[R]/dt = +d[P]/dt).
  • For a reaction with rate = k[A]^m[B]^n, the overall order is m + n, and the reaction is m-th order with respect to A and n-th order with respect to B.

Memory aids

  • **Zero-Order**: [A] vs. time is linear (like a zero slope).
  • **First-Order**: ln[A] vs. time is linear (first letter of 'linear' is 'l' for ln).
  • **Second-Order**: 1/[A] vs. time is linear (second letter of 'linear' is 'i' for inverse).
  • **Half-life of First-Order**: t₁/₂ = 0.693/k (remember '0.693' as 'nice' or '69' for fun).
  • **Arrhenius Equation**: k = A e^(-Eₐ/RT) → 'A eats (-Eₐ/RT)' to remember the exponent.
  • **Rate Constant Units**: 'M^(1-n) s⁻¹' where n is the order (e.g., second-order: M⁻¹ s⁻¹).

Common traps

  • Assuming the order of a reaction can be determined from its stoichiometry. The order must be determined experimentally and may not match the coefficients in the balanced equation.
  • Confusing the rate of reaction with the rate constant. The rate depends on concentration, while the rate constant (k) is independent of concentration but depends on temperature.
  • Forgetting that the half-life of a first-order reaction is independent of initial concentration, while for other orders, it depends on the initial concentration.
  • Misinterpreting the units of the rate constant. For example, a second-order rate constant has units of L mol⁻¹ s⁻¹, not s⁻¹.
  • Assuming that all collisions between reactant molecules lead to a reaction. Only collisions with sufficient energy (greater than Eₐ) and proper orientation result in a reaction.
