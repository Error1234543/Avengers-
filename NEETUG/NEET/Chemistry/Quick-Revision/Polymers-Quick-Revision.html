NEET Chemistry Quick Revision: Polymers

NEETEnglishQuick Revision

Definitions

  • Polymers: Large molecules formed by the repeated linking of small molecules called monomers through covalent bonds.
  • Monomers: Simple molecules that combine with each other to form polymers.
  • Polymerization: The process of converting monomers into polymers, classified into addition and condensation polymerization.
  • Degree of Polymerization (DP): The number of monomer units present in a polymer chain.
  • Homopolymer: A polymer derived from a single type of monomer (e.g., polyethylene from ethylene).
  • Copolymer: A polymer derived from two or more different types of monomers (e.g., nylon-6,6 from hexamethylenediamine and adipic acid).
  • Thermoplastic Polymers: Polymers that soften on heating and harden on cooling, reversible process (e.g., PVC, polystyrene).
  • Thermosetting Polymers: Polymers that become hard and infusible on heating, irreversible process (e.g., bakelite, melamine).
  • Elastomers: Polymers with elastic properties, can be stretched and return to original shape (e.g., natural rubber, neoprene).
  • Biodegradable Polymers: Polymers that can be broken down by microorganisms into simpler substances (e.g., PHBV, PLA).

Important facts

  • Ziegler-Natta catalysts are used in the stereospecific polymerization of alkenes to produce polymers like high-density polyethylene (HDPE) and isotactic polypropylene.
  • Nylon-6 is prepared by the ring-opening polymerization of caprolactam, while nylon-6,6 is prepared by the condensation polymerization of hexamethylenediamine and adipic acid.
  • Bakelite is formed by the condensation polymerization of phenol and formaldehyde and is a thermosetting polymer.
  • Natural rubber is cis-polyisoprene, while gutta-percha is its trans isomer, which is hard and brittle.
  • Glyptal is a polyester formed by the condensation of ethylene glycol and phthalic acid, used in paints and lacquers.
  • Neoprene (polychloroprene) is a synthetic rubber resistant to oils and chemicals, used in hoses and gaskets.
  • The process of forming polymers from monomers is exothermic, and control of temperature and pressure is crucial to avoid side reactions.
  • Thermoplastic polymers can be recycled, while thermosetting polymers cannot due to their irreversible cross-linked structure.
  • The tensile strength of polymers increases with the degree of polymerization up to a certain limit, beyond which it plateaus.
  • Biodegradable polymers like PHBV are used in controlled drug delivery systems due to their biocompatibility and degradability.

Memory aids

  • To remember the difference between thermoplastic and thermosetting polymers: Thermoplastic can be remolded (like plastic bottles), while thermosetting is set permanently (like bakelite switches).
  • For nylon-6,6: The numbers indicate the carbon atoms in the diamine (6) and diacid (6) monomers. Think of it as '6 and 6 make nylon stick'.
  • To recall vulcanization: Sulfur is added to rubber to make it stronger, like how Vulcan (Roman god of fire) forges metals to make them stronger.
  • For biodegradable polymers: Think of 'PHBV' as 'Please Help Biodegrade Very soon'.
  • To remember the structure of natural rubber: It is cis-polyisoprene, so think 'CIStern of rubber' to recall the cis configuration.

Exam tips

  • Focus on the preparation, properties, and uses of key polymers like nylon, bakelite, melamine, and teflon. NEET often asks direct questions on these.
  • Understand the difference between addition and condensation polymerization with examples. Questions may ask you to classify a given polymerization process.
  • Memorize the structures of monomers for important polymers (e.g., isoprene for natural rubber, caprolactam for nylon-6). NEET may ask for monomer identification.
  • Be thorough with the concept of vulcanization and its effect on the properties of rubber. This is a high-yield topic.
  • Practice questions on biodegradable polymers, as they are frequently tested in the context of environmental chemistry.
  • For numerical questions, focus on calculating the degree of polymerization or molecular mass averages. These are less common but important to understand.
  • In assertion-reason type questions, pay attention to the properties of thermoplastics vs. thermosetting polymers. These are often tested together.

Common traps

  • Confusing addition polymerization with condensation polymerization: Addition polymerization involves unsaturated monomers (e.g., alkenes), while condensation polymerization involves monomers with functional groups (e.g., -NH2, -COOH) and loss of small molecules.
  • Assuming all polymers are synthetic: Natural polymers like cellulose, starch, and proteins are also important and frequently tested.
  • Misidentifying thermoplastic and thermosetting polymers: Thermoplastics soften on heating (e.g., PVC), while thermosetting polymers do not (e.g., bakelite).
  • Overlooking the role of catalysts in polymerization: Ziegler-Natta catalysts are crucial for stereospecific polymerization, a common NEET question topic.
  • Forgetting the difference between homopolymers and copolymers: Homopolymers have one type of monomer (e.g., polyethylene), while copolymers have two or more (e.g., nylon-6,6).
  • Assuming all rubbers are synthetic: Natural rubber is a polymer of isoprene and is a key topic in NEET.
  • Confusing molecular mass averages: Mn is the number average, while Mw is the weight average. Mw is always greater than or equal to Mn.
  • Ignoring biodegradable polymers: These are increasingly important in NEET due to environmental concerns and are often tested in application-based questions.
