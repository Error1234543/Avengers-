NEET Chemistry Quick Revision: Hydrocarbons

NEETEnglishQuick Revision

Definitions

  • Hydrocarbons: Organic compounds consisting exclusively of carbon and hydrogen atoms.
  • Saturated hydrocarbons (Alkanes): Hydrocarbons containing only single bonds between carbon atoms, with the general formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂.
  • Unsaturated hydrocarbons: Hydrocarbons containing at least one double (alkenes, CₙH₂ₙ) or triple bond (alkynes, CₙH₂ₙ₋₂) between carbon atoms.
  • Aromatic hydrocarbons: Hydrocarbons containing a benzene ring or similar ring structures with delocalized π-electrons, exhibiting resonance stability.
  • Isomerism: Phenomenon where compounds have the same molecular formula but different structural arrangements (structural isomerism) or spatial orientations (stereoisomerism).
  • Homologous series: A family of organic compounds with the same general formula, similar chemical properties, and a gradual change in physical properties with increasing molecular mass.

Important facts

  • Methane (CH₄) is the simplest alkane and the primary component of natural gas.
  • Ethene (C₂H₄) is the simplest alkene and is used industrially to produce polyethylene.
  • Ethyne (C₂H₂), also known as acetylene, is the simplest alkyne and is used in oxy-acetylene welding.
  • Benzene (C₆H₆) is the simplest aromatic hydrocarbon and is a key precursor in the synthesis of many industrial chemicals, including plastics and pharmaceuticals.
  • The combustion of hydrocarbons produces carbon dioxide and water, releasing significant energy, which is why they are used as fuels.
  • Cis-trans isomerism is not possible in alkynes due to the linear geometry around the triple bond.
  • Alkanes are relatively unreactive compared to alkenes and alkynes due to the strength of the C-C and C-H single bonds.
  • Aromatic hydrocarbons are less reactive than alkenes in addition reactions but readily undergo substitution reactions to preserve the aromatic ring's stability.
  • The presence of a double or triple bond in alkenes and alkynes makes them more reactive and susceptible to oxidation and addition reactions.
  • Petroleum and natural gas are the primary natural sources of hydrocarbons, which are refined to produce fuels and petrochemicals.

Memory aids

  • For IUPAC nomenclature: 'My Enormous Penguin Bounces Pretty High' (Meth-, Eth-, Prop-, But-, Pent-, Hex-) to remember the first six alkane prefixes.
  • For Markovnikov's rule: 'Hydrogen goes to the Hydrogen-rich carbon' to recall the addition of HX to alkenes.
  • For Huckel's rule: '4n + 2 π-electrons' can be remembered as '4, 2' (like a benzene ring's structure).
  • For alkane substitution reactions: 'Light or heat initiates halogenation' to recall the need for UV light or heat in free radical substitution.
  • For aromatic substitution: 'Benzene loves electrophiles' to remember that aromatic compounds undergo electrophilic substitution reactions.

Exam tips

  • Focus on reaction mechanisms: NEET often tests understanding of reaction mechanisms, such as free radical substitution in alkanes or electrophilic addition in alkenes.
  • Memorize key reagents and conditions: For example, remember that Br₂ in CCl₄ is used to test for unsaturation (decolorization indicates alkenes/alkynes).
  • Practice nomenclature: Be comfortable naming complex hydrocarbons, including those with multiple substituents or functional groups.
  • Understand isomerism thoroughly: NEET frequently asks about structural and stereoisomerism, including identifying cis-trans isomers or chiral centers.

Common traps

  • Confusing structural isomers with stereoisomers: Structural isomers differ in the connectivity of atoms, while stereoisomers differ in spatial arrangement (e.g., cis-trans isomers).
  • Misapplying Markovnikov's rule: It only applies to unsymmetrical alkenes and alkynes; symmetrical alkenes (e.g., ethene) do not follow this rule.
  • Assuming all cyclic compounds are aromatic: Aromaticity requires planarity, conjugation, and (4n + 2) π-electrons; cycloalkanes and cycloalkenes are not aromatic.
  • Forgetting that alkynes can exhibit both terminal and internal triple bonds, which affects their reactivity (e.g., terminal alkynes are acidic).
  • Overlooking the role of catalysts in reactions: For example, hydrogenation of alkenes/alkynes requires a metal catalyst (Ni, Pd, Pt), while halogenation of alkanes requires UV light.
  • Misidentifying the parent chain in nomenclature: The longest continuous carbon chain must be identified, even if it is not linear (e.g., branched chains).
  • Assuming all hydrocarbons are nonpolar: While alkanes are nonpolar, alkenes and alkynes have slight polarity due to the multiple bonds, affecting their solubility and boiling points.
