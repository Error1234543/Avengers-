Alcohols, Phenols and Ethers - NEET Chemistry Notes

NEETEnglishNotes

1. Introduction and Classification

Alcohols, phenols, and ethers are organic compounds containing oxygen. They are classified based on the attachment of the hydroxyl group (-OH) or alkoxy group (-OR) to the carbon atom. **Alcohols** contain one or more hydroxyl groups attached to a saturated carbon atom (e.g., ethanol, CH₃CH₂OH). They are further classified as primary (1°), secondary (2°), or tertiary (3°) based on the carbon to which the -OH group is attached. **Phenols** have a hydroxyl group directly bonded to an aromatic benzene ring (e.g., C₆H₅OH). **Ethers** contain an oxygen atom bonded to two alkyl or aryl groups (R-O-R'), such as diethyl ether (CH₃CH₂-O-CH₂CH₃). These compounds exhibit distinct physical and chemical properties due to differences in their functional groups and molecular structures.

2. Nomenclature and Isomerism

Nomenclature follows IUPAC rules: Alcohols are named by replacing the '-e' of the parent alkane with '-ol' (e.g., methanol, ethanol). For phenols, the suffix '-phenol' is used (e.g., 2-methylphenol). Ethers are named by identifying the two alkyl/aryl groups followed by 'ether' (e.g., methoxyethane for CH₃-O-CH₂CH₃). **Isomerism** in these compounds includes: - **Chain isomerism**: Different carbon chain arrangements (e.g., 1-propanol vs. 2-propanol). - **Position isomerism**: Different positions of the -OH group (e.g., 1-butanol vs. 2-butanol). - **Functional isomerism**: Alcohols and ethers are functional isomers (e.g., ethanol vs. dimethyl ether). - **Metamerism**: Ethers with the same molecular formula but different alkyl groups (e.g., ethoxyethane vs. methoxypropane).

3. Preparation of Alcohols

Key methods for alcohol synthesis include: - **Hydration of alkenes**: Alkenes react with water in the presence of an acid catalyst (e.g., H₂SO₄) to form alcohols. Markovnikov’s rule applies (e.g., propene → 2-propanol). - **Hydroboration-oxidation**: Alkenes react with borane (BH₃) followed by oxidation with H₂O₂/NaOH to yield anti-Markovnikov alcohols (e.g., propene → 1-propanol). - **Reduction of carbonyls**: Aldehydes/ketones are reduced to primary/secondary alcohols using NaBH₄ or LiAlH₄ (e.g., acetaldehyde → ethanol). - **Grignard reagents**: Reaction of RMgX with aldehydes/ketones/esters yields alcohols (e.g., formaldehyde + CH₃MgBr → methanol). - **Fermentation**: Glucose is fermented by yeast to produce ethanol (C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂).

4. Preparation of Phenols

Phenols are prepared via: - **Cumene process**: Industrial method where benzene reacts with propene to form cumene, which is oxidized to phenol and acetone. - **Dow process**: Chlorobenzene is hydrolyzed with NaOH at high temperature/pressure to yield phenol. - **From diazonium salts**: Benzene diazonium chloride is hydrolyzed with water to form phenol. - **From sodium phenoxide**: Fusion of sodium benzene sulfonate with NaOH followed by acidification yields phenol.

5. Preparation of Ethers

Ethers are synthesized through: - **Williamson ether synthesis**: Alkyl halides react with sodium alkoxides (R-ONa) to form ethers (e.g., CH₃Br + CH₃CH₂ONa → CH₃OCH₂CH₃). This method is limited to primary alkyl halides to avoid elimination. - **Dehydration of alcohols**: Intermolecular dehydration of alcohols (e.g., 2C₂H₅OH → C₂H₅OC₂H₅ + H₂O) using H₂SO₄ at 140°C. Higher temperatures favor alkene formation. - **Alkoxymercuration-demercuration**: Alkenes react with alcohols in the presence of Hg(OAc)₂ followed by NaBH₄ reduction to yield ethers (Markovnikov addition).

6. Physical Properties

- **Alcohols/Phenols**: Polar due to -OH group, forming hydrogen bonds. Higher boiling points than hydrocarbons of similar molecular weight. Soluble in water (solubility decreases with increasing carbon chain length). Phenols are more acidic than alcohols due to resonance stabilization of the phenoxide ion. - **Ethers**: Less polar than alcohols, with lower boiling points (no hydrogen bonding). Soluble in water due to oxygen’s lone pairs but less so than alcohols. Ethers are excellent solvents for organic reactions.

7. Chemical Properties of Alcohols

Key reactions include: - **Acidity**: Alcohols are weak acids (pKa ~16-18). They react with active metals (e.g., Na) to form alkoxides (2R-OH + 2Na → 2R-ONa + H₂). - **Esterification**: Reaction with carboxylic acids or acid chlorides to form esters (R-OH + R'COOH → R'COOR + H₂O). - **Oxidation**: Primary alcohols oxidize to aldehydes/acids (e.g., ethanol → acetaldehyde → acetic acid), secondary alcohols to ketones (e.g., 2-propanol → acetone), and tertiary alcohols resist oxidation. - **Dehydration**: Forms alkenes (e.g., ethanol → ethene) using H₂SO₄ at 170°C. Follows Saytzeff’s rule (more substituted alkene is major product). - **Lucas test**: Differentiates 1°/2°/3° alcohols using ZnCl₂/HCl. Tertiary alcohols react immediately (turbidity), secondary in 5-10 minutes, and primary do not react at room temperature.

8. Chemical Properties of Phenols

Phenols exhibit: - **Acidity**: More acidic than alcohols (pKa ~10) due to resonance stabilization of phenoxide ion. React with NaOH to form phenoxides (C₆H₅OH + NaOH → C₆H₅ONa + H₂O). - **Electrophilic aromatic substitution**: -OH group is activating and ortho/para-directing. Reactions include: - **Bromination**: Forms 2,4,6-tribromophenol (white precipitate) with Br₂ water. - **Nitration**: Forms ortho/para-nitrophenol with dilute HNO₃. - **Friedel-Crafts alkylation/acylation**: Requires mild conditions due to -OH group’s reactivity. - **Oxidation**: Forms quinones (e.g., phenol → p-benzoquinone) with strong oxidizing agents. - **Kolbe-Schmitt reaction**: Carboxylation with CO₂ to form salicylic acid (used in aspirin synthesis).

9. Chemical Properties of Ethers

Ethers are relatively inert but undergo: - **Cleavage reactions**: React with HI/HBr to form alcohols and alkyl halides (e.g., R-O-R' + HI → R-OH + R'I). Follows SN2 mechanism for primary alkyl groups. - **Peroxide formation**: Ethers form explosive peroxides on exposure to air/light (e.g., diethyl ether peroxide). - **Electrophilic substitution**: Aromatic ethers (e.g., anisole) undergo substitution at ortho/para positions due to -OCH₃ group’s activation.

10. Distinction Tests and Uses

- **Distinction tests**: - **Alcohols vs. phenols**: Ferric chloride test (phenols give violet coloration). - **Primary/secondary/tertiary alcohols**: Lucas test (as described above). - **Ethers**: Insoluble in water but soluble in organic solvents; do not react with Na metal. - **Uses**: - **Alcohols**: Ethanol in beverages, methanol as solvent/fuel, isopropyl alcohol as disinfectant. - **Phenols**: Antiseptics (e.g., Dettol), resins (Bakelite), and pharmaceuticals (aspirin). - **Ethers**: Anesthetics (diethyl ether), solvents (THF), and fuel additives.

Important facts

  • Alcohols and phenols are distinguished by the ferric chloride test (phenols give violet color).
  • Tertiary alcohols do not undergo oxidation under normal conditions.
  • Ethers form explosive peroxides on prolonged exposure to air.
  • Phenol is more acidic than alcohols due to resonance stabilization of the phenoxide ion.
  • Williamson ether synthesis is limited to primary alkyl halides to avoid elimination reactions.
  • Lucas test differentiates 1°, 2°, and 3° alcohols based on reaction rate with ZnCl₂/HCl.
  • Dehydration of alcohols follows Saytzeff’s rule (more substituted alkene is the major product).
  • Kolbe-Schmitt reaction is used to synthesize salicylic acid from phenol.
  • Ethers are cleaved by HI/HBr to form alcohols and alkyl halides.
  • Phenols undergo electrophilic substitution at ortho/para positions due to the -OH group’s activation.

Formulas and relationships

General formula of alcohols: R-OH (R = alkyl group)
General formula of phenols: Ar-OH (Ar = aryl group)
General formula of ethers: R-O-R' (R, R' = alkyl/aryl groups)
Markovnikov’s rule: H adds to the carbon with more H atoms in alkene hydration.
Saytzeff’s rule: More substituted alkene is the major product in alcohol dehydration.
Lucas reagent: ZnCl₂ + conc. HCl (used to distinguish alcohols)
Ferric chloride test: C₆H₅OH + FeCl₃ → violet complex (for phenols)
Williamson ether synthesis: R-X + R'-ONa → R-O-R' + NaX
Kolbe-Schmitt reaction: C₆H₅OH + CO₂ → C₆H₄(OH)COOH (salicylic acid)
Oxidation of primary alcohol: R-CH₂OH → R-CHO → R-COOH

Common mistakes

  • Confusing alcohols and phenols in nomenclature (e.g., calling phenol an alcohol).
  • Assuming all alcohols undergo oxidation similarly (tertiary alcohols do not oxidize).
  • Using secondary/tertiary alkyl halides in Williamson ether synthesis (leads to elimination).
  • Forgetting that ethers form peroxides (safety hazard in labs).
  • Misapplying Markovnikov’s rule in hydroboration-oxidation (anti-Markovnikov product).
  • Overlooking resonance stabilization in phenol’s acidity (key difference from alcohols).
  • Incorrectly predicting substitution positions in phenol (ortho/para-directing, not meta).
  • Assuming all alcohols are equally soluble in water (solubility decreases with carbon chain length).
  • Confusing esterification with etherification (esterification involves carboxylic acids).
  • Forgetting that phenols react with NaOH (unlike alcohols, which do not).
