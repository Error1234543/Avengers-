Haloalkanes and Haloarenes - NEET Chemistry Notes

NEETEnglishNotes

1. Introduction and Classification

Haloalkanes and haloarenes are organic compounds where one or more hydrogen atoms in an alkane or arene are replaced by halogen atoms (fluorine, chlorine, bromine, or iodine). They are classified based on the type of halogen, the number of halogen atoms, and the nature of the carbon atom to which the halogen is attached. **Primary (1°), secondary (2°), and tertiary (3°) haloalkanes** are determined by whether the carbon bearing the halogen is attached to one, two, or three other carbon atoms, respectively. **Haloarenes** contain halogen atoms directly bonded to an aromatic ring (e.g., chlorobenzene). These compounds are widely used as solvents, intermediates in organic synthesis, and in pharmaceuticals, but many are also environmental pollutants (e.g., DDT, CFCs).

2. Nomenclature (IUPAC and Common Names)

In IUPAC nomenclature, haloalkanes are named by prefixing the halogen name (fluoro-, chloro-, bromo-, iodo-) to the parent alkane, with the position of the halogen indicated by the lowest possible number. For example, CH3-CH2-Cl is chloroethane. Common names often use the alkyl group followed by the halide (e.g., ethyl chloride). For haloarenes, the halogen is treated as a substituent on the benzene ring (e.g., bromobenzene). If multiple halogens are present, prefixes like di-, tri-, or tetra- are used, and their positions are numbered (e.g., 1,2-dichlorobenzene). NEET often tests naming of complex structures, so practice identifying the longest carbon chain and correct numbering.

3. Methods of Preparation

Key methods for preparing haloalkanes and haloarenes include: - **From alcohols**: Reaction with hydrogen halides (HX), phosphorus halides (PCl3, PCl5), or thionyl chloride (SOCl2). For example, R-OH + HCl → R-Cl + H2O. The order of reactivity of HX is HI > HBr > HCl > HF, and the reaction follows SN1 or SN2 mechanisms depending on the alcohol type. - **From alkenes**: Addition of HX (Markovnikov’s rule) or X2 (anti-addition). For example, CH2=CH2 + HBr → CH3-CH2-Br. - **From alkanes**: Free radical substitution (e.g., CH4 + Cl2 → CH3Cl + HCl under UV light). This method is less selective and produces mixtures. - **From haloarenes**: Electrophilic aromatic substitution (e.g., chlorination of benzene using Cl2/FeCl3). - **Finkelstein reaction**: Exchange of halogens in alkyl halides (e.g., R-Cl + NaI → R-I + NaCl in acetone). - **Swarts reaction**: Conversion of alkyl chlorides/bromides to alkyl fluorides using AgF or Hg2F2.

4. Physical Properties

Haloalkanes and haloarenes exhibit distinct physical properties due to the polar C-X bond: - **Boiling points**: Higher than alkanes of similar molecular weight due to dipole-dipole interactions. Boiling points increase with halogen size (R-I > R-Br > R-Cl > R-F) and with the number of halogen atoms. - **Solubility**: Insoluble in water (due to inability to form hydrogen bonds) but soluble in organic solvents like benzene or ether. - **Density**: Higher than water for polyhalogenated compounds (e.g., CHCl3, CCl4). - **Bond strength**: C-X bond strength decreases as halogen size increases (C-F > C-Cl > C-Br > C-I), making iodoalkanes the most reactive. - **Dipole moment**: Increases with halogen electronegativity (C-Cl > C-Br > C-I). Haloarenes have lower dipole moments than haloalkanes due to resonance in the benzene ring.

5. Chemical Reactions and Mechanisms

Haloalkanes undergo **nucleophilic substitution (SN1/SN2)** and **elimination (E1/E2)** reactions: - **SN2 (Bimolecular Nucleophilic Substitution)**: One-step mechanism where the nucleophile attacks the carbon bearing the halogen from the backside, leading to inversion of configuration (Walden inversion). Favored by primary haloalkanes, strong nucleophiles, and polar aprotic solvents (e.g., DMSO, acetone). Example: CH3-Br + OH- → CH3-OH + Br-. - **SN1 (Unimolecular Nucleophilic Substitution)**: Two-step mechanism involving carbocation formation (rate-determining step), followed by nucleophilic attack. Favored by tertiary haloalkanes, weak nucleophiles, and polar protic solvents (e.g., water, alcohol). Example: (CH3)3C-Br + H2O → (CH3)3C-OH + HBr. - **Elimination Reactions**: Compete with substitution. **E2** is a one-step, anti-elimination favored by strong bases (e.g., OH-, RO-) and high temperatures. **E1** is a two-step process favored by tertiary haloalkanes and weak bases. Example: CH3-CH2-Br + OH- → CH2=CH2 + H2O + Br- (E2). - **Reaction with Metals**: Haloalkanes react with sodium (Wurtz reaction) to form alkanes (e.g., 2R-X + 2Na → R-R + 2NaX) or with magnesium to form Grignard reagents (R-Mg-X). Haloarenes are less reactive due to resonance stabilization but undergo: - **Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution**: Halogens are ortho/para-directing but deactivate the ring (e.g., nitration of chlorobenzene). - **Nucleophilic Aromatic Substitution**: Requires strong electron-withdrawing groups (e.g., -NO2) ortho/para to the halogen (e.g., chlorobenzene with NaOH at high temperature).

6. Polyhalogen Compounds and Environmental Impact

Polyhalogen compounds include: - **Chloroform (CHCl3)**: Used as a solvent and anesthetic; toxic and carcinogenic. - **Carbon tetrachloride (CCl4)**: Formerly used in fire extinguishers and dry cleaning; banned due to ozone depletion and liver toxicity. - **Freons (CFCs)**: Used as refrigerants; phased out under the Montreal Protocol due to ozone layer depletion. - **DDT (Dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane)**: Insecticide; banned due to environmental persistence and bioaccumulation. - **Iodoform (CHI3)**: Used as an antiseptic. **Environmental concerns**: Many haloalkanes and haloarenes are persistent organic pollutants (POPs), causing ozone depletion, global warming, and toxicity to aquatic life. NEET emphasizes the environmental impact of these compounds, particularly CFCs and DDT.

7. Distinction Between Haloalkanes and Haloarenes

Key differences: - **Structure**: Haloalkanes have halogen bonded to sp3 carbon; haloarenes have halogen bonded to sp2 carbon of an aromatic ring. - **Reactivity**: Haloalkanes undergo SN1/SN2 and elimination; haloarenes undergo electrophilic substitution and require harsh conditions for nucleophilic substitution. - **Bond length**: C-X bond in haloarenes is shorter due to partial double-bond character (resonance). - **Dipole moment**: Lower in haloarenes due to resonance. - **Tests**: Haloalkanes give a positive **Beilstein test** (green flame with copper wire) and react with **silver nitrate** (precipitate of AgX). Haloarenes do not react with silver nitrate under normal conditions.

8. Important Reactions for NEET

Focus on these high-yield reactions: - **Finkelstein reaction**: R-Cl + NaI → R-I + NaCl (in acetone). - **Swarts reaction**: R-Cl + AgF → R-F + AgCl. - **Wurtz reaction**: 2R-X + 2Na → R-R + 2NaX. - **Grignard reagent formation**: R-X + Mg → R-Mg-X (in dry ether). - **Dehydrohalogenation**: R-CH2-CH2-X + KOH (alc) → R-CH=CH2 + KX + H2O (E2). - **Nucleophilic substitution**: R-X + Nu- → R-Nu + X- (SN1/SN2). - **Electrophilic substitution in haloarenes**: Halogens are ortho/para-directing but deactivating.

9. Stereochemistry in Haloalkanes

Stereochemistry is critical in SN2 reactions, which proceed with **inversion of configuration** (Walden inversion). For example, if (S)-2-bromobutane reacts with OH-, the product is (R)-2-butanol. SN1 reactions produce **racemic mixtures** due to planar carbocation intermediates. NEET often tests stereochemical outcomes, so practice drawing mechanisms and predicting products for chiral haloalkanes.

Important facts

  • Halogens are ortho/para-directing but deactivate the benzene ring in electrophilic substitution.
  • SN2 reactions are favored by primary haloalkanes, strong nucleophiles, and polar aprotic solvents.
  • SN1 reactions are favored by tertiary haloalkanes, weak nucleophiles, and polar protic solvents.
  • E2 elimination requires anti-periplanar arrangement of H and X.
  • Grignard reagents (R-Mg-X) are highly reactive and must be prepared in dry ether.
  • CFCs are responsible for ozone layer depletion and are banned under the Montreal Protocol.
  • DDT is a persistent organic pollutant (POP) and bioaccumulates in the food chain.
  • The Beilstein test (green flame) detects halogens but does not distinguish between them.
  • Haloarenes do not undergo SN1/SN2 reactions under normal conditions due to resonance stabilization.
  • The order of reactivity of haloalkanes is R-I > R-Br > R-Cl > R-F due to bond strength.

Formulas and relationships

General formula of haloalkanes: CnH2n+1X (monohaloalkanes).
General formula of haloarenes: C6H5X (monohalobenzenes).
Rate law for SN2: Rate = k[R-X][Nu-].
Rate law for SN1: Rate = k[R-X].
Markovnikov’s rule: In addition of HX to alkenes, H attaches to the carbon with more hydrogens.
Wurtz reaction: 2R-X + 2Na → R-R + 2NaX.
Finkelstein reaction: R-Cl + NaI → R-I + NaCl (in acetone).
Swarts reaction: R-Cl + AgF → R-F + AgCl.
Grignard reagent formation: R-X + Mg → R-Mg-X (in dry ether).
Dehydrohalogenation: R-CH2-CH2-X + KOH (alc) → R-CH=CH2 + KX + H2O.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing SN1 and SN2 mechanisms: SN2 is one-step with inversion; SN1 is two-step with racemization.
  • Assuming all haloalkanes react with silver nitrate: Only alkyl halides (not aryl/vinyl) give a precipitate.
  • Forgetting that halogens deactivate the benzene ring in electrophilic substitution despite being ortho/para-directing.
  • Misapplying Markovnikov’s rule: It applies to addition of HX to alkenes, not to substitution reactions.
  • Ignoring stereochemistry in SN2 reactions: Always results in inversion of configuration.
  • Assuming Grignard reagents can be prepared in water: They react violently with water, so dry ether is required.
  • Overlooking the environmental impact of polyhalogen compounds (e.g., CFCs, DDT) in NEET questions.
  • Confusing the order of reactivity of haloalkanes: R-I is the most reactive, not R-F.
  • Forgetting that E2 elimination requires anti-periplanar arrangement of H and X.
  • Assuming haloarenes undergo SN1/SN2 reactions like haloalkanes: They require harsh conditions for nucleophilic substitution.
