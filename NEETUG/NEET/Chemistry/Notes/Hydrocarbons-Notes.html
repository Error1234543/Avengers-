Hydrocarbons - NEET Chemistry Notes

NEETEnglishNotes

1. Introduction to Hydrocarbons

Hydrocarbons are organic compounds composed exclusively of carbon and hydrogen atoms. They serve as the fundamental building blocks of organic chemistry and are classified based on the type of carbon-carbon bonds present: single (alkanes), double (alkenes), or triple (alkynes). Hydrocarbons are further categorized into aliphatic (straight-chain or branched) and aromatic (containing benzene rings) compounds. In NEET, hydrocarbons form the basis for understanding reaction mechanisms, isomerism, and nomenclature, making them a high-yield topic. Key applications include their role as fuels (e.g., methane, propane), solvents, and precursors in petrochemical industries.

2. Classification of Hydrocarbons

['**A. Aliphatic Hydrocarbons**:', '- **Alkanes (CₙH₂ₙ₊₂)**: Saturated hydrocarbons with single bonds (e.g., methane, ethane). Exhibit sp³ hybridization and tetrahedral geometry. Undergo substitution reactions (e.g., halogenation).', '- **Alkenes (CₙH₂ₙ)**: Unsaturated hydrocarbons with at least one double bond (e.g., ethene, propene). Exhibit sp² hybridization and trigonal planar geometry. Undergo addition reactions (e.g., hydrogenation, halogenation).', '- **Alkynes (CₙH₂ₙ₋₂)**: Unsaturated hydrocarbons with at least one triple bond (e.g., ethyne, propyne). Exhibit sp hybridization and linear geometry. Undergo addition reactions and are more reactive than alkenes due to the triple bond.', '**B. Aromatic Hydrocarbons**:', '- Contain benzene rings (C₆H₆) or similar structures with delocalized π-electrons. Exhibit resonance stability and undergo electrophilic substitution reactions (e.g., nitration, sulfonation). Examples: benzene, toluene, naphthalene.']

3. Nomenclature of Hydrocarbons (IUPAC Rules)

["- **Alkanes**: Suffix '-ane'. Longest carbon chain determines the parent name (e.g., methane, ethane). Branches are named as alkyl groups (e.g., methyl, ethyl) with locants (e.g., 2-methylpentane).", "- **Alkenes/Alkynes**: Suffix '-ene' or '-yne'. Double/triple bond position is indicated by the lowest-numbered carbon (e.g., but-2-ene, pent-1-yne).", "- **Aromatics**: Parent name is 'benzene'. Substituents are named as prefixes (e.g., methylbenzene, nitrobenzene). For disubstituted benzenes, use ortho- (1,2), meta- (1,3), or para- (1,4) prefixes.", "- **Common Errors**: Misnumbering the longest chain, incorrect suffixes (e.g., using '-ane' for alkenes), or omitting locants for multiple bonds/substituents."]

4. Isomerism in Hydrocarbons

['**A. Structural Isomerism**: Compounds with the same molecular formula but different connectivity.', '- **Chain Isomerism**: Different carbon chain arrangements (e.g., butane vs. isobutane).', '- **Position Isomerism**: Different positions of functional groups (e.g., but-1-ene vs. but-2-ene).', '- **Functional Isomerism**: Different functional groups (e.g., alkenes vs. cycloalkanes with the same formula).', '**B. Stereoisomerism**: Same connectivity but different spatial arrangements.', '- **Geometric Isomerism (Cis-Trans)**: Occurs in alkenes due to restricted rotation around the double bond (e.g., cis-but-2-ene vs. trans-but-2-ene). Requires two different groups on each double-bonded carbon.', '- **Optical Isomerism**: Enantiomers (mirror-image isomers) in compounds with chiral centers (e.g., 3-methylhexane).']

5. Preparation Methods of Hydrocarbons

['**A. Alkanes**:', '- **Hydrogenation of Alkenes/Alkynes**: Catalytic addition of H₂ (e.g., ethene + H₂ → ethane).', '- **Wurtz Reaction**: Coupling of alkyl halides with sodium metal (e.g., 2CH₃Br + 2Na → C₂H₆ + 2NaBr).', '- **Decarboxylation**: Heating sodium salts of carboxylic acids with soda lime (e.g., CH₃COONa + NaOH → CH₄ + Na₂CO₃).', '**B. Alkenes**:', '- **Dehydration of Alcohols**: Acid-catalyzed elimination (e.g., ethanol + H₂SO₄ → ethene + H₂O).', '- **Dehydrohalogenation**: Elimination of HX from alkyl halides (e.g., CH₃CH₂Br + KOH → ethene + KBr + H₂O).', '- **Cracking of Alkanes**: Thermal decomposition of higher alkanes (e.g., C₁₀H₂₂ → C₅H₁₂ + C₅H₁₀).', '**C. Alkynes**:', '- **Dehydrohalogenation of Vicinal Dihalides**: Elimination of 2HX (e.g., CH₂Br-CH₂Br + 2KOH → ethyne + 2KBr + 2H₂O).', '- **Reaction of Calcium Carbide with Water**: CaC₂ + 2H₂O → C₂H₂ + Ca(OH)₂.', '**D. Aromatics**:', '- **Friedel-Crafts Alkylation/Acylation**: Benzene + alkyl halide/acyl halide with AlCl₃ catalyst (e.g., C₆H₆ + CH₃Cl → C₆H₅CH₃ + HCl).']

6. Chemical Reactions of Hydrocarbons

['**A. Alkanes**:', '- **Substitution Reactions**: Halogenation (e.g., CH₄ + Cl₂ → CH₃Cl + HCl), nitration, sulfonation. Proceed via free-radical mechanisms.', '- **Combustion**: Complete combustion yields CO₂ and H₂O (e.g., CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O). Incomplete combustion produces CO or carbon soot.', '**B. Alkenes/Alkynes**:', '- **Addition Reactions**:', ' - **Hydrogenation**: Addition of H₂ (e.g., ethene + H₂ → ethane).', ' - **Halogenation**: Addition of X₂ (e.g., ethene + Br₂ → 1,2-dibromoethane).', ' - **Hydrohalogenation**: Addition of HX (Markovnikov’s rule: H adds to the carbon with more H atoms).', ' - **Hydration**: Acid-catalyzed addition of H₂O (e.g., ethene + H₂O → ethanol).', '- **Oxidation**:', ' - **Cold KMnO₄ (Baeyer’s Test)**: Alkenes form diols (e.g., ethene → ethane-1,2-diol).', ' - **Hot KMnO₄/Ozonolysis**: Cleavage of double bonds to form carbonyls.', '**C. Aromatics**:', '- **Electrophilic Substitution**:', ' - **Nitration**: C₆H₆ + HNO₃ (with H₂SO₄) → C₆H₅NO₂ + H₂O.', ' - **Sulfonation**: C₆H₆ + H₂SO₄ → C₆H₅SO₃H + H₂O.', ' - **Halogenation**: C₆H₆ + Br₂ (with FeBr₃) → C₆H₅Br + HBr.', '- **Addition Reactions**: Rare due to resonance stability (e.g., benzene + 3H₂ → cyclohexane under high pressure).']

7. Physical Properties of Hydrocarbons

['- **Boiling/Melting Points**: Increase with molecular weight. Branched alkanes have lower boiling points than straight-chain isomers due to reduced surface area.', '- **Solubility**: Hydrocarbons are nonpolar and insoluble in water but soluble in organic solvents (e.g., benzene, ether).', '- **Density**: Less dense than water (e.g., oil floats on water).', '- **Aromatics**: Benzene and derivatives have higher boiling points than aliphatic hydrocarbons of similar molecular weight due to π-π interactions.']

8. Aromaticity and Huckel’s Rule

['- **Aromaticity Criteria**:', ' 1. Cyclic and planar structure.', ' 2. Complete delocalization of π-electrons (resonance).', ' 3. Follows Huckel’s Rule: (4n + 2) π-electrons, where n is an integer (e.g., benzene has 6 π-electrons, n=1).', '- **Examples**: Benzene (6 π-electrons), naphthalene (10 π-electrons), cyclopentadienyl anion (6 π-electrons).', '- **Non-Aromatic/Anti-Aromatic**: Cyclobutadiene (4 π-electrons, anti-aromatic) or cyclooctatetraene (8 π-electrons, non-aromatic due to non-planarity).']

9. Important Named Reactions

['- **Wurtz Reaction**: 2R-X + 2Na → R-R + 2NaX (alkane synthesis).', '- **Friedel-Crafts Reactions**: Alkylation/acylation of benzene with AlCl₃ catalyst.', '- **Markovnikov’s Rule**: In addition reactions, the negative part of the reagent adds to the carbon with fewer hydrogen atoms.', '- **Anti-Markovnikov’s Rule (Kharasch Effect)**: Observed in HBr addition to alkenes in the presence of peroxides (e.g., propene + HBr → 1-bromopropane).', '- **Ozonolysis**: Cleavage of alkenes with O₃ to form carbonyls (e.g., ethene → 2HCHO).']

10. NEET-Specific Focus Areas

['- **Reaction Mechanisms**: Free-radical substitution (alkanes), electrophilic addition (alkenes/alkynes), electrophilic substitution (aromatics).', '- **Isomerism**: Structural and stereoisomerism (geometric/optical) are frequently tested.', '- **Nomenclature**: IUPAC naming of complex hydrocarbons (e.g., 3-ethyl-2-methylpentane).', '- **Aromaticity**: Huckel’s rule and resonance structures of benzene derivatives.', '- **Practical Applications**: Combustion, petroleum refining, and industrial synthesis of polymers (e.g., polyethylene from ethene).']

Important facts

  • Benzene is more stable than hypothetical 'cyclohexatriene' due to resonance energy (~36 kcal/mol).
  • Markovnikov’s rule is violated in the presence of peroxides (anti-Markovnikov addition).
  • Alkanes undergo substitution reactions, while alkenes/alkynes undergo addition reactions.
  • Geometric isomerism requires restricted rotation (e.g., double bonds) and two different groups on each carbon.
  • Huckel’s rule: Aromatic compounds must have (4n + 2) π-electrons in a cyclic, planar system.
  • Ozonolysis of alkenes cleaves the double bond to form aldehydes/ketones.
  • Alkynes are more acidic than alkenes/alkanes due to the sp hybridization of the triple bond.
  • Friedel-Crafts alkylation can lead to polyalkylation due to the activating effect of alkyl groups.

Formulas and relationships

General formula for alkanes: CₙH₂ₙ₊₂
General formula for alkenes: CₙH₂ₙ
General formula for alkynes: CₙH₂ₙ₋₂
General formula for cycloalkanes: CₙH₂ₙ
Huckel’s rule: (4n + 2) π-electrons for aromaticity
Combustion of hydrocarbons: CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ + (3n+1)/2 O₂ → nCO₂ + (n+1)H₂O
Markovnikov’s rule: HX + R-CH=CH₂ → R-CHX-CH₃ (major product)
Anti-Markovnikov’s rule (peroxide effect): HBr + R-CH=CH₂ → R-CH₂-CH₂Br (major product)

Common mistakes

  • Confusing structural isomers with stereoisomers (e.g., butane vs. isobutane vs. cis-but-2-ene).
  • Incorrectly applying Markovnikov’s rule to HBr addition in the presence of peroxides.
  • Misnumbering the longest carbon chain in IUPAC nomenclature (e.g., calling 2-methylbutane as 1-methylbutane).
  • Forgetting that benzene undergoes substitution (not addition) due to resonance stability.
  • Assuming all cyclic compounds with alternating double bonds are aromatic (e.g., cyclooctatetraene is non-aromatic).
  • Overlooking the requirement of two different groups on each double-bonded carbon for geometric isomerism.
  • Incorrectly writing the products of ozonolysis (e.g., forgetting to split the double bond into two carbonyls).
  • Mixing up the conditions for alkene preparation (e.g., using KOH for dehydrohalogenation vs. NaOH for Wurtz reaction).
