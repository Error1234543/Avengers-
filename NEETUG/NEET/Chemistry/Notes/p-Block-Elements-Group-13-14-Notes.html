p-Block Elements (Group 13 & 14) - NEET Chemistry Notes

NEETEnglishNotes

Introduction to p-Block Elements

The p-block elements are located in groups 13 to 18 of the periodic table and are characterized by the filling of their p-orbitals. Group 13 and 14 elements are particularly significant in NEET due to their diverse chemical properties and applications. Group 13 includes boron (B), aluminum (Al), gallium (Ga), indium (In), and thallium (Tl), while Group 14 includes carbon (C), silicon (Si), germanium (Ge), tin (Sn), and lead (Pb). These elements exhibit a transition from non-metallic to metallic character as we move down the group. For example, boron is a metalloid, while aluminum and the elements below it are metals. This group's elements form compounds with varying oxidation states, though the +3 oxidation state is most common for Group 13, and the +4 and +2 states are prevalent for Group 14.

General Properties of Group 13 Elements

Group 13 elements show a gradual increase in metallic character down the group. Boron is a hard, black, and non-metallic solid with a high melting point, while aluminum is a soft, silvery-white metal. The atomic and ionic radii increase down the group due to the addition of new electron shells. However, the increase is not as pronounced as in Group 1 and 2 elements due to the poor shielding effect of d-electrons in heavier elements like gallium and indium. The ionization enthalpies decrease down the group, but the trend is not smooth due to the influence of d- and f-electrons. Group 13 elements typically exhibit a +3 oxidation state, though thallium also shows a stable +1 state due to the inert pair effect.

Chemical Reactivity and Compounds of Group 13

Boron is chemically inert at room temperature but reacts with strong oxidizing agents like nitric acid. Aluminum, however, is highly reactive and forms a protective oxide layer (Al₂O₃) that prevents further oxidation. Group 13 elements form trihalides (e.g., BF₃, AlCl₃) that act as Lewis acids due to their electron-deficient nature. Boron forms covalent compounds like boranes (e.g., B₂H₆) and boric acid (H₃BO₃), which is a weak acid used as an antiseptic. Aluminum forms amphoteric oxides and hydroxides, reacting with both acids and bases. For example, Al₂O₃ reacts with HCl to form AlCl₃ and with NaOH to form sodium aluminate (NaAlO₂). The diagonal relationship between boron and silicon is notable, as both form covalent hydrides and oxides.

General Properties of Group 14 Elements

Group 14 elements exhibit a clear transition from non-metallic to metallic character. Carbon is a non-metal, silicon and germanium are metalloids, and tin and lead are metals. The atomic and ionic radii increase down the group, but the increase is less pronounced after silicon due to the poor shielding of d- and f-electrons. The ionization enthalpies decrease down the group, though the trend is irregular due to the influence of inner electrons. Group 14 elements primarily exhibit +4 and +2 oxidation states, with the +2 state becoming more stable down the group due to the inert pair effect. Carbon and silicon form covalent compounds, while tin and lead form ionic compounds.

Allotropes and Compounds of Carbon

Carbon exhibits allotropy, existing in several forms such as diamond, graphite, graphene, fullerenes, and carbon nanotubes. Diamond is a hard, three-dimensional network solid with sp³ hybridization, while graphite is a soft, layered structure with sp² hybridization and delocalized π-electrons, making it a good conductor of electricity. Fullerenes (e.g., C₆₀) are spherical molecules with applications in nanotechnology. Carbon forms oxides like CO (neutral) and CO₂ (acidic), as well as carbides (e.g., CaC₂, SiC) and carbonates (e.g., CaCO₃). Carbon also forms covalent hydrides like methane (CH₄) and silane (SiH₄), though silane is less stable and more reactive.

Silicon and Its Compounds

Silicon is the second most abundant element in the Earth's crust and is a key component of silicates and silica (SiO₂). Silica exists in crystalline forms like quartz and amorphous forms like glass. Silicon forms silicones, which are synthetic polymers used in lubricants and waterproofing agents. Silicates are the building blocks of many minerals and are classified based on their structure, such as orthosilicates (e.g., olivine), pyrosilicates, and chain silicates. Silicon carbide (SiC) is a hard, refractory material used as an abrasive. Silicones are formed by the hydrolysis of alkyl or aryl chlorosilanes, resulting in polymers with Si-O-Si linkages.

Tin and Lead: Properties and Compounds

Tin and lead are metals with low melting points and exhibit both +2 and +4 oxidation states. Tin is resistant to corrosion and is used as a coating for other metals (e.g., tin cans). It exists in two allotropic forms: white tin (metallic) and grey tin (non-metallic). Lead is dense, soft, and toxic, with applications in batteries, radiation shielding, and pigments. Tin forms compounds like SnCl₂ (reducing agent) and SnCl₄ (Lewis acid), while lead forms PbO (litharge) and PbO₂ (oxidizing agent). Lead storage batteries use Pb and PbO₂ as electrodes in sulfuric acid. The inert pair effect is more pronounced in lead, making Pb²⁺ more stable than Pb⁴⁺.

Important Reactions and Trends in Group 13 and 14

Key reactions include the formation of trihalides (e.g., AlCl₃, BF₃), which act as Lewis acids. Boron forms boric acid (H₃BO₃) when reacted with water, while aluminum reacts with acids and bases to form alums (e.g., KAl(SO₄)₂·12H₂O). Group 14 elements form hydrides (e.g., CH₄, SiH₄), oxides (e.g., CO₂, SiO₂), and halides (e.g., CCl₄, SiCl₄). The stability of +2 oxidation state increases down Group 14 due to the inert pair effect, making Pb²⁺ more stable than Pb⁴⁺. The diagonal relationship between boron and silicon is evident in their covalent hydrides and oxides. Carbon and silicon form strong covalent bonds, while tin and lead form more ionic compounds.

Applications and Biological Significance

Group 13 and 14 elements have wide-ranging applications. Aluminum is used in packaging, construction, and transportation due to its lightweight and corrosion resistance. Boron is used in borosilicate glass and as a neutron absorber in nuclear reactors. Silicon is essential in semiconductors, solar cells, and silicones. Carbon is the basis of organic chemistry and is used in fuels, plastics, and pharmaceuticals. Tin is used in solder and coatings, while lead is used in batteries and radiation shielding. Biologically, boron is essential for plant growth, and silicon is important for the structural integrity of plants and some marine organisms. Aluminum and lead, however, are toxic and pose environmental and health risks.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing the oxidation states of Group 13 and 14 elements, particularly the stability of +1 and +2 states due to the inert pair effect.
  • Assuming all Group 13 elements are metals; boron is a metalloid.
  • Overlooking the amphoteric nature of aluminum oxide and hydroxide, which react with both acids and bases.
  • Misidentifying the allotropes of carbon; diamond and graphite have vastly different properties due to their structures.
  • Ignoring the diagonal relationship between boron and silicon, which share similar properties in some compounds.
  • Assuming all Group 14 hydrides are stable; silane (SiH₄) is less stable and more reactive than methane (CH₄).
  • Forgetting the inert pair effect in lead, leading to incorrect assumptions about the stability of Pb⁴⁺ over Pb²⁺.
  • Confusing the structures of silicates, which vary widely from orthosilicates to chain and sheet silicates.
  • Overlooking the toxicity of lead and its compounds, which is a critical environmental and health concern.
  • Assuming all Group 13 trihalides are ionic; they are covalent and act as Lewis acids.
