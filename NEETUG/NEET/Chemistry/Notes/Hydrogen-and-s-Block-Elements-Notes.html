Hydrogen and s-Block Elements - NEET Chemistry Notes

NEETEnglishNotes

1. Position of Hydrogen in the Periodic Table

Hydrogen is the first element in the periodic table with atomic number 1. Its position is unique due to its electronic configuration (1s¹), which resembles both alkali metals (Group 1) and halogens (Group 17). Like alkali metals, hydrogen can lose its single electron to form H⁺, but unlike them, it does not exhibit metallic properties under standard conditions. Similarly, like halogens, hydrogen can gain one electron to form H⁻ (hydride ion), but it is far less electronegative than halogens. This dual nature makes hydrogen anomalous, and it is often placed separately in the periodic table. For NEET, focus on its resemblance to Group 1 (formation of H⁺) and Group 17 (formation of H⁻), as well as its isotopes (protium, deuterium, tritium) and their properties.

2. Isotopes of Hydrogen and Their Properties

Hydrogen exists in three isotopes: protium (¹H), deuterium (²H or D), and tritium (³H or T). Protium is the most abundant (99.98%) and contains no neutrons, only one proton and one electron. Deuterium has one neutron and is stable, while tritium has two neutrons and is radioactive with a half-life of ~12.3 years. These isotopes differ significantly in physical properties due to the mass difference (e.g., boiling points: H₂O = 100°C, D₂O = 101.4°C). Deuterium oxide (D₂O), or heavy water, is used as a moderator in nuclear reactors. For NEET, memorize the composition, stability, and key applications of these isotopes, as well as their role in nuclear fusion reactions (e.g., deuterium-tritium fusion).

3. Dihydrogen (H₂): Preparation, Properties, and Uses

Dihydrogen (H₂) is a colorless, odorless, and tasteless gas with a very low boiling point (-252.87°C). It can be prepared in the laboratory by the reaction of metals (e.g., Zn, Fe) with dilute acids (e.g., HCl, H₂SO₄) or by the electrolysis of water. Industrially, it is produced via steam reforming of methane (CH₄ + H₂O → CO + 3H₂) or coal gasification. Key properties include its high bond dissociation energy (436 kJ/mol), low solubility in water, and ability to act as a reducing agent (e.g., reducing metal oxides to metals). Uses include ammonia synthesis (Haber process), hydrogenation of oils, and as a fuel (e.g., in fuel cells). For NEET, focus on laboratory preparation methods, physical/chemical properties, and industrial applications.

4. Hydrides: Classification and Properties

Hydrides are binary compounds of hydrogen with other elements and are classified into three types: (1) Ionic (saline) hydrides: formed by alkali and alkaline earth metals (e.g., NaH, CaH₂). These are crystalline solids with high melting points and act as strong reducing agents. (2) Covalent (molecular) hydrides: formed by p-block elements (e.g., CH₄, NH₃, H₂O). These are volatile, low-melting compounds with varying properties (e.g., acidic, basic, or neutral). (3) Metallic (interstitial) hydrides: formed by d- and f-block metals (e.g., PdH, TiH₂). These are non-stoichiometric, conduct electricity, and are used in hydrogen storage. For NEET, memorize examples, properties, and trends (e.g., stability decreases down Group 1 for ionic hydrides).

5. Water: Structure, Properties, and Hardness

Water (H₂O) is a polar covalent molecule with a bent shape (H-O-H bond angle = 104.5°) due to lone pair-lone pair repulsion. Its polarity leads to hydrogen bonding, which explains its high boiling point, surface tension, and solvent properties. Water exists in three states: solid (ice, less dense than liquid water), liquid, and gas (steam). Hardness in water is caused by dissolved Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ ions and is classified as temporary (due to bicarbonates, removable by boiling) or permanent (due to sulfates/chlorides, removable by ion exchange). For NEET, focus on the structure of water, hydrogen bonding, and methods to remove hardness (e.g., Clark’s method for temporary hardness, ion-exchange resins for permanent hardness).

6. Hydrogen Peroxide (H₂O₂): Preparation, Properties, and Uses

Hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) is a pale blue liquid in pure form, miscible with water, and acts as a strong oxidizing and reducing agent. It is prepared industrially by the anthraquinone process or in the laboratory by the reaction of barium peroxide with dilute sulfuric acid (BaO₂ + H₂SO₄ → BaSO₄ + H₂O₂). Key properties include its decomposition into water and oxygen (2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂), which is catalyzed by light, heat, or MnO₂. It exhibits both oxidizing (e.g., oxidizing PbS to PbSO₄) and reducing properties (e.g., reducing KMnO₄ to Mn²⁺). Uses include bleaching (paper, textiles), disinfection, and as a rocket propellant. For NEET, memorize its preparation, decomposition reaction, and dual redox behavior.

7. s-Block Elements: General Characteristics

The s-block elements include Group 1 (alkali metals: Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs, Fr) and Group 2 (alkaline earth metals: Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba, Ra). These elements have their valence electrons in the s-orbital (ns¹ for Group 1, ns² for Group 2). Key properties include: (1) Low ionization enthalpy (decreases down the group), leading to high reactivity. (2) Strong reducing agents (e.g., Na reduces water to H₂). (3) Formation of basic oxides/hydroxides (e.g., Na₂O, Ca(OH)₂). (4) Group 1 elements form +1 ions, while Group 2 forms +2 ions. Exceptions include Be (amphoteric oxide) and Li (small size, high polarization, covalent character). For NEET, focus on trends in atomic/ionic radii, ionization enthalpy, and reactivity.

8. Anomalous Properties of Lithium and Beryllium

Lithium (Group 1) and beryllium (Group 2) exhibit anomalous properties due to their small size and high charge density. Lithium differs from other alkali metals in: (1) Forming covalent compounds (e.g., LiCl is soluble in organic solvents). (2) Reacting with nitrogen to form Li₃N. (3) Having a high melting point and hardness. (4) Forming a less basic hydroxide (LiOH). Beryllium differs from other alkaline earth metals in: (1) Forming amphoteric oxide (BeO). (2) Forming covalent compounds (e.g., BeCl₂ is a polymer). (3) Not reacting with water at room temperature. (4) Having a diagonal relationship with aluminum (e.g., both form amphoteric oxides). For NEET, memorize these exceptions and their reasons (e.g., small size, high polarization).

9. Compounds of s-Block Elements: Oxides, Hydroxides, and Carbonates

s-Block elements form oxides, hydroxides, and carbonates with distinct properties: (1) Oxides: Group 1 oxides (e.g., Na₂O) are basic, while Group 2 oxides (e.g., MgO) are less basic. BeO is amphoteric. (2) Hydroxides: Group 1 hydroxides (e.g., NaOH) are strong bases, while Group 2 hydroxides (e.g., Ca(OH)₂) are less soluble and basic. (3) Carbonates: Group 1 carbonates (e.g., Na₂CO₃) are stable and soluble, while Group 2 carbonates (e.g., CaCO₃) are insoluble and decompose on heating (CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂). For NEET, focus on trends in solubility, basicity, and thermal stability (e.g., carbonates/hydroxides of Group 2 decompose more easily than Group 1).

10. Biological Importance of s-Block Elements

s-Block elements play crucial roles in biological systems: (1) Sodium (Na⁺) and potassium (K⁺): Maintain osmotic balance, nerve impulse transmission, and muscle contraction. (2) Magnesium (Mg²⁺): Present in chlorophyll (photosynthesis) and acts as a cofactor for enzymes (e.g., ATPases). (3) Calcium (Ca²⁺): Essential for bone/teeth formation, blood clotting, and muscle function. (4) Lithium (Li⁺): Used in treating bipolar disorder (mechanism unclear). For NEET, memorize the biological roles of Na⁺, K⁺, Mg²⁺, and Ca²⁺, as well as their deficiency symptoms (e.g., hypokalemia, osteoporosis).

Important facts

  • Hydrogen is the only element that can form both H⁺ and H⁻ ions.
  • Deuterium oxide (D₂O) is used as a moderator in nuclear reactors.
  • Hydrogen peroxide acts as both an oxidizing and reducing agent.
  • Lithium exhibits a diagonal relationship with magnesium in the periodic table.
  • Beryllium oxide (BeO) is amphoteric, unlike other Group 2 oxides.
  • Temporary hardness in water is caused by bicarbonates of Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ and can be removed by boiling.
  • Group 1 carbonates (e.g., Na₂CO₃) are stable to heat, while Group 2 carbonates (e.g., CaCO₃) decompose on heating.
  • Sodium and potassium are essential for nerve impulse transmission and muscle contraction.
  • Magnesium is a central atom in chlorophyll and is crucial for photosynthesis.
  • Calcium is required for blood clotting and bone formation.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing the isotopes of hydrogen: Protium has no neutrons, deuterium has one, and tritium has two.
  • Assuming all hydrides are ionic: Only Group 1 and Group 2 hydrides are ionic; p-block hydrides are covalent.
  • Misidentifying the oxidizing/reducing behavior of H₂O₂: It can act as both depending on the reaction.
  • Overlooking the anomalous properties of lithium and beryllium due to their small size and high charge density.
  • Assuming all Group 2 oxides are basic: BeO is amphoteric.
  • Confusing temporary and permanent hardness: Temporary hardness is due to bicarbonates and can be removed by boiling; permanent hardness is due to sulfates/chlorides and requires ion exchange.
  • Assuming all s-block carbonates are stable to heat: Group 2 carbonates decompose on heating, while Group 1 carbonates do not.
  • Forgetting the diagonal relationship between lithium and magnesium or beryllium and aluminum.
  • Misidentifying the biological roles of s-block elements: Sodium/potassium for nerve impulses, magnesium for chlorophyll, calcium for bones.
  • Assuming all s-block hydroxides are equally soluble: Group 2 hydroxides are less soluble than Group 1 hydroxides.
