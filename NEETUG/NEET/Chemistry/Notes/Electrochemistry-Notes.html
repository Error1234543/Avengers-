Electrochemistry - NEET Chemistry Notes

NEETEnglishNotes

1. Introduction to Electrochemistry

Electrochemistry is the branch of chemistry that deals with the relationship between electrical energy and chemical changes. It primarily focuses on two key processes: (1) the generation of electrical energy from spontaneous chemical reactions (galvanic cells) and (2) the use of electrical energy to drive non-spontaneous chemical reactions (electrolytic cells). For NEET, understanding the fundamental concepts of redox reactions, electrode potentials, and cell representations is crucial. Electrochemistry is not only theoretical but also has practical applications in batteries, corrosion prevention, and industrial processes like electroplating and electrorefining.

2. Redox Reactions and Oxidation Numbers

Redox (reduction-oxidation) reactions involve the transfer of electrons between species. Oxidation is the loss of electrons, leading to an increase in oxidation number, while reduction is the gain of electrons, leading to a decrease in oxidation number. The species that loses electrons is the reducing agent, and the species that gains electrons is the oxidizing agent. For example, in the reaction Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu, zinc is oxidized (oxidation number increases from 0 to +2), and copper is reduced (oxidation number decreases from +2 to 0). Assigning oxidation numbers correctly is essential for balancing redox reactions using the ion-electron method or the oxidation number method.

3. Electrochemical Cells: Galvanic Cells

A galvanic cell (or voltaic cell) converts chemical energy into electrical energy through a spontaneous redox reaction. It consists of two half-cells: an anode (where oxidation occurs) and a cathode (where reduction occurs). The two half-cells are connected by a salt bridge, which maintains electrical neutrality by allowing the flow of ions. The cell potential (E_cell) is the difference in electrode potentials between the cathode and anode. For example, in a Daniel cell, zinc acts as the anode (Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻), and copper acts as the cathode (Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu). The standard cell potential (E°_cell) is calculated as E°_cell = E°_cathode - E°_anode. A positive E°_cell indicates a spontaneous reaction.

4. Standard Electrode Potential and Electrochemical Series

The standard electrode potential (E°) is the potential of a half-cell measured under standard conditions (1 M concentration, 1 atm pressure, 298 K) relative to the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE), which has an assigned potential of 0 V. The electrochemical series is a list of standard electrode potentials arranged in increasing order. Species with more positive E° values are stronger oxidizing agents, while those with more negative E° values are stronger reducing agents. For NEET, memorizing key electrode potentials (e.g., E° for Li⁺/Li, Na⁺/Na, Zn²⁺/Zn, Cu²⁺/Cu, Ag⁺/Ag) is essential for predicting the feasibility of redox reactions and calculating cell potentials.

5. Nernst Equation and Cell Potential

The Nernst equation relates the cell potential (E_cell) to the standard cell potential (E°_cell) and the concentrations of the species involved in the reaction. The equation is given by: E_cell = E°_cell - (RT/nF) * ln(Q), where R is the gas constant (8.314 J/mol·K), T is the temperature in Kelvin, n is the number of moles of electrons transferred, F is Faraday's constant (96,485 C/mol), and Q is the reaction quotient. At 298 K, the equation simplifies to: E_cell = E°_cell - (0.0592/n) * log(Q). The Nernst equation is crucial for calculating cell potentials under non-standard conditions, such as varying concentrations or pressures.

6. Electrolytic Cells and Electrolysis

An electrolytic cell uses electrical energy to drive a non-spontaneous redox reaction. It consists of two electrodes immersed in an electrolyte, with an external power source providing the necessary energy. Key applications of electrolysis include the extraction of metals (e.g., aluminum from alumina), electroplating, and the production of chemicals like chlorine and sodium hydroxide. Faraday's laws of electrolysis govern the quantitative aspects of electrolysis: (1) The amount of substance deposited or liberated at an electrode is directly proportional to the quantity of electricity passed. (2) The amounts of different substances deposited or liberated by the same quantity of electricity are proportional to their equivalent weights. For NEET, understanding the electrolysis of molten salts (e.g., NaCl) and aqueous solutions (e.g., NaCl(aq)) is important, including the competition between water and ions for oxidation/reduction at the electrodes.

7. Conductance of Electrolytic Solutions

The conductance of an electrolytic solution depends on the nature of the electrolyte, concentration, temperature, and the solvent. Conductance (G) is the reciprocal of resistance (R) and is measured in siemens (S). Molar conductance (Λ_m) is the conductance of all ions produced by 1 mole of the electrolyte in a solution and is given by Λ_m = (κ * 1000)/C, where κ is the conductivity (S/cm) and C is the concentration (mol/L). Equivalent conductance (Λ_eq) is used for electrolytes that dissociate into ions with different charges. Kohlrausch's law states that the molar conductance of an electrolyte at infinite dilution (Λ°_m) is the sum of the contributions of the individual ions: Λ°_m = ν₊λ°₊ + ν₋λ°₋, where ν₊ and ν₋ are the number of cations and anions per formula unit, and λ°₊ and λ°₋ are their respective ionic conductances.

8. Batteries and Fuel Cells

Batteries are portable sources of electrical energy that convert chemical energy into electrical energy through redox reactions. Primary batteries (e.g., dry cells, mercury cells) are non-rechargeable, while secondary batteries (e.g., lead-acid batteries, lithium-ion batteries) are rechargeable. For NEET, the working principles of common batteries are important: (1) Lead-acid battery: Pb(s) + PbO₂(s) + 2H₂SO₄(aq) → 2PbSO₄(s) + 2H₂O(l). (2) Dry cell: Zn(s) + 2MnO₂(s) + 2NH₄⁺(aq) → Zn²⁺(aq) + Mn₂O₃(s) + 2NH₃(aq) + H₂O(l). Fuel cells are electrochemical devices that convert the chemical energy of a fuel (e.g., hydrogen) and an oxidant (e.g., oxygen) into electrical energy. The hydrogen-oxygen fuel cell is a key example: 2H₂(g) + O₂(g) → 2H₂O(l), with a cell potential of ~1.23 V under standard conditions.

9. Corrosion and Its Prevention

Corrosion is the gradual destruction of metals due to chemical or electrochemical reactions with their environment. Rusting of iron is the most common example, where iron reacts with oxygen and water to form hydrated iron(III) oxide (Fe₂O₃·xH₂O). The electrochemical mechanism of rusting involves the formation of anodic and cathodic regions on the metal surface: (1) Anode: Fe(s) → Fe²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻. (2) Cathode: O₂(g) + 4H⁺(aq) + 4e⁻ → 2H₂O(l). Prevention methods include: (1) Barrier protection (e.g., painting, greasing). (2) Sacrificial protection (e.g., galvanizing iron with zinc). (3) Cathodic protection (e.g., using a more reactive metal like magnesium as a sacrificial anode). (4) Alloying (e.g., stainless steel contains chromium, which forms a protective oxide layer).

10. Applications of Electrochemistry in NEET

Electrochemistry has several high-yield applications for NEET: (1) **Batteries**: Understanding the chemistry of lead-acid batteries, dry cells, and lithium-ion batteries is crucial. (2) **Electroplating**: The process of coating a metal surface with another metal using electrolysis (e.g., silver plating). (3) **Electrorefining**: Purification of metals like copper using electrolysis. (4) **Corrosion**: Mechanisms and prevention methods, especially for iron. (5) **Quantitative electrolysis**: Calculations based on Faraday's laws, such as determining the amount of metal deposited or gas liberated during electrolysis. (6) **Cell potential calculations**: Using the Nernst equation to determine cell potentials under non-standard conditions. Focus on numerical problems involving these concepts, as they are frequently tested in NEET.

Important facts

  • The standard hydrogen electrode (SHE) has an assigned electrode potential of 0 V and is used as a reference for measuring other electrode potentials.
  • A positive standard cell potential (E°_cell) indicates a spontaneous redox reaction, while a negative value indicates a non-spontaneous reaction.
  • Faraday's first law states that the mass of a substance deposited or liberated during electrolysis is proportional to the quantity of electricity passed.
  • Faraday's second law states that the masses of different substances deposited or liberated by the same quantity of electricity are proportional to their equivalent weights.
  • Kohlrausch's law is used to calculate the molar conductance of an electrolyte at infinite dilution by summing the ionic conductances of its constituent ions.
  • The Nernst equation is essential for calculating cell potentials under non-standard conditions, such as varying concentrations or temperatures.
  • In a galvanic cell, oxidation occurs at the anode (negative electrode), and reduction occurs at the cathode (positive electrode).
  • In an electrolytic cell, the anode is positive, and the cathode is negative, as the external power source drives the non-spontaneous reaction.
  • Corrosion of iron (rusting) is an electrochemical process that requires the presence of oxygen and water.
  • Sacrificial protection (e.g., galvanizing iron with zinc) prevents corrosion by making the protected metal the cathode in the electrochemical cell.

Formulas and relationships

Standard cell potential: E°_cell = E°_cathode - E°_anode
Nernst equation (general form): E_cell = E°_cell - (RT/nF) * ln(Q)
Nernst equation (at 298 K): E_cell = E°_cell - (0.0592/n) * log(Q)
Faraday's first law: m = Z * I * t, where m is mass, Z is electrochemical equivalent, I is current, and t is time.
Faraday's second law: m₁/m₂ = E₁/E₂, where m₁ and m₂ are masses, and E₁ and E₂ are equivalent weights.
Molar conductance: Λ_m = (κ * 1000)/C, where κ is conductivity and C is concentration.
Kohlrausch's law: Λ°_m = ν₊λ°₊ + ν₋λ°₋, where ν₊ and ν₋ are the number of cations and anions, and λ°₊ and λ°₋ are their ionic conductances.
Gibbs free energy and cell potential: ΔG° = -nFE°_cell, where ΔG° is standard Gibbs free energy, n is moles of electrons, F is Faraday's constant, and E°_cell is standard cell potential.
Equilibrium constant and cell potential: E°_cell = (RT/nF) * ln(K), where K is the equilibrium constant.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing the anode and cathode in galvanic vs. electrolytic cells. In a galvanic cell, the anode is negative, and the cathode is positive. In an electrolytic cell, the anode is positive, and the cathode is negative.
  • Incorrectly calculating cell potential by subtracting E°_anode from E°_cathode instead of E°_cathode from E°_anode. Always use E°_cell = E°_cathode - E°_anode.
  • Misapplying the Nernst equation by using natural logarithm (ln) instead of base-10 logarithm (log) at 298 K. The simplified Nernst equation uses log(Q).
  • Forgetting to balance the redox reaction before applying the Nernst equation, leading to incorrect values of n (moles of electrons transferred).
  • Assuming that all metals can be extracted by electrolysis of their aqueous salts. For example, sodium and potassium are extracted by electrolysis of their molten salts, not aqueous solutions, due to the reactivity of water.
  • Ignoring the role of the salt bridge in maintaining electrical neutrality in a galvanic cell. The salt bridge allows the flow of ions to balance the charge buildup at the electrodes.
  • Confusing molar conductance (Λ_m) with equivalent conductance (Λ_eq). Molar conductance is used for electrolytes that dissociate into ions with the same charge, while equivalent conductance is used for ions with different charges.
  • Incorrectly identifying the products of electrolysis of aqueous solutions. For example, in the electrolysis of aqueous NaCl, chlorine gas is produced at the anode, not oxygen, due to the overpotential of water.
  • Assuming that corrosion only occurs in the presence of oxygen. While oxygen is a common factor, corrosion can also occur in the presence of other oxidizing agents or in acidic environments.
  • Forgetting to convert temperature to Kelvin when using the Nernst equation in its general form. The equation requires the temperature in Kelvin, not Celsius.
