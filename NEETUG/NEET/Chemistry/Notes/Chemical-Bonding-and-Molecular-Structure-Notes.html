Introduction to Chemical Bonding

Chemical bonding refers to the attractive forces that hold atoms together in molecules or crystals. The primary types of chemical bonds are ionic, covalent, and metallic bonds. The formation of these bonds is driven by the tendency of atoms to achieve a stable electronic configuration, typically that of the nearest noble gas. This concept is rooted in the octet rule, which states that atoms tend to gain, lose, or share electrons to acquire eight electrons in their valence shell. However, exceptions to the octet rule exist, such as in molecules with an odd number of electrons (e.g., NO) or those with expanded octets (e.g., PCl5). Understanding chemical bonding is crucial for predicting molecular geometry, polarity, and reactivity, all of which are frequently tested in NEET.

Ionic Bonding

An ionic bond is formed by the complete transfer of one or more electrons from one atom to another, typically between a metal and a non-metal. This transfer results in the formation of positively charged cations and negatively charged anions, which are held together by strong electrostatic forces. Key characteristics of ionic compounds include high melting and boiling points, solubility in polar solvents like water, and the ability to conduct electricity in molten or aqueous states. The lattice energy of an ionic compound, which is the energy released when gaseous ions combine to form a solid lattice, is a measure of the bond's strength. For NEET, focus on predicting the formation of ionic bonds using electronegativity differences (generally > 1.7) and writing the correct formulas for ionic compounds (e.g., NaCl, MgO).

Covalent Bonding and Lewis Structures

A covalent bond is formed by the sharing of electron pairs between atoms, typically non-metals. The shared electrons are localized between the bonded atoms, and the bond strength depends on the number of shared pairs (single, double, or triple bonds). Lewis structures are diagrams that represent the valence electrons of atoms in a molecule and the bonds formed between them. To draw a Lewis structure: (1) Count the total number of valence electrons in the molecule. (2) Arrange the atoms to show connectivity, often with the least electronegative atom in the center. (3) Distribute electrons to satisfy the octet rule for each atom, starting with bonding electrons and then lone pairs. (4) If the octet rule is not satisfied, form multiple bonds. Exceptions include molecules with incomplete octets (e.g., BF3) or expanded octets (e.g., SF6). NEET often tests the ability to draw Lewis structures for molecules like CO2, NH3, and O2.

VSEPR Theory and Molecular Geometry

The Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion (VSEPR) theory predicts the shape of molecules based on the repulsion between electron pairs (bonding and lone pairs) around the central atom. The theory classifies molecular geometries into categories such as linear, trigonal planar, tetrahedral, trigonal bipyramidal, and octahedral, depending on the number of electron pairs. For example, a molecule with two bonding pairs and no lone pairs (e.g., BeCl2) adopts a linear geometry, while a molecule with three bonding pairs and one lone pair (e.g., NH3) adopts a trigonal pyramidal geometry. The presence of lone pairs often compresses bond angles due to greater repulsion. NEET questions frequently require predicting molecular shapes and bond angles, so memorize the geometries for common electron pair arrangements (e.g., AX2, AX3, AX4, AX5, AX6).

Hybridization and Molecular Orbitals

Hybridization explains the observed geometries of molecules by mixing atomic orbitals to form new hybrid orbitals. Common types of hybridization include sp (linear), sp2 (trigonal planar), and sp3 (tetrahedral). For example, in methane (CH4), carbon undergoes sp3 hybridization to form four equivalent bonds. Molecular Orbital (MO) theory, on the other hand, describes bonding in terms of the combination of atomic orbitals to form molecular orbitals that are delocalized over the entire molecule. MO theory explains phenomena like bond order, magnetism, and the stability of diatomic molecules (e.g., O2, which is paramagnetic due to unpaired electrons in its molecular orbitals). For NEET, focus on identifying hybridization states (e.g., sp3d for PCl5) and calculating bond orders using MO theory (e.g., bond order of O2 is 2).

Bond Parameters and Polarity

Bond parameters include bond length, bond angle, and bond energy. Bond length is the equilibrium distance between the nuclei of two bonded atoms and is inversely related to bond order (e.g., triple bonds are shorter than single bonds). Bond angle is the angle between two adjacent bonds and is determined by molecular geometry. Bond energy is the energy required to break one mole of bonds in a gaseous molecule and is a measure of bond strength. Polarity arises when there is an uneven distribution of electron density in a molecule, resulting in a dipole moment. A molecule is polar if it has polar bonds (due to electronegativity differences) and an asymmetric geometry (e.g., H2O). Non-polar molecules have either non-polar bonds or symmetric geometries that cancel out dipole moments (e.g., CO2). NEET often tests the ability to predict polarity and dipole moments, so practice identifying polar vs. non-polar molecules.

Resonance and Formal Charge

Resonance occurs when a molecule cannot be accurately represented by a single Lewis structure and instead exists as a hybrid of multiple structures. Resonance structures differ only in the arrangement of electrons, not atoms. For example, the carbonate ion (CO3^2-) has three equivalent resonance structures. The actual structure is a hybrid of these, with delocalized electrons. Formal charge is a tool to determine the most stable resonance structure by calculating the charge assigned to an atom in a molecule, assuming equal sharing of bonding electrons. The formula for formal charge is: Formal Charge = (Valence Electrons) - (Non-bonding Electrons) - 0.5(Bonding Electrons). The most stable resonance structure is the one with the lowest formal charges and negative charges on the most electronegative atoms. NEET questions often require drawing resonance structures and calculating formal charges for molecules like O3 and SO2.

Hydrogen Bonding and van der Waals Forces

Hydrogen bonding is a special type of dipole-dipole interaction that occurs when hydrogen is bonded to highly electronegative atoms like nitrogen, oxygen, or fluorine. These bonds are stronger than typical dipole-dipole interactions and significantly influence the physical properties of substances (e.g., high boiling point of water). Van der Waals forces are weak intermolecular forces that include London dispersion forces (present in all molecules) and dipole-dipole interactions (present in polar molecules). London dispersion forces arise from temporary fluctuations in electron density and are stronger in larger molecules. For NEET, understand how hydrogen bonding affects properties like solubility, boiling points, and viscosity, and be able to compare the strengths of different intermolecular forces.
