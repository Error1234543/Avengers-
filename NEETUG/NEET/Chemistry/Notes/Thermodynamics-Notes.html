Thermodynamics - NEET Chemistry Notes

NEETEnglishNotes

1. Introduction to Thermodynamics

Thermodynamics is the branch of physical chemistry that studies the relationships between heat, work, temperature, and energy. It deals with the transfer of energy from one place to another and from one form to another. The key concept in thermodynamics is the system, which is the part of the universe under study, and the surroundings, which are everything else. Systems can be classified as open (exchange of matter and energy), closed (exchange of energy only), or isolated (no exchange of matter or energy). For NEET, focus on closed systems, as they are most commonly tested. Thermodynamic processes can be reversible (ideal, infinitely slow) or irreversible (real, spontaneous). Understanding these distinctions is crucial for solving problems related to work, heat, and efficiency.

2. Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics

The Zeroth Law of Thermodynamics states that if two systems are each in thermal equilibrium with a third system, they are in thermal equilibrium with each other. This law forms the basis for the concept of temperature and the use of thermometers. It implies that temperature is a property that determines whether two systems are in thermal equilibrium. For example, if system A is in thermal equilibrium with system B, and system B is in thermal equilibrium with system C, then system A is also in thermal equilibrium with system C. This law is foundational for understanding heat transfer and the measurement of temperature in thermodynamic processes.

3. First Law of Thermodynamics

The First Law of Thermodynamics is a statement of the conservation of energy. It states that energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred or converted from one form to another. Mathematically, it is expressed as: ΔU = q + w, where ΔU is the change in internal energy of the system, q is the heat added to the system, and w is the work done on the system. For NEET, it is critical to remember the sign conventions: q is positive if heat is added to the system and negative if heat is removed; w is positive if work is done on the system and negative if work is done by the system. This law is applied to various processes such as isothermal (constant temperature), adiabatic (no heat transfer), isobaric (constant pressure), and isochoric (constant volume) processes.

4. Work Done in Thermodynamic Processes

Work done in thermodynamic processes depends on the type of process. For an isobaric process (constant pressure), work done by the system is given by w = -PΔV, where P is the pressure and ΔV is the change in volume. For an isochoric process (constant volume), no work is done (w = 0) because ΔV = 0. In an isothermal process (constant temperature), work done by an ideal gas is calculated using the formula w = -nRT ln(V₂/V₁), where n is the number of moles, R is the gas constant, T is the temperature, and V₂ and V₁ are the final and initial volumes, respectively. For an adiabatic process (no heat transfer), work done is related to the change in internal energy: w = ΔU. Understanding these formulas and their applications is essential for solving numerical problems in NEET.

5. Enthalpy (H) and Heat Capacity

Enthalpy (H) is a thermodynamic property defined as H = U + PV, where U is the internal energy, P is the pressure, and V is the volume. For processes at constant pressure, the change in enthalpy (ΔH) is equal to the heat added to the system (qₚ). Enthalpy is a state function, meaning it depends only on the initial and final states of the system, not on the path taken. Heat capacity is the amount of heat required to raise the temperature of a system by 1°C or 1 K. It can be expressed as molar heat capacity (C) or specific heat capacity (c). For an ideal gas, the molar heat capacities at constant pressure (Cₚ) and constant volume (Cᵥ) are related by the equation Cₚ - Cᵥ = R, where R is the gas constant. This relationship is crucial for solving problems involving heat transfer and thermodynamic cycles.

6. Second Law of Thermodynamics and Entropy

The Second Law of Thermodynamics introduces the concept of entropy (S), a measure of the disorder or randomness of a system. It states that for any spontaneous process, the total entropy of the universe (system + surroundings) always increases. Mathematically, ΔS_universe = ΔS_system + ΔS_surroundings > 0 for a spontaneous process. Entropy is a state function, and its change for a reversible process is given by ΔS = q_rev / T, where q_rev is the reversible heat transfer and T is the temperature. For an irreversible process, ΔS > q_irrev / T. The Second Law also states that no heat engine can be 100% efficient, as some heat is always lost to the surroundings. This law is fundamental for understanding the direction of thermodynamic processes and the concept of spontaneity.

7. Gibbs Free Energy (G) and Spontaneity

Gibbs Free Energy (G) is a thermodynamic potential that combines enthalpy (H) and entropy (S) to predict the spontaneity of a process at constant temperature and pressure. It is defined as G = H - TS, where T is the temperature in Kelvin. The change in Gibbs free energy (ΔG) for a process is given by ΔG = ΔH - TΔS. For a process to be spontaneous, ΔG must be negative (ΔG < 0). If ΔG = 0, the process is at equilibrium, and if ΔG > 0, the process is non-spontaneous. The relationship between ΔG and the equilibrium constant (K) is given by ΔG° = -RT ln K, where ΔG° is the standard Gibbs free energy change. This equation is vital for solving problems related to chemical equilibrium and reaction spontaneity in NEET.

8. Thermodynamic Potentials and Maxwell Relations

Thermodynamic potentials are state functions used to describe the state of a system. The four main thermodynamic potentials are: Internal Energy (U), Enthalpy (H), Helmholtz Free Energy (A = U - TS), and Gibbs Free Energy (G = H - TS). These potentials are related through Maxwell relations, which are derived from the fundamental thermodynamic equations and the properties of exact differentials. For example, one of the Maxwell relations is (∂T/∂V)ₛ = -(∂P/∂S)ᵥ. While Maxwell relations are not frequently tested in NEET, understanding their conceptual basis helps in solving problems involving partial derivatives and thermodynamic properties. Focus on the applications of Gibbs free energy and enthalpy for NEET-level questions.

9. Applications of Thermodynamics in Chemistry

Thermodynamics has wide-ranging applications in chemistry, particularly in understanding chemical reactions, phase transitions, and equilibrium. For example, the spontaneity of a chemical reaction can be predicted using Gibbs free energy (ΔG). If ΔG is negative, the reaction is spontaneous. The enthalpy change (ΔH) of a reaction can be determined using Hess's Law, which states that the total enthalpy change for a reaction is the sum of the enthalpy changes for the individual steps. Thermodynamics also explains phase transitions, such as melting, vaporization, and sublimation, using concepts like entropy and Gibbs free energy. Additionally, the efficiency of heat engines and refrigerators is analyzed using thermodynamic cycles, such as the Carnot cycle, which provides the maximum possible efficiency for a heat engine operating between two temperatures.

Important facts

  • The First Law of Thermodynamics is a statement of the conservation of energy: ΔU = q + w.
  • For an isothermal process, the work done by an ideal gas is w = -nRT ln(V₂/V₁).
  • Enthalpy (H) is defined as H = U + PV, and ΔH = qₚ for a constant-pressure process.
  • The Second Law of Thermodynamics introduces entropy (S), a measure of disorder, and states that ΔS_universe > 0 for a spontaneous process.
  • Gibbs Free Energy (G) is used to predict spontaneity: ΔG = ΔH - TΔS. A process is spontaneous if ΔG < 0.
  • For a reversible adiabatic process, PV^γ = constant, where γ = Cₚ/Cᵥ.
  • The efficiency of a Carnot engine is given by η = 1 - (T_cold / T_hot), where T_cold and T_hot are the temperatures of the cold and hot reservoirs, respectively.
  • Hess's Law states that the enthalpy change for a reaction is independent of the pathway taken.
  • The relationship between Gibbs free energy and the equilibrium constant is ΔG° = -RT ln K.
  • For an ideal gas, Cₚ - Cᵥ = R, where Cₚ and Cᵥ are the molar heat capacities at constant pressure and volume, respectively.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing the sign conventions for heat (q) and work (w) in the First Law of Thermodynamics. Remember: q is positive if heat is added to the system, and w is positive if work is done on the system.
  • Assuming that work done in an isochoric process is non-zero. In reality, w = 0 because ΔV = 0.
  • Misapplying the formula for work done in an isothermal process. Ensure that the process is truly isothermal and that the gas is ideal before using w = -nRT ln(V₂/V₁).
  • Forgetting that entropy (S) is a state function and depends only on the initial and final states, not the path taken.
  • Incorrectly calculating ΔG for a process without considering the temperature (T) in Kelvin. Always use Kelvin for thermodynamic calculations.
  • Assuming that a negative ΔH always means a process is spontaneous. Spontaneity is determined by ΔG, not ΔH alone.
  • Mixing up the formulas for Cₚ and Cᵥ. Remember that Cₚ is always greater than Cᵥ for an ideal gas.
  • Ignoring the difference between reversible and irreversible processes when calculating entropy changes. ΔS = q_rev / T is only valid for reversible processes.
  • Forgetting to convert units consistently, especially when using the gas constant (R). Ensure that all units (e.g., pressure in Pa or atm, volume in m³ or L) are compatible with the value of R used.
  • Assuming that the efficiency of a heat engine can be 100%. The Second Law of Thermodynamics states that no heat engine can be 100% efficient.
