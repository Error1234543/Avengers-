Introduction to d and f Block Elements

The d and f block elements, also known as transition and inner transition elements, respectively, occupy the central and bottom portions of the periodic table. The d-block elements (Groups 3 to 12) are characterized by the filling of the (n-1)d orbitals, while the f-block elements (lanthanoids and actinoids) involve the filling of the (n-2)f orbitals. These elements exhibit unique properties such as variable oxidation states, formation of colored compounds, magnetic behavior, and catalytic activity, which are crucial for their applications in industries and biological systems. For NEET, focus on understanding their electronic configurations, trends in properties, and practical significance.

Electronic Configuration of d-Block Elements

The general electronic configuration of d-block elements is (n-1)d¹⁻¹⁰ ns¹⁻². However, exceptions exist due to the stability of half-filled and fully-filled d-orbitals. For example: - Chromium (Cr): [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹ (not 3d⁴ 4s²) due to half-filled stability. - Copper (Cu): [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹ (not 3d⁹ 4s²) due to fully-filled d-orbital stability. Key points for NEET: - The (n-1)d orbitals are filled after the ns orbital but before the np orbital. - Transition metals lose electrons from the ns orbital first during ionization. - The number of unpaired electrons determines magnetic properties (paramagnetism or diamagnetism).

General Properties of d-Block Elements

- **Variable Oxidation States**: Due to the small energy gap between (n-1)d and ns orbitals, transition metals exhibit multiple oxidation states. For example, manganese (Mn) shows oxidation states from +2 to +7. - **Formation of Colored Compounds**: The presence of unpaired electrons in d-orbitals allows d-d transitions, absorbing visible light and imparting color. For example, Cu²⁺ compounds are blue. - **Magnetic Properties**: Most transition metals are paramagnetic due to unpaired electrons. Diamagnetism occurs in species with all paired electrons (e.g., Zn²⁺). - **Catalytic Activity**: Transition metals and their compounds act as catalysts due to their ability to adopt multiple oxidation states and form intermediate complexes (e.g., V₂O₅ in the Contact Process). - **Complex Formation**: Transition metals form coordination complexes due to the availability of empty d-orbitals for ligand bonding (e.g., [Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻).

Trends in Properties Across the d-Block

- **Atomic and Ionic Radii**: Decrease initially across a period due to increasing nuclear charge but then increase slightly due to electron-electron repulsion in the d-orbitals. For example, the radii of 3d series elements decrease from Sc to Cr, then increase toward Zn. - **Ionization Enthalpy**: Generally increases across a period but with irregularities due to the stability of half-filled and fully-filled configurations. For example, Zn has a higher ionization enthalpy than Cu. - **Melting and Boiling Points**: High due to strong metallic bonding involving d-electrons. Exceptions include Zn, Cd, and Hg, which have low melting points due to weak metallic bonding. - **Standard Electrode Potentials**: The E° values for M²⁺/M couples vary widely. For example, Cu has a positive E° (less reactive), while Mn has a negative E° (more reactive).

Important Compounds of d-Block Elements

- **Potassium Dichromate (K₂Cr₂O₇)**: A strong oxidizing agent used in volumetric analysis. Its orange color is due to Cr⁶⁺. In acidic medium, it reduces to Cr³⁺ (green). - **Potassium Permanganate (KMnO₄)**: A powerful oxidizing agent. In acidic medium, it reduces to Mn²⁺ (colorless), while in neutral/alkaline medium, it forms MnO₂ (brown precipitate). - **Copper Sulfate (CuSO₄·5H₂O)**: Blue vitriol, used as a fungicide and in electroplating. Loses water molecules on heating to form anhydrous CuSO₄ (white). - **Silver Nitrate (AgNO₃)**: Used in qualitative analysis (e.g., halide detection) and photography. Forms a white precipitate with Cl⁻ (AgCl), soluble in NH₃. - **Iron Compounds**: FeSO₄·7H₂O (green vitriol) is used in medicine and as a reducing agent. FeCl₃ is used as a coagulant in water treatment.

f-Block Elements: Lanthanoids and Actinoids

- **Lanthanoids (4f series)**: Elements from Ce (Z=58) to Lu (Z=71). Exhibit a +3 oxidation state predominantly, with some showing +2 or +4. The 4f orbitals are deeply buried, leading to poor shielding and the **lanthanoid contraction** (gradual decrease in atomic/ionic radii). This affects the properties of post-lanthanoid elements (e.g., Zr and Hf have similar radii). - **Actinoids (5f series)**: Elements from Th (Z=90) to Lr (Z=103). Exhibit multiple oxidation states (e.g., U shows +3 to +6). All actinoids are radioactive, with Th, U, and Pu being the most important for nuclear energy. - **Properties**: Highly electropositive, form colored compounds, and exhibit paramagnetism. Actinoids are more reactive than lanthanoids due to larger atomic sizes and lower ionization enthalpies.

Applications of d and f Block Elements

- **Industrial Applications**: - **Iron (Fe)**: Steel production, construction, and machinery. - **Copper (Cu)**: Electrical wiring, plumbing, and alloys (brass, bronze). - **Nickel (Ni)**: Stainless steel, batteries, and catalysts. - **Titanium (Ti)**: Aerospace, medical implants, and pigments. - **Chromium (Cr)**: Stainless steel, plating, and pigments. - **Biological Significance**: - **Iron (Fe)**: Hemoglobin (oxygen transport) and cytochromes (electron transport). - **Cobalt (Co)**: Vitamin B₁₂ (pernicious anemia prevention). - **Zinc (Zn)**: Enzymes (e.g., carbonic anhydrase) and immune function. - **Manganese (Mn)**: Photosynthesis (oxygen evolution) and enzymes. - **Nuclear Applications**: - **Uranium (U) and Plutonium (Pu)**: Nuclear reactors and weapons. - **Thorium (Th)**: Potential nuclear fuel.

Coordination Compounds and d-Block Elements

Transition metals form coordination compounds due to their ability to act as Lewis acids (accepting electron pairs from ligands). Key concepts for NEET: - **Ligands**: Molecules/ions with lone pairs (e.g., NH₃, H₂O, Cl⁻, CN⁻). Ligands can be monodentate (single donor atom) or polydentate (multiple donor atoms, e.g., EDTA). - **Coordination Number**: Number of ligand donor atoms bonded to the central metal ion (e.g., [Co(NH₃)₆]³⁺ has a coordination number of 6). - **Nomenclature**: Follow IUPAC rules (e.g., [Cu(NH₃)₄]SO₄ is tetraamminecopper(II) sulfate). - **Isomerism**: Structural (ionization, hydrate, linkage, coordination) and stereoisomerism (geometric, optical). For example, [Co(NH₃)₄Cl₂]⁺ exhibits cis-trans isomerism. - **Crystal Field Theory (CFT)**: Explains color and magnetic properties. Ligands split d-orbitals into eg and t2g sets, with the energy gap (Δ₀) determining the color absorbed.

NEET-Focused Problem-Solving Tips

- **Oxidation States**: Memorize common oxidation states (e.g., Cr: +3, +6; Mn: +2, +4, +7; Fe: +2, +3). Use them to balance redox reactions (e.g., KMnO₄ in acidic medium). - **Color of Ions**: Associate colors with common ions (e.g., Cu²⁺: blue, Fe³⁺: yellow/brown, Ni²⁺: green, Co²⁺: pink). - **Lanthanoid Contraction**: Understand its impact on post-lanthanoid elements (e.g., Zr and Hf have similar radii, making separation difficult). - **Magnetic Properties**: Calculate the number of unpaired electrons to determine paramagnetism (e.g., Fe³⁺ has 5 unpaired electrons, making it strongly paramagnetic). - **Catalytic Reactions**: Recall examples (e.g., V₂O₅ in the Contact Process, Fe in the Haber Process).

Important facts

  • The d-block elements are called transition elements because they transition between the highly reactive s-block and the less reactive p-block.
  • Lanthanoid contraction explains why the radii of Zr (160 pm) and Hf (159 pm) are nearly identical.
  • Potassium dichromate (K₂Cr₂O₇) is orange in color due to the charge transfer spectrum of Cr⁶⁺.
  • The +3 oxidation state is the most common for lanthanoids, while actinoids exhibit a wider range of oxidation states.
  • Transition metals form interstitial compounds (e.g., steel) due to their ability to trap small atoms like H, C, and N in their lattice.
  • The color of transition metal complexes arises from d-d transitions, which are forbidden in free ions but allowed in complexes due to ligand field effects.
  • Actinoids are all radioactive, with uranium and thorium being the primary fuels in nuclear reactors.
  • The magnetic moment of a transition metal ion can be calculated using the spin-only formula: μ = √[n(n+2)] BM, where n is the number of unpaired electrons.

Formulas and relationships

General electronic configuration of d-block elements: (n-1)d¹⁻¹⁰ ns¹⁻²
Spin-only magnetic moment: μ = √[n(n+2)] BM, where n = number of unpaired electrons
Nernst equation for electrode potential: E = E° - (RT/nF) ln Q (used for transition metal redox couples)
Crystal field splitting energy (Δ₀) for octahedral complexes: Δ₀ = hc/λ, where λ is the wavelength of absorbed light
Lanthanoid contraction: Gradual decrease in atomic/ionic radii from Ce to Lu due to poor shielding of 4f electrons

Common mistakes

  • Assuming all transition metals exhibit the same oxidation states. For example, Zn and Cd show only +2, while Mn shows +2 to +7.
  • Confusing the colors of transition metal ions. For example, Cu²⁺ is blue, not green (Ni²⁺ is green).
  • Forgetting that transition metals lose electrons from the ns orbital first during ionization, not the (n-1)d orbital.
  • Misapplying the lanthanoid contraction to explain properties of d-block elements (it specifically affects post-lanthanoid elements like Zr/Hf).
  • Assuming all transition metal complexes are colored. d¹⁰ (e.g., Zn²⁺) and d⁰ (e.g., Sc³⁺) complexes are colorless due to no d-d transitions.
  • Incorrectly balancing redox reactions involving transition metals. For example, forgetting to account for the acidic/basic medium in KMnO₄ reactions.
  • Overlooking the role of ligands in determining the color of complexes. For example, [Co(H₂O)₆]²⁺ is pink, while [Co(NH₃)₆]²⁺ is yellow.
  • Assuming all actinoids are synthetic. Thorium and uranium are naturally occurring, while elements beyond uranium are synthetic.
