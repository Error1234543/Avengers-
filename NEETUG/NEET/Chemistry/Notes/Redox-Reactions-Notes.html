Redox Reactions - NEET Chemistry Notes

NEETEnglishNotes

1. Introduction to Redox Reactions

Redox (reduction-oxidation) reactions are fundamental chemical processes involving the transfer of electrons between reactants. In these reactions, one species undergoes oxidation (loses electrons) while another undergoes reduction (gains electrons). The species that loses electrons is called the reducing agent, and the species that gains electrons is called the oxidizing agent. Redox reactions are ubiquitous in nature and technology, playing critical roles in processes such as respiration, corrosion, combustion, and electrochemical cells. For NEET, understanding redox reactions is essential not only for inorganic chemistry but also for electrochemistry and organic reaction mechanisms.

2. Oxidation and Reduction: Definitions and Examples

['**Oxidation**: Loss of electrons, increase in oxidation number, or gain of oxygen/loss of hydrogen. Example: In the reaction 2Mg + O₂ → 2MgO, magnesium (Mg) is oxidized to Mg²⁺ as it loses two electrons.', '**Reduction**: Gain of electrons, decrease in oxidation number, or loss of oxygen/gain of hydrogen. Example: In the same reaction, oxygen (O₂) is reduced to O²⁻ as it gains electrons.', '**Oxidizing Agent**: A species that oxidizes another substance by accepting electrons (e.g., O₂, KMnO₄, K₂Cr₂O₇).', '**Reducing Agent**: A species that reduces another substance by donating electrons (e.g., Na, H₂, C).', 'Key Point: Oxidation and reduction always occur simultaneously in a redox reaction.']

3. Oxidation Number: Rules and Calculation

['Oxidation number (or oxidation state) is a hypothetical charge assigned to an atom in a compound or ion, assuming all bonds are ionic. It helps identify redox processes and balance equations. The following rules are used to determine oxidation numbers:', '- The oxidation number of an element in its free (uncombined) state is zero (e.g., Na, O₂, Cl₂).', '- The oxidation number of a monatomic ion is equal to its charge (e.g., Na⁺ = +1, Cl⁻ = -1).', '- The oxidation number of hydrogen is +1 in most compounds, except in metal hydrides where it is -1 (e.g., NaH).', '- The oxidation number of oxygen is -2 in most compounds, except in peroxides (e.g., H₂O₂, where it is -1) and superoxides (e.g., KO₂, where it is -½).', "- The sum of oxidation numbers in a neutral compound is zero. For polyatomic ions, the sum equals the ion's charge.", '**Example**: Calculate the oxidation number of sulfur in H₂SO₄. Let the oxidation number of S be *x*. Then, 2(+1) + *x* + 4(-2) = 0 → *x* = +6.']

4. Balancing Redox Reactions: Ion-Electron Method

['The ion-electron method (or half-reaction method) is a systematic way to balance redox reactions in acidic or basic media. Steps include:', '1. **Write the skeletal equation** and identify the species undergoing oxidation and reduction.', '2. **Split into half-reactions**: Write separate equations for oxidation and reduction.', '3. **Balance atoms other than O and H**: Ensure the same number of atoms on both sides.', '4. **Balance oxygen atoms** by adding H₂O molecules to the side deficient in oxygen.', '5. **Balance hydrogen atoms** by adding H⁺ ions to the side deficient in hydrogen (for acidic media). For basic media, add H₂O to the side deficient in hydrogen and OH⁻ to the opposite side.', '6. **Balance charges** by adding electrons (e⁻) to the side with the higher positive charge.', '7. **Equalize electrons** in both half-reactions by multiplying with appropriate coefficients.', '8. **Add the half-reactions** and cancel common terms (e⁻, H⁺, H₂O).', '**Example**: Balance the reaction MnO₄⁻ + Fe²⁺ → Mn²⁺ + Fe³⁺ in acidic medium.', '- Oxidation half: Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺ + e⁻', '- Reduction half: MnO₄⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4H₂O', '- Multiply oxidation half by 5 and reduction half by 1, then add: MnO₄⁻ + 5Fe²⁺ + 8H⁺ → Mn²⁺ + 5Fe³⁺ + 4H₂O.']

5. Types of Redox Reactions

['**Combination Reactions**: Two or more substances combine to form a single product (e.g., 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O).', '**Decomposition Reactions**: A single compound breaks down into two or more products (e.g., 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂).', '**Displacement Reactions**: An element displaces another element from its compound (e.g., Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu). These can be metal displacement or non-metal displacement reactions.', '**Disproportionation Reactions**: A single species is simultaneously oxidized and reduced (e.g., 2H₂O₂ → 2H₂O + O₂, where oxygen in H₂O₂ has an oxidation number of -1 and changes to -2 and 0).', '**Comproportionation Reactions**: Two species of the same element in different oxidation states form a product where the element is in an intermediate oxidation state (e.g., SO₂ + 2H₂S → 3S + 2H₂O).']

6. Redox Reactions in Everyday Life and Industry

['- **Corrosion**: Oxidation of metals (e.g., rusting of iron: 4Fe + 3O₂ + 6H₂O → 4Fe(OH)₃).', '- **Combustion**: Rapid oxidation of fuels (e.g., CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O).', '- **Respiration**: Oxidation of glucose in cells (C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy).', '- **Bleaching**: Oxidizing agents like Cl₂ or H₂O₂ remove color from substances (e.g., Cl₂ + H₂O → HCl + HClO; HClO bleaches by oxidation).', '- **Electrochemical Cells**: Redox reactions generate electricity in batteries (e.g., Zn-Cu cell: Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ (oxidation), Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu (reduction)).']

7. Common Oxidizing and Reducing Agents

['**Oxidizing Agents**:', '- Potassium permanganate (KMnO₄): Strong oxidizer in acidic, neutral, or basic media.', '- Potassium dichromate (K₂Cr₂O₇): Used in acidic media (Cr₂O₇²⁻ → 2Cr³⁺).', '- Hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂): Acts as both oxidizing and reducing agent.', '- Halogens (Cl₂, Br₂, I₂): Strong oxidizers (e.g., Cl₂ + 2e⁻ → 2Cl⁻).', '**Reducing Agents**:', '- Metals (Na, Zn, Fe): Donate electrons (e.g., Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻).', '- Hydrogen (H₂): Reduces metal oxides (e.g., CuO + H₂ → Cu + H₂O).', '- Carbon (C): Used in metallurgy (e.g., Fe₂O₃ + 3C → 2Fe + 3CO).', '- Sulfur dioxide (SO₂): Reduces halogens (e.g., SO₂ + Cl₂ + 2H₂O → H₂SO₄ + 2HCl).']

8. Applications in NEET: Problem-Solving Strategies

['- **Identify redox pairs**: Determine which species is oxidized and which is reduced by tracking oxidation numbers.', '- **Balance equations**: Use the ion-electron method for complex reactions, especially in acidic/basic media.', '- **Predict products**: For displacement reactions, use the reactivity series to predict feasibility (e.g., Zn displaces Cu²⁺ but not Na⁺).', '- **Stoichiometry**: Relate moles of oxidizing/reducing agents to products using balanced equations (e.g., how many moles of KMnO₄ are needed to oxidize 5 moles of Fe²⁺?).', '- **Real-world context**: Apply redox concepts to electrochemical cells, corrosion, and biological processes (e.g., calculate cell potential or identify anodic/cathodic reactions).']

9. Common Mistakes and Confusion Points

['- **Confusing oxidation number with valency**: Oxidation number is a hypothetical charge, while valency is the combining capacity of an element.', '- **Incorrectly balancing half-reactions**: Forgetting to balance oxygen/hydrogen or charges can lead to errors. Always verify the final equation.', '- **Misidentifying oxidizing/reducing agents**: The species that is reduced is the oxidizing agent, not the one being oxidized.', '- **Overlooking disproportionation**: Not recognizing when a single species undergoes both oxidation and reduction (e.g., Cl₂ in alkaline medium: Cl₂ + 2OH⁻ → Cl⁻ + ClO⁻ + H₂O).', '- **Ignoring media conditions**: The products of redox reactions can differ in acidic vs. basic media (e.g., MnO₄⁻ reduces to Mn²⁺ in acid but MnO₂ in neutral/basic media).']

Important facts

  • Oxidation involves loss of electrons, increase in oxidation number, or gain of oxygen/loss of hydrogen.
  • Reduction involves gain of electrons, decrease in oxidation number, or loss of oxygen/gain of hydrogen.
  • Oxidation and reduction always occur simultaneously in a redox reaction.
  • The oxidation number of oxygen is -2 in most compounds, except in peroxides (-1) and superoxides (-½).
  • In acidic media, balance oxygen by adding H₂O and hydrogen by adding H⁺; in basic media, add H₂O to the side deficient in hydrogen and OH⁻ to the opposite side.
  • Disproportionation reactions involve a single species being both oxidized and reduced.
  • Potassium permanganate (KMnO₄) is a stronger oxidizing agent in acidic media than in neutral or basic media.
  • The reactivity series helps predict the feasibility of metal displacement reactions (e.g., Zn can displace Cu²⁺ but not Na⁺).

Formulas and relationships

Oxidation number calculation: Sum of oxidation numbers in a neutral compound = 0; in a polyatomic ion = ion charge.
Balancing redox reactions (ion-electron method):
- Acidic medium: Add H₂O to balance O, H⁺ to balance H, and e⁻ to balance charge.
- Basic medium: Add H₂O to balance O, H₂O to the side deficient in H, OH⁻ to the opposite side, and e⁻ to balance charge.
Nernst equation (for electrochemical cells): E = E° - (RT/nF) ln(Q), where E° is standard cell potential, R is gas constant, T is temperature, n is moles of electrons, F is Faraday constant, and Q is reaction quotient.

Common mistakes

  • Forgetting to balance charges in half-reactions, leading to incorrect final equations.
  • Assuming all reactions involving oxygen are redox reactions (e.g., acid-base reactions like CaO + H₂O → Ca(OH)₂ are not redox).
  • Incorrectly assigning oxidation numbers to elements in polyatomic ions or complex compounds.
  • Mixing up oxidizing and reducing agents (e.g., identifying the species being oxidized as the oxidizing agent).
  • Not considering the effect of pH on redox reactions (e.g., MnO₄⁻ reduces to Mn²⁺ in acid but MnO₂ in neutral/basic media).
