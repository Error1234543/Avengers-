Chemical Kinetics - NEET Chemistry Notes

NEETEnglishNotes

1. Introduction to Chemical Kinetics

Chemical kinetics is the branch of physical chemistry that studies the rates of chemical reactions, the factors affecting these rates, and the mechanisms by which reactions proceed. Unlike thermodynamics, which deals with the feasibility of a reaction, kinetics focuses on how fast a reaction occurs. For NEET, understanding kinetics is crucial as it frequently appears in both conceptual and numerical questions. Key terms include reaction rate (change in concentration of reactants or products per unit time), rate law (mathematical expression relating reaction rate to reactant concentrations), and order of reaction (sum of exponents in the rate law). The rate of a reaction is always positive and is typically expressed in mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹.

2. Rate of Reaction and Rate Law

The rate of a reaction is defined as the change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time. For a general reaction: aA + bB → cC + dD, the rate can be expressed as: Rate = -1/a d[A]/dt = -1/b d[B]/dt = 1/c d[C]/dt = 1/d d[D]/dt. The negative sign for reactants indicates their concentration decreases over time. The rate law expresses the rate as a function of reactant concentrations: Rate = k[A]^m[B]^n, where k is the rate constant, and m and n are the orders of the reaction with respect to A and B, respectively. The overall order of the reaction is m + n. It is important to note that the order of a reaction is determined experimentally and cannot be predicted from the stoichiometry of the balanced equation.

3. Order of Reaction and Molecularity

The order of a reaction refers to the sum of the exponents of the concentration terms in the rate law. It can be zero, first, second, or even fractional. For example, if Rate = k[A]^2[B]^1, the reaction is second-order with respect to A and first-order with respect to B, with an overall order of 3. Molecularity, on the other hand, refers to the number of molecules, atoms, or ions participating in the rate-determining step of a reaction mechanism. It is always a whole number (unimolecular, bimolecular, or termolecular) and is a theoretical concept. While order is experimental, molecularity is mechanistic. For NEET, it is critical to distinguish between these two terms, as they are often confused.

4. Integrated Rate Laws and Half-Life

Integrated rate laws relate the concentration of reactants to time and are derived from the differential rate laws. They are essential for determining the order of a reaction and calculating the rate constant. For a zero-order reaction (Rate = k), the integrated rate law is [A] = [A]₀ - kt. For a first-order reaction (Rate = k[A]), it is ln[A] = ln[A]₀ - kt. For a second-order reaction (Rate = k[A]²), it is 1/[A] = 1/[A]₀ + kt. The half-life (t₁/₂) of a reaction is the time required for the concentration of a reactant to reduce to half its initial value. For a first-order reaction, t₁/₂ = 0.693/k, which is independent of the initial concentration. For zero-order and second-order reactions, the half-life depends on the initial concentration.

5. Factors Affecting Reaction Rates

Several factors influence the rate of a chemical reaction, and understanding these is vital for NEET: 1. **Concentration of Reactants**: Higher concentrations generally increase the reaction rate (except for zero-order reactions). 2. **Temperature**: An increase in temperature typically increases the reaction rate, as described by the Arrhenius equation: k = A e^(-Ea/RT), where A is the pre-exponential factor, Ea is the activation energy, R is the gas constant, and T is the temperature in Kelvin. 3. **Catalysts**: Catalysts speed up reactions by providing an alternative pathway with lower activation energy. They are not consumed in the reaction. 4. **Surface Area**: For reactions involving solids, increasing the surface area (e.g., by grinding) increases the rate. 5. **Pressure**: For gaseous reactions, increasing pressure increases the rate by increasing the concentration of gas molecules. 6. **Nature of Reactants**: Reactions involving ionic compounds or simple molecules are generally faster than those involving covalent bonds or complex molecules.

6. Arrhenius Equation and Activation Energy

The Arrhenius equation (k = A e^(-Ea/RT)) quantitatively describes the effect of temperature on the rate constant (k). Here, A is the frequency factor (related to the frequency of collisions with correct orientation), Ea is the activation energy (minimum energy required for reactants to form products), R is the universal gas constant (8.314 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹), and T is the temperature in Kelvin. The equation can be linearized as ln k = ln A - Ea/RT, which allows for the determination of Ea from a plot of ln k vs. 1/T. A higher Ea means the reaction rate is more sensitive to temperature changes. For NEET, numerical problems often involve calculating Ea or the rate constant at a different temperature using the Arrhenius equation.

7. Collision Theory and Transition State Theory

Collision theory explains reaction rates in terms of molecular collisions. For a reaction to occur, reactant molecules must collide with sufficient energy (greater than or equal to Ea) and proper orientation. The rate of reaction is proportional to the number of effective collisions per unit time. Transition state theory (or activated complex theory) proposes that reactants form a high-energy, unstable intermediate called the activated complex before converting into products. The energy difference between the reactants and the activated complex is the activation energy. This theory helps explain why catalysts lower Ea by stabilizing the transition state.

8. Reaction Mechanisms and Rate-Determining Step

A reaction mechanism is a step-by-step sequence of elementary reactions that describes how the overall reaction occurs. Each step has its own rate law and molecularity. The slowest step in the mechanism is called the rate-determining step (RDS), as it limits the overall reaction rate. The rate law for the overall reaction is determined by the RDS. For example, if the RDS is A → B (unimolecular), the rate law is Rate = k[A]. If the RDS is A + B → C (bimolecular), the rate law is Rate = k[A][B]. For NEET, it is important to identify the RDS from a given mechanism and derive the rate law accordingly.

9. Catalysis and Types of Catalysts

A catalyst is a substance that increases the rate of a reaction without being consumed. It works by providing an alternative pathway with lower activation energy. Catalysts can be classified into: 1. **Homogeneous Catalysts**: Present in the same phase as the reactants (e.g., enzymes in biological systems). 2. **Heterogeneous Catalysts**: Present in a different phase than the reactants (e.g., solid catalysts in gaseous reactions). 3. **Positive Catalysts**: Increase the reaction rate. 4. **Negative Catalysts (Inhibitors)**: Decrease the reaction rate. 5. **Autocatalysts**: A product of the reaction that acts as a catalyst (e.g., Mn²⁺ in the oxidation of oxalic acid by KMnO₄). For NEET, understanding the role of catalysts in industrial processes (e.g., Haber process, Contact process) and biological systems (e.g., enzymes) is important.

10. Numerical Problems and Applications

Chemical kinetics is heavily numerical in NEET, and aspirants must be comfortable solving problems involving: 1. **Determining Order of Reaction**: Using initial rate data or integrated rate laws. 2. **Calculating Rate Constants**: From given concentration vs. time data. 3. **Half-Life Calculations**: For first-order, zero-order, and second-order reactions. 4. **Arrhenius Equation Problems**: Calculating Ea or k at different temperatures. 5. **Reaction Mechanisms**: Identifying the RDS and deriving the rate law. Example Problem: For the reaction 2A + B → C, the rate law is Rate = k[A][B]². If the concentration of A is doubled and B is halved, what is the new rate? Solution: New rate = k[2A][(1/2)B]² = k * 2[A] * (1/4)[B]² = (1/2) k[A][B]² = (1/2) original rate.

Important facts

  • The rate of a reaction is always positive and is expressed in mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹.
  • The order of a reaction is determined experimentally and cannot be predicted from the stoichiometry of the balanced equation.
  • For a first-order reaction, the half-life is independent of the initial concentration and is given by t₁/₂ = 0.693/k.
  • The Arrhenius equation (k = A e^(-Ea/RT)) shows that the rate constant increases exponentially with temperature.
  • A catalyst lowers the activation energy of a reaction but does not affect the equilibrium position or the Gibbs free energy change (ΔG).
  • The rate-determining step in a reaction mechanism is the slowest step and determines the overall rate law.
  • Molecularity is always a whole number (1, 2, or 3), while the order of a reaction can be zero, fractional, or negative.
  • For a zero-order reaction, the rate is constant and independent of the reactant concentration.
  • Enzymes are biological catalysts that increase the rate of biochemical reactions by lowering the activation energy.
  • The integrated rate law for a second-order reaction is 1/[A] = 1/[A]₀ + kt.

Formulas and relationships

Rate law: Rate = k[A]^m[B]^n
Zero-order integrated rate law: [A] = [A]₀ - kt
First-order integrated rate law: ln[A] = ln[A]₀ - kt
Second-order integrated rate law: 1/[A] = 1/[A]₀ + kt
Half-life for zero-order reaction: t₁/₂ = [A]₀ / 2k
Half-life for first-order reaction: t₁/₂ = 0.693 / k
Half-life for second-order reaction: t₁/₂ = 1 / k[A]₀
Arrhenius equation: k = A e^(-Ea/RT)
Linear form of Arrhenius equation: ln k = ln A - Ea/RT
Effect of temperature on rate constant: ln(k₂/k₁) = (Ea/R) (1/T₁ - 1/T₂)

Common mistakes

  • Confusing order of reaction with molecularity. Order is experimental, while molecularity is theoretical and applies to elementary steps.
  • Assuming the order of a reaction can be determined from the stoichiometric coefficients in the balanced equation. Order must be determined experimentally.
  • Forgetting the negative sign in the rate expression for reactants (e.g., Rate = -d[A]/dt).
  • Misapplying the integrated rate laws. For example, using the first-order integrated rate law for a reaction that is not first-order.
  • Incorrectly calculating the half-life for zero-order or second-order reactions. Remember that for these, the half-life depends on the initial concentration.
  • Assuming that all collisions between reactant molecules lead to a reaction. Only collisions with sufficient energy and proper orientation are effective.
  • Forgetting that the rate constant (k) is temperature-dependent and changes with temperature according to the Arrhenius equation.
  • Misidentifying the rate-determining step in a reaction mechanism. The slowest step is the RDS, and it determines the rate law.
  • Assuming that a catalyst changes the equilibrium position of a reaction. Catalysts only speed up the attainment of equilibrium but do not affect the equilibrium constant.
  • Using incorrect units for the rate constant (k). The units of k depend on the order of the reaction (e.g., mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹ for zero-order, s⁻¹ for first-order, L mol⁻¹ s⁻¹ for second-order).
