Introduction to Biomolecules

Biomolecules are organic compounds that are essential for the structure and function of living organisms. They are primarily composed of carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen, phosphorus, and sulfur. The four major classes of biomolecules are carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids. Each class plays a distinct role in biological systems: carbohydrates serve as energy sources and structural components, proteins act as enzymes, hormones, and structural elements, lipids store energy and form cell membranes, and nucleic acids store and transmit genetic information. Understanding the structure and function of these biomolecules is crucial for NEET, as questions often test their chemical properties, biological roles, and interrelationships.

Carbohydrates: Structure and Classification

Carbohydrates are polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones and their derivatives. They are classified based on the number of sugar units: monosaccharides (e.g., glucose, fructose), disaccharides (e.g., sucrose, lactose), and polysaccharides (e.g., starch, cellulose, glycogen). Monosaccharides are the simplest form and cannot be hydrolyzed further. They exist in cyclic forms (pyranose or furanose) due to intramolecular hemiacetal or hemiketal formation. Disaccharides consist of two monosaccharide units linked by a glycosidic bond, while polysaccharides are long chains of monosaccharides. For NEET, focus on the structural differences between starch (α-1,4-glycosidic bonds) and cellulose (β-1,4-glycosidic bonds), as well as the reducing and non-reducing nature of sugars.

Proteins: Amino Acids and Peptide Bonds

Proteins are polymers of amino acids linked by peptide bonds. Amino acids contain an amino group (-NH₂), a carboxyl group (-COOH), and a unique side chain (R-group) attached to the α-carbon. There are 20 standard amino acids, classified as non-polar, polar, acidic, or basic based on their R-groups. The peptide bond is formed by a condensation reaction between the carboxyl group of one amino acid and the amino group of another, releasing a water molecule. Proteins exhibit four levels of structure: primary (linear sequence of amino acids), secondary (α-helices and β-pleated sheets stabilized by hydrogen bonds), tertiary (3D folding due to interactions like disulfide bridges, hydrogen bonds, and hydrophobic interactions), and quaternary (assembly of multiple polypeptide chains). NEET often tests the denaturation of proteins (loss of structure due to heat, pH, or chemicals) and the role of enzymes as biological catalysts.

Lipids: Classification and Biological Roles

Lipids are hydrophobic or amphipathic molecules insoluble in water but soluble in organic solvents. They are classified into simple lipids (fats and waxes), compound lipids (phospholipids and glycolipids), and derived lipids (steroids and eicosanoids). Fats (triglycerides) are esters of glycerol and three fatty acids, serving as energy reserves. Phospholipids are major components of cell membranes, with a hydrophilic head (phosphate group) and hydrophobic tails (fatty acids). Steroids, like cholesterol, regulate membrane fluidity and serve as precursors for hormones. NEET questions frequently focus on the structure of phospholipid bilayers, the role of cholesterol, and the differences between saturated and unsaturated fatty acids (e.g., presence of double bonds, melting points).

Nucleic Acids: DNA and RNA

Nucleic acids are polymers of nucleotides, which consist of a nitrogenous base, a pentose sugar (ribose in RNA, deoxyribose in DNA), and a phosphate group. DNA (deoxyribonucleic acid) stores genetic information, while RNA (ribonucleic acid) is involved in protein synthesis. The nitrogenous bases are classified as purines (adenine, guanine) and pyrimidines (cytosine, thymine in DNA, uracil in RNA). Nucleotides are linked by phosphodiester bonds between the 3'-hydroxyl group of one sugar and the 5'-phosphate group of the next. DNA is double-stranded, with complementary base pairing (A-T, G-C) via hydrogen bonds, forming a double helix. RNA is typically single-stranded but can fold into complex structures. NEET emphasizes the central dogma of molecular biology (DNA → RNA → Protein), the differences between DNA and RNA, and the role of mRNA, tRNA, and rRNA in translation.

Enzymes: Mechanism and Regulation

Enzymes are biological catalysts that speed up chemical reactions without being consumed. They are typically proteins, though some RNA molecules (ribozymes) also exhibit catalytic activity. Enzymes lower the activation energy of reactions by binding to substrates at the active site, forming an enzyme-substrate complex. The lock-and-key model and induced-fit model explain substrate specificity. Enzyme activity is influenced by temperature, pH, substrate concentration, and inhibitors. Competitive inhibitors bind to the active site, while non-competitive inhibitors bind elsewhere, altering the enzyme's shape. NEET questions often test the Michaelis-Menten kinetics (Kₘ and Vₘₐₓ), enzyme classification (e.g., oxidoreductases, hydrolases), and the effects of inhibitors on reaction rates.

Vitamins and Hormones

Vitamins are organic compounds required in small amounts for normal metabolic functions. They are classified as fat-soluble (A, D, E, K) or water-soluble (B-complex, C). Fat-soluble vitamins are stored in the body, while water-soluble vitamins are excreted and must be replenished regularly. Hormones are chemical messengers secreted by endocrine glands, regulating physiological processes. They are classified as steroid hormones (e.g., cortisol, estrogen) or peptide/amino acid-derived hormones (e.g., insulin, adrenaline). For NEET, focus on the biological roles of key vitamins (e.g., vitamin A for vision, vitamin C for collagen synthesis) and hormones (e.g., insulin regulating blood glucose, thyroxine controlling metabolism), as well as deficiencies leading to diseases (e.g., scurvy, rickets).

Biomolecules in Metabolism

Metabolism encompasses all biochemical reactions in living organisms, divided into catabolism (breakdown of molecules to release energy) and anabolism (synthesis of molecules requiring energy). Carbohydrates are metabolized via glycolysis (glucose → pyruvate), the Krebs cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation to produce ATP. Proteins are broken down into amino acids, which enter the Krebs cycle or are used for biosynthesis. Lipids are metabolized via β-oxidation to produce acetyl-CoA, which enters the Krebs cycle. NEET questions often test the integration of these pathways, such as the role of acetyl-CoA as a central metabolite, the Cori cycle (lactate recycling), and the regulation of metabolic pathways by hormones like insulin and glucagon.

Biomolecules and Disease

Biomolecules are implicated in various diseases due to genetic mutations, deficiencies, or metabolic disorders. For example, sickle cell anemia results from a single amino acid substitution in hemoglobin, altering its structure and function. Diabetes mellitus is caused by insulin deficiency or resistance, disrupting glucose metabolism. Phenylketonuria (PKU) is a genetic disorder where phenylalanine cannot be metabolized, leading to intellectual disabilities if untreated. NEET often includes questions on the molecular basis of such diseases, the role of biomolecules in diagnosis (e.g., glucose levels in diabetes), and therapeutic interventions (e.g., enzyme replacement therapy for lysosomal storage disorders).

Important facts

  • Glucose is a reducing sugar due to its free aldehyde group, while sucrose is non-reducing because the glycosidic bond involves both anomeric carbons.
  • The primary structure of a protein is determined by the sequence of amino acids, while the secondary structure is stabilized by hydrogen bonds between backbone atoms.
  • Phospholipids are amphipathic, with hydrophilic heads and hydrophobic tails, making them ideal for forming cell membranes.
  • DNA replication is semi-conservative, meaning each new double helix consists of one original strand and one newly synthesized strand.
  • Enzymes are highly specific due to the shape and chemical properties of their active sites, which complement their substrates.
  • Vitamin D is synthesized in the skin upon exposure to UV light and regulates calcium and phosphate metabolism.
  • The genetic code is degenerate, meaning multiple codons can code for the same amino acid, but each codon specifies only one amino acid.
  • Lactose intolerance is caused by a deficiency of the enzyme lactase, leading to the inability to digest lactose.
  • Cholesterol is a precursor for steroid hormones like cortisol and sex hormones (estrogen, testosterone).
  • The Krebs cycle (citric acid cycle) is a central metabolic pathway that generates NADH and FADH₂ for oxidative phosphorylation.

Formulas and relationships

General formula for monosaccharides: CₙH₂ₙOₙ (where n = 3 to 7).
Peptide bond formation: R₁-COOH + H₂N-R₂ → R₁-CONH-R₂ + H₂O.
Michaelis-Menten equation: V = (Vₘₐₓ [S]) / (Kₘ + [S]), where V is the reaction velocity, Vₘₐₓ is the maximum velocity, [S] is the substrate concentration, and Kₘ is the Michaelis constant.
Energy yield from glucose oxidation: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + ~30-38 ATP.
β-oxidation of fatty acids: Each cycle shortens the fatty acid chain by 2 carbons, producing 1 FADH₂, 1 NADH, and 1 acetyl-CoA.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing the structures of glucose and fructose: glucose is an aldohexose, while fructose is a ketohexose.
  • Assuming all disaccharides are reducing sugars: sucrose is non-reducing because its glycosidic bond involves both anomeric carbons.
  • Misidentifying the levels of protein structure: primary (sequence), secondary (α-helix/β-sheet), tertiary (3D folding), quaternary (multi-subunit assembly).
  • Overlooking the difference between saturated and unsaturated fatty acids: saturated fats have no double bonds and are solid at room temperature, while unsaturated fats have double bonds and are liquid.
  • Forgetting that DNA contains thymine, while RNA contains uracil.
  • Assuming all enzymes are proteins: ribozymes are RNA molecules with catalytic activity.
  • Confusing competitive and non-competitive inhibition: competitive inhibitors bind to the active site, while non-competitive inhibitors bind elsewhere, altering the enzyme's shape.
  • Misinterpreting the role of vitamins: fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K) can be stored in the body, while water-soluble vitamins (B-complex, C) must be replenished regularly.
  • Assuming all hormones are proteins: steroid hormones (e.g., cortisol) are derived from cholesterol, while peptide hormones (e.g., insulin) are proteins.
  • Forgetting that the genetic code is universal (with minor exceptions) and degenerate (multiple codons for the same amino acid).
