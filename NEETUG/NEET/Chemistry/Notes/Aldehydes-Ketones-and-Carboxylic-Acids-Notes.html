Aldehydes, Ketones and Carboxylic Acids - NEET Chemistry Notes

NEETEnglishNotes

1. Introduction and Nomenclature

Aldehydes, ketones, and carboxylic acids are important classes of carbonyl compounds in organic chemistry. Aldehydes contain the carbonyl group (C=O) bonded to at least one hydrogen atom, with the general formula R-CHO. Ketones contain the carbonyl group bonded to two carbon atoms, with the general formula R-CO-R'. Carboxylic acids contain the carboxyl group (-COOH), which is a combination of a carbonyl group and a hydroxyl group, with the general formula R-COOH. Nomenclature follows IUPAC rules: - Aldehydes: Replace the '-e' ending of the parent alkane with '-al' (e.g., methanal, ethanal). - Ketones: Replace the '-e' ending of the parent alkane with '-one' and specify the position of the carbonyl group (e.g., propan-2-one). - Carboxylic acids: Replace the '-e' ending of the parent alkane with '-oic acid' (e.g., ethanoic acid). Common names are also frequently used, such as formaldehyde (methanal), acetaldehyde (ethanal), acetone (propan-2-one), and acetic acid (ethanoic acid).

2. Structure and Bonding of Carbonyl Group

The carbonyl group (C=O) is planar due to sp² hybridization of the carbon atom. The bond angle is approximately 120°, and the C=O bond is polar due to the electronegativity difference between carbon and oxygen. This polarity makes the carbonyl carbon electrophilic and the oxygen nucleophilic, which is crucial for the reactivity of aldehydes, ketones, and carboxylic acids. Key points: - The C=O bond length is shorter than a C-O single bond but longer than a C≡O triple bond. - The carbonyl group exhibits resonance, with the major contributor being the structure where oxygen bears a negative charge and carbon bears a positive charge. - The polarity of the carbonyl group influences physical properties such as boiling points and solubility in water.

3. Preparation of Aldehydes and Ketones

Aldehydes and ketones can be prepared through various methods: **Aldehydes:** - Oxidation of primary alcohols using mild oxidizing agents like pyridinium chlorochromate (PCC) or Swern oxidation. - Reduction of acyl chlorides, esters, or nitriles using hydrogen in the presence of palladium on barium sulfate (Rosenmund reduction). - Ozonolysis of alkenes, followed by reductive workup. - Hydroboration-oxidation of terminal alkynes. **Ketones:** - Oxidation of secondary alcohols using oxidizing agents like potassium dichromate (K₂Cr₂O₇) or chromium trioxide (CrO₃). - Friedel-Crafts acylation of aromatic compounds with acyl chlorides in the presence of AlCl₃. - Hydration of alkynes (Markovnikov addition). - Reaction of nitriles with Grignard reagents, followed by hydrolysis.

4. Chemical Reactions of Aldehydes and Ketones

Aldehydes and ketones undergo nucleophilic addition reactions due to the electrophilic nature of the carbonyl carbon. Key reactions include: **Nucleophilic Addition Reactions:** - Addition of hydrogen cyanide (HCN) to form cyanohydrins. - Addition of Grignard reagents to form alcohols. - Addition of alcohols to form hemiacetals and acetals (in the presence of acid catalysts). - Addition of ammonia and its derivatives (e.g., hydroxylamine, hydrazine) to form imines, oximes, and hydrazones. **Oxidation Reactions:** - Aldehydes are easily oxidized to carboxylic acids using mild oxidizing agents like Tollens' reagent (silver mirror test) or Fehling's solution. - Ketones are generally resistant to oxidation but can be cleaved under vigorous conditions (e.g., with hot potassium permanganate). **Reduction Reactions:** - Reduction to alcohols using sodium borohydride (NaBH₄) or lithium aluminium hydride (LiAlH₄). - Clemmensen reduction (using zinc amalgam and hydrochloric acid) or Wolff-Kishner reduction (using hydrazine and a base) to reduce the carbonyl group to a methylene group. **Aldol Condensation:** - Aldehydes and ketones with α-hydrogen atoms undergo aldol condensation in the presence of a base to form β-hydroxy aldehydes or ketones (aldols). Dehydration of the aldol product yields α,β-unsaturated carbonyl compounds.

5. Carboxylic Acids: Structure and Nomenclature

Carboxylic acids contain the carboxyl group (-COOH), which is a combination of a carbonyl group (C=O) and a hydroxyl group (-OH). The carboxyl group is highly polar and can form hydrogen bonds, leading to higher boiling points and solubility in water compared to aldehydes and ketones. Nomenclature: - IUPAC names are derived by replacing the '-e' ending of the parent alkane with '-oic acid' (e.g., methanoic acid, ethanoic acid). - Common names are widely used, such as formic acid (methanoic acid), acetic acid (ethanoic acid), and benzoic acid (benzenecarboxylic acid). - Dicarboxylic acids are named by adding the suffix '-dioic acid' to the parent alkane (e.g., ethanedioic acid, commonly known as oxalic acid).

6. Preparation of Carboxylic Acids

Carboxylic acids can be prepared through several methods: - Oxidation of primary alcohols or aldehydes using strong oxidizing agents like potassium permanganate (KMnO₄) or potassium dichromate (K₂Cr₂O₇). - Oxidation of alkylbenzenes using chromic acid or potassium permanganate to form benzoic acid. - Hydrolysis of nitriles (R-CN) using aqueous acids or bases to form carboxylic acids. - Carbonation of Grignard reagents (R-MgX) with carbon dioxide (CO₂), followed by hydrolysis. - Hydrolysis of esters or amides under acidic or basic conditions.

7. Chemical Reactions of Carboxylic Acids

Carboxylic acids exhibit a wide range of reactions due to the presence of the carboxyl group: **Acidity:** - Carboxylic acids are weak acids and can donate a proton (H⁺) to form carboxylate anions (R-COO⁻). The acidity is influenced by the stability of the carboxylate anion, which is resonance-stabilized. - Electron-withdrawing groups (e.g., -NO₂, -Cl) increase acidity, while electron-donating groups (e.g., -CH₃, -OCH₃) decrease acidity. **Formation of Derivatives:** - Reaction with alcohols to form esters (esterification) in the presence of an acid catalyst. - Reaction with thionyl chloride (SOCl₂) or phosphorus pentachloride (PCl₅) to form acyl chlorides. - Reaction with ammonia or amines to form amides. - Reaction with phosphorus pentoxide (P₂O₅) to form acid anhydrides. **Reduction:** - Reduction to primary alcohols using lithium aluminium hydride (LiAlH₄). - Partial reduction to aldehydes using diisobutylaluminium hydride (DIBAL-H). **Decarboxylation:** - Carboxylic acids lose carbon dioxide (CO₂) when heated with soda lime (NaOH + CaO) to form alkanes. β-Keto acids and malonic acids undergo decarboxylation more readily.

8. Important Tests and Distinctions

Several qualitative tests are used to distinguish between aldehydes, ketones, and carboxylic acids: - **Tollens' Test:** Aldehydes reduce Tollens' reagent (ammoniacal silver nitrate) to form a silver mirror, while ketones do not react. - **Fehling's Test:** Aldehydes reduce Fehling's solution (a mixture of copper(II) sulfate, sodium potassium tartrate, and sodium hydroxide) to form a red precipitate of copper(I) oxide, while ketones do not react. - **2,4-Dinitrophenylhydrazine (2,4-DNP) Test:** Both aldehydes and ketones react with 2,4-DNP to form yellow, orange, or red precipitates. - **Sodium Bicarbonate Test:** Carboxylic acids react with sodium bicarbonate (NaHCO₃) to evolve carbon dioxide gas, which can be detected by effervescence. - **Neutral Ferric Chloride Test:** Phenolic compounds (not carboxylic acids) give a violet coloration with neutral ferric chloride solution.

9. Uses and Biological Importance

Aldehydes, ketones, and carboxylic acids have significant industrial and biological applications: - **Aldehydes:** Formaldehyde is used as a disinfectant and preservative, while acetaldehyde is used in the synthesis of acetic acid and other chemicals. - **Ketones:** Acetone is a widely used solvent and is also a key intermediate in the synthesis of plastics and pharmaceuticals. - **Carboxylic Acids:** Acetic acid is used in the production of vinegar and as a solvent. Benzoic acid and its salts are used as food preservatives. Fatty acids are essential components of lipids and play a crucial role in biological membranes and energy storage. Biologically, carboxylic acids are involved in metabolic pathways such as the citric acid cycle (Krebs cycle), where they are intermediates in the oxidation of carbohydrates, fats, and proteins.

10. Common Mistakes and Confusion Points

Students often confuse the following concepts: - **Aldehydes vs. Ketones:** Aldehydes have at least one hydrogen atom attached to the carbonyl carbon, while ketones have two carbon atoms attached. This difference affects their reactivity, particularly in oxidation reactions. - **Oxidation of Aldehydes vs. Ketones:** Aldehydes are easily oxidized to carboxylic acids, while ketones are generally resistant to oxidation under mild conditions. - **Reduction of Carboxylic Acids:** Carboxylic acids are reduced to primary alcohols using LiAlH₄, not NaBH₄, which is insufficiently strong. - **Aldol Condensation:** Only aldehydes and ketones with α-hydrogen atoms can undergo aldol condensation. The reaction requires a base catalyst and proceeds via the formation of an enolate ion. - **Decarboxylation:** Not all carboxylic acids undergo decarboxylation readily. β-Keto acids and malonic acids are exceptions that decarboxylate more easily due to the stability of the resulting enolate ion.

Important facts

  • Aldehydes and ketones are polar compounds due to the carbonyl group, which influences their physical properties and reactivity.
  • Aldehydes can be distinguished from ketones using Tollens' reagent or Fehling's solution, which aldehydes reduce but ketones do not.
  • Carboxylic acids are weak acids and form resonance-stabilized carboxylate anions, which explains their acidity.
  • The presence of electron-withdrawing groups increases the acidity of carboxylic acids, while electron-donating groups decrease it.
  • Aldol condensation is a key reaction for aldehydes and ketones with α-hydrogen atoms, leading to the formation of β-hydroxy carbonyl compounds.
  • Carboxylic acids can be reduced to primary alcohols using LiAlH₄, but not with NaBH₄.
  • Esters, acyl chlorides, amides, and acid anhydrides are important derivatives of carboxylic acids, formed through nucleophilic acyl substitution reactions.
  • Decarboxylation of β-keto acids and malonic acids occurs readily due to the stability of the resulting enolate ion.

Formulas and relationships

General formula of aldehydes: R-CHO
General formula of ketones: R-CO-R'
General formula of carboxylic acids: R-COOH
Nucleophilic addition to carbonyl group: R₂C=O + Nu⁻ → R₂C(Nu)O⁻
Aldol condensation: 2 R-CH₂-CHO → R-CH₂-CH(OH)-CH(R)-CHO (in the presence of a base)
Esterification: R-COOH + R'-OH → R-COOR' + H₂O (in the presence of an acid catalyst)
Decarboxylation: R-COOH → R-H + CO₂ (in the presence of soda lime or heat)

Common mistakes

  • Confusing aldehydes with ketones in oxidation reactions, as aldehydes are easily oxidized while ketones are not.
  • Using NaBH₄ for the reduction of carboxylic acids instead of LiAlH₄, which is the correct reducing agent.
  • Assuming all carboxylic acids undergo decarboxylation readily, when only β-keto acids and malonic acids do so easily.
  • Forgetting that aldehydes and ketones without α-hydrogen atoms cannot undergo aldol condensation.
  • Misidentifying the products of nucleophilic addition reactions, such as confusing hemiacetals with acetals.
  • Overlooking the role of resonance in stabilizing the carboxylate anion, which explains the acidity of carboxylic acids.
  • Incorrectly applying the conditions for esterification, which requires an acid catalyst and not just mixing an alcohol with a carboxylic acid.
