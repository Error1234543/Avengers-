Introduction to Kinetic Theory of Gases

The kinetic theory of gases explains the behavior of gases based on the motion of their constituent molecules. It assumes that gases consist of a large number of tiny particles (atoms or molecules) in constant random motion. These particles collide elastically with each other and the walls of their container, leading to observable macroscopic properties such as pressure, temperature, and volume. The theory bridges the gap between microscopic molecular behavior and macroscopic gas laws (e.g., Boyle’s Law, Charles’s Law, and Avogadro’s Law). For NEET, focus on understanding the assumptions of the kinetic theory, as questions often test conceptual clarity rather than just formula application.

Assumptions of the Kinetic Theory

['1. **Molecular Size**: Gas molecules are point masses with negligible volume compared to the container’s volume. This assumption holds true for ideal gases but breaks down at high pressures or low temperatures.', '2. **Random Motion**: Molecules move in straight lines with constant velocities until they collide with other molecules or the container walls. The motion is random and follows Newton’s laws of motion.', '3. **Elastic Collisions**: Collisions between molecules and with the container walls are perfectly elastic, meaning kinetic energy is conserved. No energy is lost as heat or sound during collisions.', '4. **No Intermolecular Forces**: Ideal gases have no attractive or repulsive forces between molecules. This assumption simplifies calculations but is invalid for real gases, especially at low temperatures.', '5. **Average Kinetic Energy**: The average kinetic energy of gas molecules is directly proportional to the absolute temperature of the gas (in Kelvin). This is a cornerstone of the theory and links microscopic motion to macroscopic temperature.']

Pressure Exerted by a Gas

The pressure exerted by a gas on the walls of its container arises from the collisions of molecules with the walls. Using the kinetic theory, pressure (P) can be derived as: $$ P = \frac{1}{3} \frac{mN}{V} \overline{v^2} $$ where: - $ m $ = mass of one molecule, - $ N $ = total number of molecules, - $ V $ = volume of the container, - $ \overline{v^2} $ = mean square velocity of the molecules. This equation shows that pressure depends on the number of molecules, their mass, and their average kinetic energy. For NEET, memorize this formula and understand how changes in volume, temperature, or molecular mass affect pressure.

Kinetic Energy and Temperature

The average kinetic energy of a gas molecule is given by: $$ \overline{KE} = \frac{1}{2} m \overline{v^2} = \frac{3}{2} k_B T $$ where: - $ k_B $ = Boltzmann constant ($ 1.38 \times 10^{-23} \, \text{J/K} $), - $ T $ = absolute temperature (in Kelvin). This equation establishes a direct relationship between temperature and the average kinetic energy of molecules. For a gas at temperature $ T $, the root mean square (RMS) velocity of molecules is: $$ v_{rms} = \sqrt{\frac{3k_B T}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{3RT}{M}} $$ where $ R $ is the universal gas constant and $ M $ is the molar mass of the gas. NEET often tests the relationship between RMS velocity, temperature, and molecular mass.

Degrees of Freedom and Law of Equipartition of Energy

The law of equipartition of energy states that energy is equally distributed among all degrees of freedom of a molecule. For a gas molecule: - **Monatomic gases** (e.g., He, Ar): 3 translational degrees of freedom. Average energy per molecule = $ \frac{3}{2} k_B T $. - **Diatomic gases** (e.g., $ O_2 $, $ N_2 $): 3 translational + 2 rotational degrees of freedom (at room temperature). Average energy per molecule = $ \frac{5}{2} k_B T $. At high temperatures, vibrational modes contribute 2 additional degrees of freedom, making the total 7. - **Polyatomic gases** (e.g., $ CO_2 $, $ H_2O $): 3 translational + 3 rotational degrees of freedom. Average energy per molecule = $ 3 k_B T $. For NEET, focus on calculating molar specific heats ($ C_V $ and $ C_P $) using degrees of freedom. For example, for a diatomic gas: $ C_V = \frac{5}{2} R $ and $ C_P = \frac{7}{2} R $.

Maxwell’s Distribution of Molecular Speeds

Maxwell’s distribution describes the range of speeds of molecules in a gas at a given temperature. The distribution curve is asymmetric, with most molecules having speeds close to the most probable speed ($ v_{mp} $). The three key speeds are: 1. **Most Probable Speed ($ v_{mp} $)**: Speed possessed by the largest number of molecules. $$ v_{mp} = \sqrt{\frac{2k_B T}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{2RT}{M}} $$ 2. **Average Speed ($ \overline{v} $)**: Arithmetic mean of all molecular speeds. $$ \overline{v} = \sqrt{\frac{8k_B T}{\pi m}} = \sqrt{\frac{8RT}{\pi M}} $$ 3. **Root Mean Square Speed ($ v_{rms} $)**: Square root of the average of the squares of the speeds. $$ v_{rms} = \sqrt{\frac{3k_B T}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{3RT}{M}} $$ For NEET, remember the order: $ v_{mp} < \overline{v} < v_{rms} $. Questions often ask to compare these speeds or calculate them for different gases.

Real Gases and Deviations from Ideal Behavior

Real gases deviate from ideal behavior at high pressures and low temperatures due to: 1. **Finite Molecular Size**: Molecules occupy volume, reducing the effective space for movement. This is accounted for in the van der Waals equation: $$ \left( P + \frac{a n^2}{V^2} \right) (V - n b) = n R T $$ where $ a $ and $ b $ are van der Waals constants. 2. **Intermolecular Forces**: Attractive forces between molecules reduce pressure. The term $ \frac{a n^2}{V^2} $ corrects for this. For NEET, understand the conditions under which real gases behave ideally (low pressure, high temperature) and the significance of the van der Waals equation. Questions may ask about the effect of intermolecular forces on pressure or volume.

Mean Free Path and Collision Frequency

The mean free path ($ \lambda $) is the average distance a molecule travels between collisions. It is given by: $$ \lambda = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2} \pi d^2 n} $$ where: - $ d $ = molecular diameter, - $ n $ = number of molecules per unit volume. Collision frequency ($ Z $) is the number of collisions a molecule undergoes per second: $$ Z = \frac{\overline{v}}{\lambda} = \sqrt{2} \pi d^2 n \overline{v} $$ For NEET, focus on how mean free path depends on pressure and temperature. For example, at constant temperature, $ \lambda \propto \frac{1}{P} $, while at constant pressure, $ \lambda \propto T $.

Important facts

  • The kinetic theory assumes ideal gas behavior, which is a simplification of real gases.
  • Pressure exerted by a gas is due to molecular collisions with the container walls.
  • The average kinetic energy of a gas molecule is directly proportional to its absolute temperature.
  • For a monatomic ideal gas, the molar specific heat at constant volume ($ C_V $) is $ \frac{3}{2} R $, and at constant pressure ($ C_P $) is $ \frac{5}{2} R $.
  • The root mean square speed ($ v_{rms} $) of gas molecules is inversely proportional to the square root of their molar mass.
  • Real gases deviate from ideal behavior at high pressures and low temperatures due to intermolecular forces and finite molecular size.
  • The mean free path of a gas molecule decreases with increasing pressure and increases with increasing temperature.
  • Maxwell’s distribution of molecular speeds shows that most molecules have speeds close to the most probable speed, not the average or RMS speed.

Formulas and relationships

Pressure of an ideal gas: $$ P = \frac{1}{3} \frac{mN}{V} \overline{v^2} $$
Average kinetic energy of a gas molecule: $$ \overline{KE} = \frac{1}{2} m \overline{v^2} = \frac{3}{2} k_B T $$
Root mean square speed: $$ v_{rms} = \sqrt{\frac{3k_B T}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{3RT}{M}} $$
Most probable speed: $$ v_{mp} = \sqrt{\frac{2k_B T}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{2RT}{M}} $$
Average speed: $$ \overline{v} = \sqrt{\frac{8k_B T}{\pi m}} = \sqrt{\frac{8RT}{\pi M}} $$
Degrees of freedom and energy: $$ \text{Average energy per molecule} = \frac{f}{2} k_B T $$ where $ f $ = degrees of freedom.
Molar specific heats: $$ C_V = \frac{f}{2} R \quad \text{and} \quad C_P = C_V + R $$
Van der Waals equation for real gases: $$ \left( P + \frac{a n^2}{V^2} \right) (V - n b) = n R T $$
Mean free path: $$ \lambda = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2} \pi d^2 n} $$
Collision frequency: $$ Z = \sqrt{2} \pi d^2 n \overline{v} $$
Graham’s Law of diffusion: $$ \frac{v_1}{v_2} = \sqrt{\frac{M_2}{M_1}} $$

Common mistakes

  • Confusing RMS speed with average speed or most probable speed. Remember: $ v_{mp} < \overline{v} < v_{rms} $.
  • Using Celsius instead of Kelvin for temperature in kinetic theory formulas. Always convert to Kelvin.
  • Assuming all gases have the same degrees of freedom. Monatomic, diatomic, and polyatomic gases have different degrees of freedom, which affect their specific heats.
  • Ignoring the effect of temperature on degrees of freedom for diatomic gases. Vibrational modes are not excited at room temperature but contribute at high temperatures.
  • Forgetting that the van der Waals equation corrects for both intermolecular forces ($ a $ term) and molecular volume ($ b $ term).
  • Misapplying the relationship between pressure and mean free path. At constant temperature, $ \lambda \propto \frac{1}{P} $, but at constant pressure, $ \lambda \propto T $.
  • Assuming ideal gas behavior for all conditions. Real gases deviate from ideal behavior at high pressures and low temperatures.
  • Incorrectly calculating molar specific heats. For example, using $ C_V = \frac{3}{2} R $ for diatomic gases at room temperature (it should be $ \frac{5}{2} R $).
