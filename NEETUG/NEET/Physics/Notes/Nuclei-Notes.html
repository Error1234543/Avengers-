Nuclei - NEET Physics Notes

NEETEnglishNotes

1. Introduction to Nucleus

The nucleus is the central core of an atom, containing protons and neutrons, collectively called nucleons. It occupies a very small volume compared to the entire atom but accounts for nearly all the atomic mass. The radius of a nucleus is approximately given by R = R₀A^(1/3), where R₀ is about 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ m (1.2 femtometers) and A is the mass number. The density of nuclear matter is extraordinarily high, roughly 2.3 × 10¹⁷ kg/m³, and is nearly constant for all nuclei. This indicates that nucleons are tightly packed in the nucleus, leading to strong nuclear forces that overcome the electrostatic repulsion between protons.

2. Atomic Number, Mass Number, and Isotopes

The atomic number (Z) is the number of protons in the nucleus and defines the element. The mass number (A) is the total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus. Isotopes are nuclei of the same element with the same atomic number but different mass numbers due to varying numbers of neutrons. For example, carbon has isotopes like ¹²C, ¹³C, and ¹⁴C. Isotopes exhibit nearly identical chemical properties but differ in physical properties such as mass and nuclear stability. In nuclear physics, isotopes are often represented as ᴬZX, where X is the chemical symbol.

3. Nuclear Forces and Stability

Nuclear forces are the strong attractive forces that bind protons and neutrons together in the nucleus. These forces are short-range (effective up to ~2-3 femtometers) and are much stronger than electrostatic repulsion between protons. The stability of a nucleus depends on the neutron-to-proton ratio (n/p ratio). For lighter nuclei (Z ≤ 20), stability is achieved when the n/p ratio is approximately 1. For heavier nuclei, a higher n/p ratio (greater than 1) is required to counteract the increasing electrostatic repulsion. Nuclei with Z > 83 are inherently unstable and undergo radioactive decay. The binding energy per nucleon is a key indicator of nuclear stability; higher binding energy per nucleon corresponds to greater stability.

4. Mass Defect and Binding Energy

The mass defect is the difference between the mass of a nucleus and the sum of the masses of its individual nucleons. This mass defect (Δm) is converted into binding energy (E) according to Einstein’s mass-energy equivalence: E = Δm × c², where c is the speed of light. The binding energy is the energy required to disassemble a nucleus into its constituent protons and neutrons. The binding energy per nucleon is calculated as the total binding energy divided by the mass number (A). The binding energy per nucleon curve peaks around A = 56 (iron), indicating that nuclei around this mass number are the most stable. This concept explains why energy is released in nuclear fusion (for lighter nuclei) and nuclear fission (for heavier nuclei).

5. Radioactivity and Decay Laws

Radioactivity is the spontaneous emission of particles or electromagnetic radiation from an unstable nucleus. The three primary types of radioactive decay are: - **Alpha (α) decay**: Emission of an alpha particle (⁴₂He), reducing the atomic number by 2 and the mass number by 4. Example: ²³⁸₉₂U → ²³⁴₉₀Th + ⁴₂He. - **Beta (β⁻) decay**: Emission of a beta particle (electron, e⁻) and an antineutrino (ν̅), converting a neutron into a proton. The atomic number increases by 1, while the mass number remains unchanged. Example: ¹⁴₆C → ¹⁴₇N + e⁻ + ν̅. - **Gamma (γ) decay**: Emission of high-energy photons (gamma rays) from an excited nucleus, with no change in atomic or mass number. This often follows alpha or beta decay to allow the nucleus to reach a lower energy state. The decay process follows an exponential law: N = N₀e^(-λt), where N is the number of undecayed nuclei at time t, N₀ is the initial number of nuclei, and λ is the decay constant. The half-life (T₁/₂) is the time required for half of the radioactive nuclei to decay and is related to the decay constant by T₁/₂ = ln(2)/λ.

6. Nuclear Reactions and Q-Value

Nuclear reactions involve the interaction of a nucleus with another particle (e.g., neutron, proton, alpha particle) or another nucleus, resulting in the formation of new nuclei and particles. Nuclear reactions are represented as: X + a → Y + b, where X is the target nucleus, a is the incident particle, Y is the product nucleus, and b is the emitted particle. The Q-value of a nuclear reaction is the energy released or absorbed during the reaction and is calculated as: Q = (mₓ + mₐ - mᵧ - m_b)c², where mₓ, mₐ, mᵧ, and m_b are the masses of the respective particles. If Q > 0, the reaction is exothermic (energy is released); if Q < 0, the reaction is endothermic (energy is absorbed). Key nuclear reactions include: - **Fusion**: Combining lighter nuclei to form a heavier nucleus, releasing energy. Example: ²₁H + ³₁H → ⁴₂He + ¹₀n + 17.6 MeV. - **Fission**: Splitting a heavy nucleus into lighter nuclei, releasing energy. Example: ²³⁵₉₂U + ¹₀n → ¹⁴¹₅₆Ba + ⁹²₃₆Kr + 3¹₀n + 200 MeV.

7. Nuclear Fission and Fusion

Nuclear fission is the process where a heavy nucleus (e.g., uranium-235 or plutonium-239) splits into two smaller nuclei, along with the release of neutrons and a large amount of energy. Fission is typically induced by the absorption of a slow (thermal) neutron. The released neutrons can trigger further fission reactions, leading to a chain reaction. Controlled chain reactions are used in nuclear reactors, while uncontrolled chain reactions occur in atomic bombs. Nuclear fusion is the process where two light nuclei combine to form a heavier nucleus, releasing energy. Fusion reactions power stars, including the Sun, where hydrogen nuclei fuse to form helium. Fusion requires extremely high temperatures (millions of Kelvin) to overcome the electrostatic repulsion between nuclei. Example: The proton-proton chain in the Sun converts hydrogen into helium, releasing energy in the process. Fusion is the basis for hydrogen bombs and is being researched for controlled energy production in fusion reactors.

8. Applications of Nuclear Physics

Nuclear physics has numerous applications in medicine, energy, industry, and research: - **Medical Applications**: - **Radiotherapy**: Use of gamma rays (e.g., from cobalt-60) to treat cancer by destroying malignant cells. - **Diagnostic Imaging**: Techniques like PET (Positron Emission Tomography) and SPECT (Single Photon Emission Computed Tomography) use radioactive isotopes to create images of internal body structures. - **Radiopharmaceuticals**: Radioactive isotopes (e.g., iodine-131, technetium-99m) are used for both diagnosis and treatment of diseases. - **Energy Production**: Nuclear reactors use controlled fission reactions to generate electricity. Fusion research aims to develop a clean and abundant energy source. - **Industrial Applications**: - **Radiography**: Use of gamma rays to inspect welds and detect flaws in materials. - **Tracers**: Radioactive isotopes are used to track the flow of substances in industrial processes. - **Carbon Dating**: The decay of carbon-14 is used to determine the age of archaeological and geological samples.

9. Radiation Hazards and Safety

Radiation from radioactive materials can cause biological damage by ionizing atoms and molecules in living tissues. The hazards depend on the type of radiation (alpha, beta, gamma), its energy, and the duration of exposure. Alpha particles are highly ionizing but have low penetration power, while gamma rays are highly penetrating and can cause significant damage. Radiation exposure is measured in units like: - **Gray (Gy)**: Absorbed dose of radiation (1 Gy = 1 J/kg). - **Sievert (Sv)**: Equivalent dose, accounting for the biological effectiveness of different types of radiation (1 Sv = 1 Gy × radiation weighting factor). Safety measures include: - **Shielding**: Use of materials like lead or concrete to absorb radiation. - **Distance**: Increasing distance from the radiation source reduces exposure (inverse square law). - **Time**: Minimizing the duration of exposure. - **Containment**: Proper storage and handling of radioactive materials to prevent contamination.

Formulas and relationships

Nuclear radius: R = R₀A^(1/3), where R₀ ≈ 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ m.
Mass defect: Δm = (Zmₚ + Nmₙ) - M_nucleus, where mₚ = mass of proton, mₙ = mass of neutron, M_nucleus = mass of nucleus.
Binding energy: E = Δm × c².
Binding energy per nucleon: E/A = (Δm × c²)/A.
Radioactive decay law: N = N₀e^(-λt).
Half-life: T₁/₂ = ln(2)/λ.
Q-value of nuclear reaction: Q = (mₓ + mₐ - mᵧ - m_b)c².
Inverse square law for radiation intensity: I ∝ 1/r², where r is the distance from the source.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing atomic number (Z) with mass number (A); Z is the number of protons, while A is the total number of protons and neutrons.
  • Assuming all isotopes of an element are radioactive; many isotopes are stable and do not undergo decay.
  • Misapplying the binding energy formula by not accounting for the mass defect correctly; ensure Δm is calculated as (mass of nucleons) - (mass of nucleus).
  • Forgetting that alpha decay reduces both the atomic number and mass number, while beta decay only changes the atomic number.
  • Incorrectly calculating the Q-value of a nuclear reaction by mixing up the masses of reactants and products; Q = (mass of reactants - mass of products)c².
  • Assuming that all nuclei with Z > 83 are equally unstable; some heavy nuclei (e.g., uranium-238) have very long half-lives.
  • Overlooking the role of neutrons in nuclear stability; neutrons help counteract the electrostatic repulsion between protons.
  • Confusing nuclear fission and fusion; fission splits heavy nuclei, while fusion combines light nuclei.
  • Ignoring the units of radiation exposure; Gray (Gy) measures absorbed dose, while Sievert (Sv) accounts for biological effectiveness.
  • Assuming that gamma decay changes the atomic or mass number; gamma decay only releases energy from an excited nucleus.
