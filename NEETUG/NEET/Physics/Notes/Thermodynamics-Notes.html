Thermodynamics - NEET Physics Notes

NEETEnglishNotes

1. Introduction to Thermodynamics

Thermodynamics is the branch of physics that deals with the relationships between heat, work, temperature, and energy. It is governed by four fundamental laws: the Zeroth Law (thermal equilibrium), the First Law (conservation of energy), the Second Law (entropy and irreversibility), and the Third Law (absolute zero). For NEET, focus on the First and Second Laws, as they form the core of most numerical and conceptual questions. Thermodynamic systems are classified as open (exchange of matter and energy), closed (exchange of energy only), or isolated (no exchange). The state of a system is described by state variables such as pressure (P), volume (V), temperature (T), and internal energy (U).

2. First Law of Thermodynamics

The First Law of Thermodynamics states that energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred or converted from one form to another. Mathematically, it is expressed as: ΔU = Q - W, where ΔU is the change in internal energy of the system, Q is the heat added to the system, and W is the work done by the system. For NEET, remember the sign conventions: Q is positive if heat is added to the system, and W is positive if work is done by the system. This law is crucial for solving problems involving cyclic processes, isothermal processes, and adiabatic processes. Common applications include calculating work done in expansion or compression of gases and determining heat transfer in thermodynamic cycles.

3. Work Done in Thermodynamic Processes

Work done by a thermodynamic system depends on the path taken between initial and final states. For a quasi-static process (slow enough to maintain equilibrium), work done by the system is given by W = ∫ P dV, where P is pressure and dV is the infinitesimal change in volume. Key processes include: - **Isobaric Process (constant pressure)**: W = P ΔV. - **Isochoric Process (constant volume)**: W = 0 (no work done). - **Isothermal Process (constant temperature)**: W = nRT ln(V₂/V₁), where n is the number of moles, R is the gas constant, and T is temperature. - **Adiabatic Process (no heat transfer)**: W = (P₁V₁ - P₂V₂)/(γ - 1), where γ is the adiabatic index (Cp/Cv). For NEET, focus on calculating work done in these processes and interpreting P-V diagrams, where the area under the curve represents work done.

4. Heat Capacities and Specific Heats

Heat capacity (C) is the amount of heat required to raise the temperature of a system by 1°C or 1 K. It is given by C = Q/ΔT. For gases, two specific heat capacities are defined: - **Molar Heat Capacity at Constant Volume (Cv)**: Heat required to raise the temperature of 1 mole of gas by 1 K at constant volume. For an ideal gas, Cv = (f/2)R, where f is the degrees of freedom (3 for monatomic, 5 for diatomic, 6 for polyatomic). - **Molar Heat Capacity at Constant Pressure (Cp)**: Heat required to raise the temperature of 1 mole of gas by 1 K at constant pressure. For an ideal gas, Cp = Cv + R. The ratio of specific heats, γ = Cp/Cv, is critical for adiabatic processes. For NEET, memorize the values of Cv, Cp, and γ for monatomic (e.g., He, Ar) and diatomic (e.g., O₂, N₂) gases, as these are frequently tested.

5. Second Law of Thermodynamics and Entropy

The Second Law of Thermodynamics introduces the concept of entropy (S), a measure of the disorder or randomness of a system. It states that for any spontaneous process, the total entropy of the universe (system + surroundings) always increases. Mathematically, ΔS = ∫ dQ_rev/T, where dQ_rev is the infinitesimal heat transfer in a reversible process. Key points for NEET: - Entropy is a state function, meaning it depends only on the initial and final states, not the path. - For an isothermal reversible expansion of an ideal gas, ΔS = nR ln(V₂/V₁). - In an adiabatic reversible process, ΔS = 0 (isentropic process). - The Second Law also states that no heat engine can be 100% efficient (Kelvin-Planck statement) and that heat cannot spontaneously flow from a colder to a hotter body (Clausius statement).

6. Thermodynamic Processes and P-V Diagrams

Thermodynamic processes can be visualized using Pressure-Volume (P-V) diagrams, where the area under the curve represents work done. Key processes include: - **Isothermal Process**: Hyperbolic curve (P ∝ 1/V). Work done is given by W = nRT ln(V₂/V₁). - **Adiabatic Process**: Steeper curve than isothermal (P ∝ V^(-γ)). Work done is W = (P₁V₁ - P₂V₂)/(γ - 1). - **Isobaric Process**: Horizontal line (constant P). Work done is W = P ΔV. - **Isochoric Process**: Vertical line (constant V). Work done is W = 0. For NEET, practice interpreting P-V diagrams to determine work done, heat transferred, and changes in internal energy. Cyclic processes (e.g., Carnot cycle) are also important, where the net work done is the area enclosed by the cycle on the P-V diagram.

7. Carnot Engine and Efficiency

The Carnot engine is an idealized heat engine that operates on the Carnot cycle, consisting of two isothermal processes and two adiabatic processes. It is the most efficient heat engine possible, with efficiency (η) given by η = 1 - (T₂/T₁), where T₁ is the temperature of the hot reservoir and T₂ is the temperature of the cold reservoir. Key points for NEET: - Efficiency depends only on the temperatures of the reservoirs, not on the working substance. - No real engine can exceed the Carnot efficiency. - The Carnot cycle is reversible, meaning it can be run in reverse as a refrigerator or heat pump. - For a refrigerator, the coefficient of performance (COP) is given by COP = T₂/(T₁ - T₂).

8. Thermodynamic Potentials and Maxwell Relations

Thermodynamic potentials are state functions used to describe the state of a system. The four main potentials are: - **Internal Energy (U)**: ΔU = Q - W (First Law). - **Enthalpy (H)**: H = U + PV. For a process at constant pressure, ΔH = Qp. - **Helmholtz Free Energy (F)**: F = U - TS. For a process at constant temperature and volume, ΔF ≤ W. - **Gibbs Free Energy (G)**: G = H - TS. For a process at constant temperature and pressure, ΔG ≤ 0 for spontaneity. Maxwell relations are derived from these potentials and relate partial derivatives of thermodynamic variables. For NEET, focus on the definitions and applications of enthalpy and Gibbs free energy, particularly in chemical reactions and phase transitions.

9. Applications and Problem-Solving Strategies

To excel in NEET thermodynamics questions, follow these strategies: - **Identify the process**: Determine whether the process is isothermal, adiabatic, isobaric, or isochoric, as this dictates the applicable formulas. - **Use the First Law**: For any process, start with ΔU = Q - W and substitute the appropriate expressions for Q and W. - **Memorize key formulas**: Ensure you know the formulas for work done, heat capacities, and efficiency of Carnot engines. - **P-V diagrams**: Draw diagrams for cyclic processes to visualize work done and heat transfer. - **Sign conventions**: Be consistent with signs for Q and W (heat added to the system and work done by the system are positive). - **Units**: Always use SI units (Joules for energy, Pascals for pressure, cubic meters for volume) unless specified otherwise. Common pitfalls include mixing up Cp and Cv, misapplying the First Law, and confusing the signs of Q and W. Practice numerical problems to avoid these mistakes.

Important facts

  • The First Law of Thermodynamics is a statement of the conservation of energy: ΔU = Q - W.
  • Work done in an isothermal process is given by W = nRT ln(V₂/V₁).
  • For an adiabatic process, PV^γ = constant, where γ = Cp/Cv.
  • The efficiency of a Carnot engine is η = 1 - (T₂/T₁), and it is the maximum possible efficiency for any heat engine.
  • Entropy (S) is a measure of disorder and increases in spontaneous processes (Second Law).
  • For an ideal gas, Cp - Cv = R, where R is the universal gas constant.
  • In an isochoric process, no work is done (W = 0), so ΔU = Q.
  • The area under a P-V diagram represents the work done by the system.
  • Gibbs free energy (G = H - TS) determines the spontaneity of a process at constant temperature and pressure.
  • The Third Law of Thermodynamics states that the entropy of a perfect crystal approaches zero as temperature approaches absolute zero.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing the signs of Q and W in the First Law. Remember: Q is positive if heat is added to the system, and W is positive if work is done by the system.
  • Assuming work done is always P ΔV. This is only true for isobaric processes; for other processes, use the appropriate formula (e.g., W = nRT ln(V₂/V₁) for isothermal).
  • Mixing up Cp and Cv. Cp is always greater than Cv because it includes the work done during expansion at constant pressure.
  • Forgetting that in an isochoric process, no work is done (W = 0), so ΔU = Q.
  • Misapplying the adiabatic relation PV^γ = constant. This only holds for reversible adiabatic processes in ideal gases.
  • Assuming all processes are isothermal or adiabatic. Always check the conditions given in the problem.
  • Incorrectly calculating efficiency as η = (T₁ - T₂)/T₂. The correct formula is η = 1 - (T₂/T₁).
  • Ignoring the difference between heat capacity (C) and specific heat capacity (c). Heat capacity is extensive (depends on mass), while specific heat capacity is intensive (per unit mass).
  • Forgetting to convert temperature to Kelvin when using formulas involving T (e.g., ideal gas law, Carnot efficiency).
  • Assuming entropy always increases. While the total entropy of the universe increases, the entropy of a system can decrease if the surroundings gain more entropy.
