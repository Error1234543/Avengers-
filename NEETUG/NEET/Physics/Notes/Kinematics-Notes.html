1. Introduction to Kinematics

Kinematics is the branch of physics that studies the motion of objects without considering the forces causing the motion. It focuses on parameters like displacement, velocity, acceleration, and time. In NEET, kinematics is a foundational topic, often accounting for 2-4 questions, typically involving numerical problems or conceptual understanding of motion in one or two dimensions. Key distinctions to remember: (1) **Scalar vs. Vector Quantities**: Scalars (e.g., distance, speed) have only magnitude, while vectors (e.g., displacement, velocity, acceleration) have both magnitude and direction. (2) **Frame of Reference**: Motion is always described relative to a reference point or frame (e.g., ground, moving train). For NEET, assume the ground as the default frame unless stated otherwise.

2. Key Definitions and Variables

Average velocity = Δx/Δt; instantaneous velocity is the derivative of displacement with respect to time. Units: m/s.', '**Speed**: A scalar quantity representing the rate of change of distance. Average speed = total distance/total time. Units: m/s.', '**Acceleration (a)**: A vector quantity representing the rate of change of velocity. Average acceleration = Δv/Δt; instantaneous acceleration is the derivative of velocity with respect to time. Units: m/s². Note: Deceleration is negative acceleration.', '**Uniform Motion**: Motion with constant velocity (zero acceleration).', '**Non-Uniform Motion**: Motion with changing velocity (non-zero acceleration).']

3. Equations of Motion for Uniformly Accelerated Motion

For motion with constant acceleration, the following three equations relate displacement (s), initial velocity (u), final velocity (v), acceleration (a), and time (t). These are derived from the definitions of velocity and acceleration and are essential for solving NEET kinematics problems: 1. **First Equation (Velocity-Time Relation)**: v = u + at - Derivation: Acceleration (a) = (v - u)/t → v = u + at. 2. **Second Equation (Displacement-Time Relation)**: s = ut + (1/2)at² - Derivation: Displacement = average velocity × time = [(u + v)/2] × t. Substitute v from the first equation: s = [(u + u + at)/2] × t = ut + (1/2)at². 3. **Third Equation (Velocity-Displacement Relation)**: v² = u² + 2as - Derivation: From v = u + at, express t = (v - u)/a. Substitute into s = ut + (1/2)at²: s = u(v - u)/a + (1/2)a[(v - u)/a]². Simplify to get v² = u² + 2as. **Conditions for Use**: These equations apply **only** when acceleration is constant. For variable acceleration, calculus (integration/differentiation) is required, which is beyond NEET scope.

4. Graphical Representation of Motion

Graphs are a powerful tool in kinematics to visualize and solve problems. NEET often tests interpretation of these graphs: 1. **Displacement-Time (s-t) Graph**: - Slope = velocity (v = Δs/Δt). - Straight line → uniform velocity; curved line → non-uniform velocity. - Horizontal line → object at rest (zero velocity). 2. **Velocity-Time (v-t) Graph**: - Slope = acceleration (a = Δv/Δt). - Area under the graph = displacement (s = ∫v dt). - Straight line → uniform acceleration; horizontal line → zero acceleration (uniform velocity). 3. **Acceleration-Time (a-t) Graph**: - Area under the graph = change in velocity (Δv = ∫a dt). - Horizontal line → constant acceleration. **Common Mistakes**: Confusing the slope of s-t graph (velocity) with the area under v-t graph (displacement). Always label axes and units to avoid errors.

5. Projectile Motion

Projectile motion is the motion of an object thrown into the air, subject to only the acceleration due to gravity (g = 9.8 m/s², downward). It is a two-dimensional motion with: 1. **Horizontal Motion**: Uniform velocity (no acceleration, aₓ = 0). - Horizontal velocity (vₓ) = u cosθ (where θ is the launch angle). - Horizontal displacement (x) = vₓ × t. 2. **Vertical Motion**: Uniformly accelerated motion (aᵧ = -g). - Vertical velocity (vᵧ) = u sinθ - gt. - Vertical displacement (y) = u sinθ × t - (1/2)gt². **Key Equations**: - Time of flight (T) = 2u sinθ / g. - Maximum height (H) = (u sinθ)² / (2g). - Horizontal range (R) = u² sin(2θ) / g. Note: Range is maximum at θ = 45°. **Assumptions**: Air resistance is neglected; g is constant. For NEET, always resolve motion into horizontal and vertical components and treat them independently.

6. Relative Motion

Relative motion describes the motion of an object with respect to another moving object. The relative velocity of object A with respect to object B is given by: **vₐᵦ = vₐ - vᵦ** - If two objects move in the same direction, subtract their velocities. - If they move in opposite directions, add their velocities. **Example**: A boat moving downstream in a river. The boat's velocity relative to the ground (vₐ) = boat's velocity relative to water (vₐᵦ) + water's velocity relative to ground (vᵦ). **NEET Tip**: For problems involving two moving objects (e.g., trains, boats), always define the reference frame clearly. Use vector addition/subtraction for relative velocity.

For inclined planes, align axes along and perpendicular to the plane.', '**Step 3: Resolve Vectors**: Break velocities or accelerations into components (e.g., vₓ = v cosθ, vᵧ = v sinθ).', '**Step 4: Apply Equations of Motion**: Use the three equations for uniformly accelerated motion, or relative motion formulas as needed. Ensure units are consistent (e.g., convert km/h to m/s).', '**Step 5: Solve for Unknowns**: Isolate the variable of interest. For projectile motion, solve horizontal and vertical motions separately.', '**Step 6: Check for Physical Reasonableness**: Ensure the answer makes sense (e.g., time cannot be negative; range cannot exceed theoretical maximum).']

Important facts

  • Displacement is a vector; distance is a scalar. Displacement can be zero even if distance is not (e.g., circular motion).
  • Average speed is not the magnitude of average velocity unless the object moves in a straight line without changing direction.
  • The three equations of motion apply only to uniformly accelerated motion (constant acceleration).
  • In projectile motion, the horizontal and vertical motions are independent. The time of flight depends only on the vertical motion.
  • The maximum range of a projectile is achieved at a launch angle of 45° (ignoring air resistance).
  • Relative velocity is calculated as vₐᵦ = vₐ - vᵦ. The order of subtraction matters (A with respect to B).
  • The slope of a displacement-time graph gives velocity; the slope of a velocity-time graph gives acceleration.
  • The area under a velocity-time graph gives displacement; the area under an acceleration-time graph gives change in velocity.

Common mistakes

  • Using the equations of motion for non-uniform acceleration (e.g., motion with air resistance).
  • Confusing scalar and vector quantities (e.g., using speed instead of velocity in vector equations).
  • Forgetting to resolve vectors into components for 2D motion (e.g., projectile motion).
  • Incorrectly calculating the area under a velocity-time graph (e.g., using the wrong shape for the area).
  • Misapplying sign conventions (e.g., taking downward acceleration as positive in projectile motion).
  • Assuming the path of a projectile is a parabola in all reference frames (it is only parabolic in the ground frame).
  • Ignoring the independence of horizontal and vertical motions in projectile motion.
  • Using the wrong formula for relative velocity (e.g., adding velocities when they are in the same direction).
