Moving Charges and Magnetism - NEET Physics Notes

NEETEnglishNotes

Introduction to Magnetic Force on Moving Charges

When a charged particle moves through a magnetic field, it experiences a magnetic force. This force is fundamental to understanding the behavior of charged particles in magnetic fields and is a key concept in electromagnetism. The magnetic force on a moving charge is given by the Lorentz force law: **F = q(v × B)**, where **F** is the magnetic force, **q** is the charge of the particle, **v** is the velocity of the particle, and **B** is the magnetic field. The direction of this force is perpendicular to both the velocity of the particle and the magnetic field, determined by the right-hand rule for positive charges and the left-hand rule for negative charges. This force does no work on the particle because it is always perpendicular to the velocity, meaning the kinetic energy of the particle remains constant, though its direction changes.

Lorentz Force and Its Applications

The Lorentz force is the combined force experienced by a charged particle due to electric and magnetic fields: **F = q(E + v × B)**, where **E** is the electric field. In the absence of an electric field, the force simplifies to the magnetic force. This principle is applied in devices like cyclotrons, mass spectrometers, and velocity selectors. For example, in a velocity selector, perpendicular electric and magnetic fields are used to allow only particles with a specific velocity to pass through undeflected. The condition for undeflected motion is **v = E/B**, where the electric force **qE** balances the magnetic force **qvB**.

Motion of Charged Particles in a Uniform Magnetic Field

When a charged particle enters a uniform magnetic field perpendicular to its velocity, it moves in a circular path due to the centripetal force provided by the magnetic force. The radius **r** of this circular path is derived from the balance between the magnetic force and the centripetal force: **qvB = mv²/r**, leading to **r = mv/qB**. The angular frequency of this motion, called the cyclotron frequency, is **ω = qB/m** and is independent of the particle's velocity. If the particle's velocity has a component parallel to the magnetic field, it moves in a helical path, with the radius of the helix determined by the perpendicular component of the velocity.

Biot-Savart Law and Magnetic Field Due to Current-Carrying Conductors

The Biot-Savart Law quantifies the magnetic field produced by a steady current. It states that the magnetic field **dB** at a point due to a small current element **Idl** is given by: **dB = (μ₀/4π) * (Idl × r̂)/r²**, where **μ₀** is the permeability of free space (4π × 10⁻⁷ T·m/A), **Idl** is the current element, **r** is the distance from the current element to the point, and **r̂** is the unit vector pointing from the current element to the point. This law is used to derive the magnetic field due to various current configurations, such as a straight wire, circular loop, and solenoid. For a long straight wire, the magnetic field at a distance **r** is **B = (μ₀I)/(2πr)**, and its direction is given by the right-hand thumb rule.

Ampere's Circuital Law and Its Applications

Ampere's Circuital Law relates the magnetic field to the current that produces it. It states that the line integral of the magnetic field around a closed loop is equal to **μ₀** times the total current passing through the loop: **∮B·dl = μ₀I_enc**. This law is particularly useful for calculating magnetic fields in situations with high symmetry, such as infinite straight wires, solenoids, and toroids. For example, inside a long solenoid with **n** turns per unit length carrying current **I**, the magnetic field is uniform and given by **B = μ₀nI**. Ampere's Law is analogous to Gauss's Law in electrostatics but is applied to magnetic fields.

Force on a Current-Carrying Conductor in a Magnetic Field

A current-carrying conductor placed in a magnetic field experiences a force due to the interaction between the magnetic field and the moving charges in the conductor. The force on a small segment **dl** of the conductor is given by **dF = I(dl × B)**, where **I** is the current and **B** is the magnetic field. For a straight conductor of length **L** carrying current **I** in a uniform magnetic field **B**, the force simplifies to **F = ILB sinθ**, where **θ** is the angle between the conductor and the magnetic field. The direction of the force is determined by Fleming's left-hand rule. This principle is applied in electric motors, where the force on current-carrying coils in a magnetic field produces rotational motion.

Torque on a Current Loop in a Magnetic Field

A current loop placed in a uniform magnetic field experiences a torque that tends to align the plane of the loop perpendicular to the magnetic field. The torque **τ** on a loop of area **A** carrying current **I** in a magnetic field **B** is given by **τ = NIAB sinθ**, where **N** is the number of turns in the loop and **θ** is the angle between the normal to the loop and the magnetic field. The torque is maximum when the plane of the loop is parallel to the magnetic field (**θ = 90°**) and zero when the plane is perpendicular (**θ = 0°**). This principle is the basis for the working of galvanometers and electric motors, where the torque on current-carrying coils is used to produce measurable or mechanical effects.

Magnetic Dipole Moment and Its Analogy with Electric Dipole

A current loop behaves like a magnetic dipole, characterized by its magnetic dipole moment **μ = NIA**, where **N** is the number of turns, **I** is the current, and **A** is the area vector of the loop. The direction of **μ** is perpendicular to the plane of the loop, given by the right-hand rule. The magnetic dipole moment is analogous to the electric dipole moment in electrostatics. In a uniform magnetic field, the potential energy of a magnetic dipole is **U = -μ·B = -μB cosθ**, where **θ** is the angle between **μ** and **B**. The torque on the dipole tends to align it with the magnetic field, minimizing its potential energy. This concept is crucial in understanding the behavior of bar magnets and current loops in magnetic fields.

Hall Effect and Its Applications

The Hall Effect is the development of a voltage difference (Hall voltage) across a conductor or semiconductor when a magnetic field is applied perpendicular to the current flow. This occurs due to the deflection of charge carriers by the magnetic force, leading to a separation of charges and an electric field **E_H** perpendicular to both the current and the magnetic field. The Hall voltage **V_H** is given by **V_H = (IB)/(ned)**, where **I** is the current, **B** is the magnetic field, **n** is the charge carrier density, **e** is the charge of the carrier, and **d** is the thickness of the conductor. The Hall Effect is used to determine the type (n-type or p-type) and density of charge carriers in semiconductors, as well as to measure magnetic fields in Hall probes.

Cyclotron and Its Working Principle

A cyclotron is a device used to accelerate charged particles to high energies using a combination of a static magnetic field and a rapidly varying electric field. It consists of two hollow semicircular electrodes (dees) placed in a uniform magnetic field perpendicular to their plane. An alternating voltage is applied between the dees, creating an electric field that accelerates the particles each time they cross the gap between the dees. The magnetic field causes the particles to move in a circular path, with the radius increasing as their velocity increases. The frequency of the alternating voltage is synchronized with the cyclotron frequency **ω = qB/m**, ensuring that the particles are accelerated each time they cross the gap. Cyclotrons are used in nuclear physics research and medical applications, such as producing radioisotopes for cancer treatment.

Important facts

  • The magnetic force on a moving charge is always perpendicular to both the velocity of the charge and the magnetic field, meaning it does no work on the charge.
  • The radius of the circular path of a charged particle in a uniform magnetic field is directly proportional to its momentum and inversely proportional to the magnetic field strength.
  • The Biot-Savart Law and Ampere's Circuital Law are fundamental tools for calculating magnetic fields due to current distributions.
  • A current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field experiences a force given by **F = ILB sinθ**, where **θ** is the angle between the conductor and the field.
  • The torque on a current loop in a magnetic field is maximum when the plane of the loop is parallel to the field and zero when it is perpendicular.
  • The Hall Effect is used to determine the sign and density of charge carriers in semiconductors and to measure magnetic fields.
  • The cyclotron frequency is independent of the velocity of the charged particle and depends only on the magnetic field strength and the charge-to-mass ratio of the particle.
  • The magnetic dipole moment of a current loop is a vector quantity whose direction is given by the right-hand rule and whose magnitude is **NIA**.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing the direction of the magnetic force for positive and negative charges. Remember to use the right-hand rule for positive charges and the left-hand rule for negative charges.
  • Assuming the magnetic force does work on a charged particle. The magnetic force is always perpendicular to the velocity, so it does no work and does not change the kinetic energy of the particle.
  • Misapplying the right-hand rule for the direction of the magnetic field around a current-carrying wire. The thumb points in the direction of the current, and the fingers curl in the direction of the magnetic field.
  • Forgetting that the angle **θ** in the formula **F = ILB sinθ** is the angle between the conductor and the magnetic field, not the angle between the current and the field.
  • Incorrectly calculating the radius of the circular path of a charged particle in a magnetic field by using the wrong velocity component. Only the component of velocity perpendicular to the magnetic field contributes to the circular motion.
  • Assuming that the magnetic field inside a solenoid is dependent on the radius of the solenoid. The field inside a long solenoid is uniform and depends only on the number of turns per unit length and the current.
  • Mixing up the formulas for the magnetic field due to a straight wire and a circular loop. The field due to a straight wire is **B = (μ₀I)/(2πr)**, while the field at the center of a circular loop is **B = (μ₀I)/(2R)**.
  • Neglecting the vector nature of the magnetic dipole moment. The direction of **μ** is perpendicular to the plane of the current loop and is determined by the right-hand rule.
