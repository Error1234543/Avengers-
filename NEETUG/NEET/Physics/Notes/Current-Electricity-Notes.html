Current Electricity - NEET Physics Notes

NEETEnglishNotes

1. Introduction to Electric Current

Electric current is the rate of flow of electric charge through a conductor. It is denoted by the symbol 'I' and measured in amperes (A), where 1 A = 1 C/s (coulomb per second). The direction of conventional current is taken as the direction of flow of positive charge, which is opposite to the direction of electron flow. Current can be classified into two types: direct current (DC), where the flow of charge is unidirectional, and alternating current (AC), where the flow of charge reverses direction periodically. For NEET, focus on DC circuits unless specified otherwise. Key points to remember: current is a scalar quantity, and its magnitude depends on the rate of flow of charge, not the speed of individual charge carriers.

2. Ohm's Law and Resistance

Ohm's Law states that the current (I) flowing through a conductor is directly proportional to the potential difference (V) applied across its ends, provided the physical conditions (temperature, pressure, etc.) remain constant. Mathematically, V = IR, where R is the resistance of the conductor, measured in ohms (Ω). Resistance is a property of the conductor that opposes the flow of current and depends on the material's resistivity (ρ), length (L), and cross-sectional area (A): R = ρL/A. Resistivity is a material-specific constant and varies with temperature. For metals, resistivity increases with temperature, while for semiconductors, it decreases. Common mistakes include confusing resistance with resistivity or misapplying Ohm's Law to non-ohmic devices like diodes or transistors.

3. Electrical Energy and Power

When current flows through a conductor, electrical energy is dissipated as heat due to the resistance of the conductor. The work done (W) by the electric field in moving a charge (Q) through a potential difference (V) is given by W = VQ. Since Q = It, the work done can also be written as W = VIt. Power (P) is the rate of doing work, so P = W/t = VI. Using Ohm's Law, power can also be expressed as P = I²R or P = V²/R. The SI unit of power is the watt (W), where 1 W = 1 J/s. For NEET, remember the practical units: 1 kilowatt-hour (kWh) = 3.6 × 10⁶ J. Common applications include calculating the power rating of appliances or the energy consumed over time.

4. Combination of Resistors

Resistors can be combined in series or parallel to achieve desired equivalent resistance. In a **series combination**, resistors are connected end-to-end, and the same current flows through each resistor. The equivalent resistance (R_eq) is the sum of individual resistances: R_eq = R₁ + R₂ + ... + Rₙ. The potential difference across each resistor is proportional to its resistance. In a **parallel combination**, resistors are connected across the same two points, and the potential difference across each resistor is the same. The equivalent resistance is given by 1/R_eq = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + ... + 1/Rₙ. For two resistors in parallel, R_eq = (R₁R₂)/(R₁ + R₂). Key points: series combinations increase total resistance, while parallel combinations decrease it. Common mistakes include misapplying the formulas or confusing the voltage/current distribution in series vs. parallel.

5. Kirchhoff's Laws

**Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL)**: The algebraic sum of currents entering a junction is equal to the algebraic sum of currents leaving the junction. This is based on the conservation of charge. Mathematically, ΣI_in = ΣI_out. **Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL)**: The algebraic sum of all potential differences around any closed loop is zero. This is based on the conservation of energy. Mathematically, ΣV = 0. To apply KVL, assign a direction to the loop (clockwise or counterclockwise) and follow these rules: (1) If you traverse a resistor in the direction of current, the potential drop is -IR; if opposite, it is +IR. (2) If you traverse a battery from negative to positive, the potential rise is +E; if from positive to negative, it is -E. Kirchhoff's laws are essential for analyzing complex circuits where Ohm's Law alone is insufficient.

6. Wheatstone Bridge and Meter Bridge

A **Wheatstone Bridge** is a circuit used to measure unknown resistances. It consists of four resistors (R₁, R₂, R₃, R₄) arranged in a diamond shape, with a galvanometer connected between the junctions of R₁-R₂ and R₃-R₄. The bridge is balanced when the galvanometer shows zero deflection, indicating no current flows through it. The condition for balance is R₁/R₂ = R₃/R₄. A **Meter Bridge** is a practical application of the Wheatstone Bridge, where a wire of uniform cross-section is used to determine unknown resistances. The balance condition is L₁/L₂ = R₁/R₂, where L₁ and L₂ are the lengths of the wire on either side of the null point. For NEET, focus on the balance condition and the principle of null deflection.

7. Potentiometer and Its Applications

A **potentiometer** is a device used to measure the electromotive force (emf) of a cell or to compare the emfs of two cells. It consists of a long wire of uniform cross-section and resistivity, with a steady current flowing through it. The potential gradient (k) along the wire is given by k = V/L, where V is the potential difference across the wire and L is its length. To measure the emf of a cell, the cell is connected in opposition to the potentiometer wire, and the balance length (l) is found where the galvanometer shows zero deflection. The emf (E) of the cell is then E = kl. The potentiometer is preferred over a voltmeter for measuring emf because it does not draw current from the cell, ensuring accurate measurement. Applications include comparing emfs of two cells and determining internal resistance of a cell.

8. Cells, EMF, and Internal Resistance

An **electrochemical cell** converts chemical energy into electrical energy. The electromotive force (emf, E) of a cell is the maximum potential difference between its terminals when no current is drawn. The **terminal voltage (V)** of a cell is the potential difference across its terminals when current is drawn, and it is less than the emf due to the cell's internal resistance (r). The relationship is given by V = E - Ir, where I is the current. For cells in series, the total emf is the sum of individual emfs, and the total internal resistance is the sum of individual internal resistances. For cells in parallel, the total emf is the same as the emf of one cell (if identical), and the total internal resistance decreases. Key points: emf is a property of the cell, while terminal voltage depends on the circuit conditions.

9. Drift Velocity and Mobility

Drift velocity (v_d) is the average velocity acquired by charge carriers (electrons) in a conductor under the influence of an electric field. It is given by v_d = I/(nAe), where I is the current, n is the number of charge carriers per unit volume, A is the cross-sectional area, and e is the charge of an electron. The **mobility (μ)** of a charge carrier is defined as the drift velocity per unit electric field: μ = v_d/E. Mobility depends on the material and temperature. The current in a conductor can also be expressed in terms of drift velocity: I = nAev_d. Key points: drift velocity is very small (on the order of mm/s) compared to the random thermal velocity of electrons, and it is directly proportional to the electric field.

10. Thermoelectric Effects

Thermoelectric effects describe the direct conversion between thermal and electrical energy. The **Seebeck Effect** occurs when a temperature difference between two dissimilar conductors produces a voltage difference. The **Peltier Effect** is the reverse: an electric current through a junction of two dissimilar conductors causes heating or cooling. The **Thomson Effect** describes the heating or cooling of a single conductor when a current flows through it in the presence of a temperature gradient. For NEET, focus on the Seebeck Effect and the concept of thermoelectric emf, which is used in thermocouples to measure temperature. The thermoelectric emf (E) is given by E = αΔT, where α is the Seebeck coefficient and ΔT is the temperature difference.

Important facts

  • Electric current is a scalar quantity, while current density is a vector quantity.
  • Ohm's Law is not universally applicable; it holds only for ohmic conductors (e.g., metals) under constant physical conditions.
  • The resistance of a conductor increases with temperature for metals but decreases for semiconductors.
  • In a series combination of resistors, the current is the same through all resistors, but the voltage divides.
  • In a parallel combination of resistors, the voltage is the same across all resistors, but the current divides.
  • Kirchhoff's laws are based on the conservation of charge (KCL) and energy (KVL).
  • A Wheatstone Bridge is balanced when the ratio of resistances in the adjacent arms is equal.
  • A potentiometer measures emf accurately because it does not draw current from the cell at the balance point.
  • The terminal voltage of a cell is always less than its emf when current is drawn due to internal resistance.
  • Drift velocity of electrons in a conductor is very small (typically mm/s) compared to their random thermal velocity.
  • The Seebeck Effect is the basis for thermocouples used in temperature measurement.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing current (I) with current density (J); current is scalar, while current density is vector.
  • Applying Ohm's Law to non-ohmic devices like diodes or transistors.
  • Assuming resistance is independent of temperature; it varies with temperature for most materials.
  • Misapplying the formulas for series and parallel combinations of resistors.
  • Forgetting to assign correct signs to potential differences in Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL).
  • Assuming the terminal voltage of a cell is equal to its emf; it is less due to internal resistance.
  • Confusing drift velocity with the speed of light or the speed of charge carriers' random motion.
  • Misinterpreting the balance condition in a Wheatstone Bridge or Meter Bridge.
  • Forgetting that a potentiometer measures emf accurately only when no current is drawn from the cell.
  • Incorrectly calculating power or energy in circuits; remember to use the correct formula based on given quantities.
