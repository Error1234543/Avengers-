NEET Physics: Oscillations - Complete Study Notes

NEETEnglishNotes

1. Introduction to Oscillatory Motion

Oscillatory motion refers to the repeated back-and-forth movement of an object about a fixed point, called the equilibrium position. This motion is periodic, meaning it repeats itself at regular intervals of time. Examples include the swinging of a pendulum, the vibration of a guitar string, and the oscillation of a mass attached to a spring. In NEET, oscillatory motion is primarily studied in the context of simple harmonic motion (SHM), which is a specific type of periodic motion where the restoring force is directly proportional to the displacement from the equilibrium position and acts in the opposite direction. Key characteristics of oscillatory motion include amplitude (maximum displacement), time period (time for one complete oscillation), frequency (number of oscillations per unit time), and phase (position of the particle in its cycle at a given time).

2. Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM): Definition and Conditions

Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM) is a type of oscillatory motion where the restoring force (F) acting on the particle is directly proportional to its displacement (x) from the equilibrium position and is directed toward the equilibrium position. Mathematically, this is expressed as F = -kx, where k is the force constant (or spring constant in the case of a spring-mass system). For SHM to occur, the following conditions must be satisfied: (1) The motion must be periodic and oscillatory. (2) The restoring force must be proportional to the displacement. (3) The force must always act opposite to the direction of displacement. SHM can be observed in systems like a mass-spring system, a simple pendulum (for small angles), and a liquid column in a U-tube. It is important to note that SHM is an idealized motion and assumes no energy loss due to friction or other dissipative forces.

3. Differential Equation of SHM and Its Solution

The differential equation governing SHM is derived from Newton's second law of motion. For a particle of mass m undergoing SHM, the restoring force F = -kx leads to the equation: m(d²x/dt²) = -kx. Rearranging, we get the standard differential equation of SHM: d²x/dt² + (k/m)x = 0. This is a second-order linear differential equation with constant coefficients. The general solution to this equation is x(t) = A sin(ωt + φ) or x(t) = A cos(ωt + φ), where A is the amplitude, ω is the angular frequency, and φ is the phase constant. The angular frequency ω is related to the time period T and frequency f by the relations: ω = 2πf = 2π/T. The velocity and acceleration of the particle in SHM can be obtained by differentiating the displacement equation: v(t) = dx/dt = Aω cos(ωt + φ), and a(t) = d²x/dt² = -Aω² sin(ωt + φ).

4. Energy in Simple Harmonic Motion

In SHM, the total mechanical energy of the system remains constant if no dissipative forces (like friction) are present. The total energy is the sum of kinetic energy (KE) and potential energy (PE). The potential energy at any displacement x is given by PE = (1/2)kx², and the kinetic energy is KE = (1/2)mv². Since v = ω√(A² - x²), the kinetic energy can also be written as KE = (1/2)mω²(A² - x²). The total energy E is thus E = KE + PE = (1/2)mω²A² = (1/2)kA². This shows that the total energy is proportional to the square of the amplitude. At the equilibrium position (x = 0), the energy is entirely kinetic, and at the extreme positions (x = ±A), the energy is entirely potential. Energy variations in SHM are crucial for solving numerical problems in NEET, especially those involving energy conservation.

5. Time Period and Frequency of SHM

The time period (T) of SHM is the time taken to complete one full oscillation, while the frequency (f) is the number of oscillations per unit time. They are related by T = 1/f. For a mass-spring system, the time period is given by T = 2π√(m/k), where m is the mass and k is the spring constant. For a simple pendulum, the time period for small angular displacements is T = 2π√(L/g), where L is the length of the pendulum and g is the acceleration due to gravity. The angular frequency ω is related to the time period by ω = 2π/T. In NEET, questions often require calculating the time period or frequency for different systems, so it is essential to memorize these formulas and understand their derivations. Note that the time period of SHM is independent of the amplitude, which is a key characteristic of SHM.

6. Spring-Mass System and Pendulum

The spring-mass system and the simple pendulum are two classic examples of systems exhibiting SHM. In a spring-mass system, a mass m is attached to a spring of spring constant k. When displaced from its equilibrium position, the system oscillates with a time period T = 2π√(m/k). The restoring force is provided by the spring, and the motion is SHM as long as the spring obeys Hooke's law (F = -kx). In a simple pendulum, a point mass m is suspended by a massless string of length L. For small angular displacements (θ < 15°), the motion is approximately SHM with a time period T = 2π√(L/g). The restoring force is the component of gravitational force along the arc of motion. For NEET, it is important to understand the assumptions and limitations of these systems, such as the small-angle approximation for the pendulum and the ideal spring assumption for the spring-mass system.

7. Damped and Forced Oscillations

In real-world scenarios, oscillatory motion is often subject to damping forces, such as friction or air resistance, which cause the amplitude of oscillation to decrease over time. This is called damped oscillation. The damping force is typically proportional to the velocity of the particle and acts opposite to the direction of motion. The equation of motion for a damped oscillator is m(d²x/dt²) + b(dx/dt) + kx = 0, where b is the damping coefficient. Depending on the value of b, the system can be underdamped (oscillatory motion with decreasing amplitude), critically damped (returns to equilibrium in the shortest time without oscillating), or overdamped (returns to equilibrium slowly without oscillating). Forced oscillations occur when an external periodic force is applied to the system, leading to a steady-state oscillation at the frequency of the external force. Resonance occurs when the frequency of the external force matches the natural frequency of the system, resulting in a large amplitude of oscillation. While NEET primarily focuses on undamped SHM, understanding damped and forced oscillations is important for a comprehensive grasp of oscillatory motion.

8. Superposition of SHM and Lissajous Figures

When two or more simple harmonic motions are superposed, the resultant motion can be complex. If two SHMs act along the same line, the resultant displacement is the algebraic sum of the individual displacements. If the two SHMs have the same frequency, the resultant motion is also SHM with the same frequency but different amplitude and phase. If the frequencies are different, the resultant motion is not SHM but can be periodic if the frequencies are commensurate (i.e., their ratio is a rational number). Lissajous figures are patterns formed when two SHMs act along perpendicular directions. The shape of the Lissajous figure depends on the ratio of the frequencies and the phase difference between the two motions. These figures are useful in analyzing the relationship between two oscillatory motions and are occasionally tested in NEET, particularly in the context of wave superposition.

9. Common Mistakes and Conceptual Pitfalls

Students often make the following mistakes while studying oscillations for NEET: (1) Confusing angular frequency (ω) with frequency (f). Remember that ω = 2πf. (2) Assuming that the time period of a pendulum depends on the mass of the bob. The time period of a simple pendulum depends only on the length of the string and the acceleration due to gravity. (3) Forgetting that SHM is an idealized motion and that real-world oscillations are often damped. (4) Misapplying the formula for the time period of a spring-mass system by not accounting for the effective mass of the spring. (5) Incorrectly calculating the phase difference between two SHMs, especially when using sine and cosine functions interchangeably. (6) Overlooking the small-angle approximation for the simple pendulum, which is valid only for θ < 15°. (7) Confusing the energy expressions for SHM, particularly the relationship between kinetic and potential energy at different points in the oscillation.

10. NEET-Focused Problem-Solving Strategies

To excel in NEET questions on oscillations, follow these strategies: (1) Memorize the key formulas for time period, frequency, angular frequency, and energy in SHM. (2) Understand the derivations of these formulas to apply them in novel situations. (3) Practice numerical problems involving the spring-mass system and simple pendulum, as these are the most commonly tested systems. (4) Pay attention to units and dimensions, especially when dealing with angular frequency and phase constants. (5) For energy-based questions, always start by writing the total energy expression and then substitute the given values. (6) For problems involving superposition of SHM, break down the motion into components and analyze each component separately. (7) Review common misconceptions and pitfalls to avoid losing marks on conceptual questions. (8) Solve previous years' NEET questions on oscillations to familiarize yourself with the exam's question patterns and difficulty level.

Important facts

  • Simple Harmonic Motion (SHM) is defined by the restoring force F = -kx, where k is the force constant and x is the displacement from equilibrium.
  • The time period of a spring-mass system is T = 2π√(m/k), and for a simple pendulum, it is T = 2π√(L/g) for small angles.
  • The angular frequency ω is related to the time period T by ω = 2π/T and to the frequency f by ω = 2πf.
  • In SHM, the total mechanical energy is conserved and is given by E = (1/2)kA², where A is the amplitude.
  • The displacement in SHM can be expressed as x(t) = A sin(ωt + φ) or x(t) = A cos(ωt + φ), where φ is the phase constant.
  • The velocity and acceleration in SHM are given by v(t) = Aω cos(ωt + φ) and a(t) = -Aω² sin(ωt + φ), respectively.
  • At the equilibrium position, the kinetic energy is maximum, and the potential energy is zero. At the extreme positions, the potential energy is maximum, and the kinetic energy is zero.
  • Damped oscillations occur when dissipative forces like friction are present, leading to a decrease in amplitude over time.
  • Forced oscillations occur when an external periodic force drives the system, and resonance occurs when the driving frequency matches the natural frequency of the system.
  • Lissajous figures are formed by the superposition of two perpendicular SHMs and are used to analyze the relationship between their frequencies and phase differences.

Formulas and relationships

Restoring force in SHM: F = -kx
Angular frequency: ω = √(k/m) for spring-mass system, ω = √(g/L) for simple pendulum
Time period: T = 2π√(m/k) for spring-mass system, T = 2π√(L/g) for simple pendulum
Frequency: f = 1/T = ω/(2π)
Displacement in SHM: x(t) = A sin(ωt + φ) or x(t) = A cos(ωt + φ)
Velocity in SHM: v(t) = Aω cos(ωt + φ) = ω√(A² - x²)
Acceleration in SHM: a(t) = -Aω² sin(ωt + φ) = -ω²x
Potential energy in SHM: PE = (1/2)kx²
Kinetic energy in SHM: KE = (1/2)mv² = (1/2)mω²(A² - x²)
Total energy in SHM: E = (1/2)kA² = (1/2)mω²A²
Phase difference between two SHMs: Δφ = (ωΔt) or Δφ = (2πΔt)/T

Common mistakes

  • Confusing angular frequency (ω) with frequency (f). Remember ω = 2πf.
  • Assuming the time period of a pendulum depends on the mass of the bob. It depends only on the length of the string and g.
  • Forgetting the small-angle approximation for the simple pendulum, which is valid only for θ < 15°.
  • Incorrectly calculating the phase difference between two SHMs, especially when using sine and cosine functions.
  • Misapplying the formula for the time period of a spring-mass system by not accounting for the effective mass of the spring.
  • Overlooking the fact that the total energy in SHM is proportional to the square of the amplitude.
  • Assuming that the velocity and acceleration in SHM are always in phase. Velocity leads displacement by 90°, and acceleration is out of phase with displacement by 180°.
  • Forgetting that the time period of SHM is independent of the amplitude.
  • Confusing the energy expressions for SHM, particularly the relationship between kinetic and potential energy at different points in the oscillation.
  • Not recognizing that real-world oscillations are often damped, while SHM is an idealized motion.
