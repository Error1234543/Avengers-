Alternating Current - NEET Physics Notes

NEETEnglishNotes

1. Introduction to Alternating Current (AC)

Alternating Current (AC) is an electric current that periodically reverses direction, unlike Direct Current (DC), which flows in a single direction. AC is the form of electrical power delivered to businesses and residences, and it is the primary form of electrical energy used in power systems worldwide. The standard waveform of an AC power circuit is a sine wave, which can be mathematically represented as I(t) = I₀ sin(ωt), where I(t) is the instantaneous current, I₀ is the peak current, ω is the angular frequency (ω = 2πf, where f is the frequency), and t is time. In India, the standard frequency of AC supply is 50 Hz, meaning the current completes 50 cycles per second. AC is preferred for power transmission over long distances because it can be easily stepped up or down using transformers, reducing energy loss during transmission.

2. Key Parameters of AC

['**Peak Value (I₀ or V₀):** The maximum value of current or voltage in an AC cycle. For a sine wave, it is the amplitude of the wave.', '**Instantaneous Value (I(t) or V(t)):** The value of current or voltage at any given instant of time. It varies continuously with time.', '**Root Mean Square (RMS) Value (I_rms or V_rms):** The effective value of AC, which is equivalent to the DC value that would produce the same power dissipation in a resistive load. For a sinusoidal AC, I_rms = I₀ / √2 and V_rms = V₀ / √2. Most AC measuring instruments display RMS values.', '**Average Value (I_avg or V_avg):** The average value of AC over one half-cycle (since the average over a full cycle is zero). For a sine wave, I_avg = 2I₀ / π and V_avg = 2V₀ / π.', '**Frequency (f):** The number of cycles completed per second, measured in Hertz (Hz). In India, f = 50 Hz.', '**Angular Frequency (ω):** Related to frequency by ω = 2πf, measured in radians per second (rad/s).', '**Phase Difference (φ):** The difference in phase angle between two AC quantities (e.g., voltage and current). It determines whether the quantities are in phase, leading, or lagging.']

3. AC Circuit with Pure Resistor (R)

In a purely resistive AC circuit, the voltage and current are in phase with each other. This means the phase difference (φ) between them is zero. The instantaneous voltage V(t) and current I(t) can be written as: V(t) = V₀ sin(ωt) and I(t) = I₀ sin(ωt). The relationship between the RMS values of voltage and current is given by Ohm's Law: V_rms = I_rms * R. The power dissipated in the resistor is given by P = I_rms² * R = V_rms² / R. Since the voltage and current are in phase, the power is always positive, meaning energy is continuously dissipated as heat in the resistor.

4. AC Circuit with Pure Inductor (L)

In a purely inductive AC circuit, the current lags behind the voltage by a phase angle of 90° (π/2 radians). This is because the inductor opposes changes in current, causing a delay. The instantaneous voltage V(t) and current I(t) are given by: V(t) = V₀ sin(ωt) and I(t) = I₀ sin(ωt - π/2). The relationship between the RMS values is V_rms = I_rms * X_L, where X_L is the inductive reactance, defined as X_L = ωL = 2πfL. Inductive reactance increases with frequency, meaning inductors offer higher opposition to high-frequency AC. The power in a purely inductive circuit is zero over a full cycle because the energy stored in the magnetic field during one half-cycle is returned to the circuit during the next half-cycle.

5. AC Circuit with Pure Capacitor (C)

In a purely capacitive AC circuit, the current leads the voltage by a phase angle of 90° (π/2 radians). This is because the capacitor charges and discharges, causing the current to reach its peak before the voltage. The instantaneous voltage V(t) and current I(t) are given by: V(t) = V₀ sin(ωt) and I(t) = I₀ sin(ωt + π/2). The relationship between the RMS values is V_rms = I_rms * X_C, where X_C is the capacitive reactance, defined as X_C = 1 / (ωC) = 1 / (2πfC). Capacitive reactance decreases with frequency, meaning capacitors offer lower opposition to high-frequency AC. Like the inductive circuit, the power in a purely capacitive circuit is zero over a full cycle because the energy stored in the electric field during one half-cycle is returned to the circuit during the next half-cycle.

6. Series R-L-C Circuit and Impedance

In a series R-L-C AC circuit, the total opposition to the flow of current is called impedance (Z), which is a combination of resistance (R), inductive reactance (X_L), and capacitive reactance (X_C). The impedance is given by Z = √(R² + (X_L - X_C)²). The phase angle (φ) between the voltage and current is given by tan(φ) = (X_L - X_C) / R. If X_L > X_C, the circuit is inductive, and the current lags behind the voltage. If X_C > X_L, the circuit is capacitive, and the current leads the voltage. If X_L = X_C, the circuit is purely resistive, and the current and voltage are in phase (resonance condition). The resonant frequency (f₀) is given by f₀ = 1 / (2π√(LC)). At resonance, the impedance is minimum (Z = R), and the current is maximum.

7. Power in AC Circuits

['**Instantaneous Power (P(t)):** The power at any instant, given by P(t) = V(t) * I(t). For a series R-L-C circuit, P(t) = V₀ I₀ sin(ωt) sin(ωt - φ).', '**Average Power (P_avg):** The average power dissipated in the circuit over one full cycle. It is given by P_avg = V_rms * I_rms * cos(φ), where cos(φ) is the power factor. The average power is also called real power or active power and is measured in watts (W).', '**Power Factor (cos(φ)):** The ratio of real power to apparent power (V_rms * I_rms). It indicates the efficiency of the circuit in utilizing the supplied power. A power factor of 1 (φ = 0) means the circuit is purely resistive, while a power factor of 0 (φ = 90°) means the circuit is purely inductive or capacitive.', '**Apparent Power (S):** The product of RMS voltage and RMS current, given by S = V_rms * I_rms. It is measured in volt-amperes (VA).', '**Reactive Power (Q):** The power stored and released by inductors and capacitors, given by Q = V_rms * I_rms * sin(φ). It is measured in volt-amperes reactive (VAR). Reactive power does not perform any useful work but is necessary for the functioning of inductive and capacitive devices.']

8. Resonance in AC Circuits

Resonance occurs in an AC circuit when the inductive reactance (X_L) equals the capacitive reactance (X_C), i.e., X_L = X_C. At resonance, the impedance of the circuit is minimum (Z = R), and the current is maximum. The resonant frequency (f₀) is given by f₀ = 1 / (2π√(LC)). Resonance is a crucial concept in tuning circuits, such as radio receivers, where the circuit is tuned to resonate at a specific frequency to select a particular station. Key characteristics of resonance include: (1) The phase angle (φ) between voltage and current is zero, meaning the circuit behaves like a purely resistive circuit. (2) The power factor is 1, indicating maximum power transfer. (3) The current in the circuit is maximum, and the voltage across the inductor and capacitor can be much higher than the applied voltage (voltage magnification).

9. Transformers and AC Power Transmission

A transformer is a device used to step up or step down AC voltages. It consists of two coils, the primary and secondary, wound on a common magnetic core. The working principle of a transformer is based on Faraday's law of electromagnetic induction. The voltage ratio between the primary (V_p) and secondary (V_s) is equal to the turns ratio: V_s / V_p = N_s / N_p, where N_p and N_s are the number of turns in the primary and secondary coils, respectively. The current ratio is inversely proportional to the turns ratio: I_s / I_p = N_p / N_s. Transformers are highly efficient (typically >95%) and are used in power transmission to reduce energy loss. By stepping up the voltage at the generating station, the current is reduced, minimizing I²R losses during transmission. At the receiving end, the voltage is stepped down for safe distribution to consumers.

10. Common Mistakes and Exam Tips

['**Confusing RMS and Peak Values:** Remember that RMS values are used for power calculations and are related to peak values by V_rms = V₀ / √2 and I_rms = I₀ / √2. Most AC meters display RMS values.', '**Phase Relationships:** In a purely resistive circuit, voltage and current are in phase. In a purely inductive circuit, current lags voltage by 90°. In a purely capacitive circuit, current leads voltage by 90°. Do not mix these up.', '**Impedance Calculation:** For a series R-L-C circuit, impedance is Z = √(R² + (X_L - X_C)²), not Z = R + X_L + X_C. Inductive and capacitive reactances oppose each other.', '**Power Factor:** The power factor is cos(φ), where φ is the phase angle between voltage and current. It is not the same as the ratio of resistance to impedance (R/Z).', '**Resonance Condition:** At resonance, X_L = X_C, and the impedance is minimum (Z = R). The resonant frequency is f₀ = 1 / (2π√(LC)). Do not confuse this with the condition for maximum impedance (which occurs at very low or very high frequencies).', '**Transformer Efficiency:** Transformers are not 100% efficient. Energy losses occur due to copper losses (I²R losses in the windings), hysteresis losses (magnetic core), and eddy current losses. However, for ideal transformers, efficiency is assumed to be 100%.', '**Units and Formulas:** Always use consistent units (e.g., henry for inductance, farad for capacitance, ohm for resistance/reactance). Double-check formulas, especially those involving ω = 2πf.']

Important facts

  • AC reverses direction periodically, unlike DC, which flows in one direction.
  • The standard frequency of AC supply in India is 50 Hz.
  • RMS value of AC is the equivalent DC value that produces the same power dissipation in a resistor.
  • In a purely resistive AC circuit, voltage and current are in phase.
  • In a purely inductive AC circuit, current lags voltage by 90° (π/2 radians).
  • In a purely capacitive AC circuit, current leads voltage by 90° (π/2 radians).
  • Impedance (Z) in a series R-L-C circuit is given by Z = √(R² + (X_L - X_C)²).
  • At resonance in an R-L-C circuit, X_L = X_C, impedance is minimum (Z = R), and current is maximum.
  • The power factor (cos(φ)) indicates the efficiency of power utilization in an AC circuit.
  • Transformers step up or step down AC voltages based on the turns ratio (V_s / V_p = N_s / N_p).
  • High-voltage transmission reduces I²R losses in power lines.

Formulas and relationships

RMS current: I_rms = I₀ / √2
RMS voltage: V_rms = V₀ / √2

Average voltage (half-cycle): V_avg = 2V₀ / π

Capacitive reactance: X_C = 1 / (ωC) = 1 / (2πfC)

Resonant frequency: f₀ = 1 / (2π√(LC))

Power factor: cos(φ) = R / Z
Transformer voltage ratio: V_s / V_p = N_s / N_p
Transformer current ratio: I_s / I_p = N_p / N_s

Common mistakes

  • Using peak values instead of RMS values for power calculations.
  • Assuming voltage and current are always in phase in AC circuits.
  • Incorrectly calculating impedance as Z = R + X_L + X_C instead of Z = √(R² + (X_L - X_C)²).
  • Confusing the conditions for resonance (X_L = X_C) with other circuit behaviors.
  • Forgetting that power factor is cos(φ), not R/Z (though they are equal in purely resistive circuits).
  • Misapplying the transformer turns ratio (e.g., using V_s / V_p = N_p / N_s instead of N_s / N_p).
  • Ignoring the phase relationships in inductive and capacitive circuits, leading to incorrect power calculations.
  • Using incorrect units for reactance (e.g., using henry for capacitance or farad for inductance).
