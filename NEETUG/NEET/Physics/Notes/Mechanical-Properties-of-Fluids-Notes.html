Mechanical Properties of Fluids - NEET Physics Notes

NEETEnglishNotes

Introduction to Fluids

Fluids are substances that can flow and do not have a fixed shape. They include liquids and gases. Unlike solids, fluids cannot sustain a shear stress; they deform continuously under the application of such stress. The study of fluids at rest is called fluid statics, while the study of fluids in motion is called fluid dynamics. For NEET, the focus is primarily on fluid statics, covering concepts like pressure, buoyancy, and surface tension. Understanding the mechanical properties of fluids is crucial for solving problems related to fluid pressure, floating bodies, and capillary action.

Pressure in Fluids

Pressure is defined as the force exerted per unit area. In fluids, pressure at a point is the same in all directions due to the fluid's inability to sustain shear stress. The SI unit of pressure is the Pascal (Pa), where 1 Pa = 1 N/m². Pressure in a fluid increases with depth due to the weight of the fluid above. The pressure at a depth 'h' in a fluid of density 'ρ' under gravity 'g' is given by the formula: P = P₀ + ρgh, where P₀ is the atmospheric pressure at the surface. This relationship is fundamental for solving problems involving fluid columns, such as manometers and barometers.

Pascal's Law

Pascal's Law states that a change in pressure applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted undiminished to every portion of the fluid and to the walls of its container. This principle is the basis for hydraulic systems, such as hydraulic lifts and brakes. Mathematically, if a pressure ΔP is applied to a fluid in a closed system, the same pressure change is observed at all points in the fluid. For example, in a hydraulic press, a small force applied to a small-area piston is transmitted to a larger-area piston, resulting in a larger force. This is expressed as F₁/A₁ = F₂/A₂, where F₁ and F₂ are the forces, and A₁ and A₂ are the areas of the pistons.

Buoyancy and Archimedes' Principle

Buoyancy is the upward force exerted by a fluid on an immersed object. Archimedes' Principle states that the buoyant force on an object submerged in a fluid is equal to the weight of the fluid displaced by the object. This principle explains why objects float or sink. The buoyant force (F_b) is given by F_b = ρ_fluid * V_displaced * g, where ρ_fluid is the density of the fluid, V_displaced is the volume of fluid displaced, and g is the acceleration due to gravity. For an object to float, the buoyant force must equal the weight of the object. This concept is critical for solving problems involving floating bodies, such as ships and icebergs.

Density and Relative Density

Density (ρ) is defined as mass per unit volume and is a measure of how compact a substance is. The SI unit of density is kg/m³. Relative density (or specific gravity) is the ratio of the density of a substance to the density of water at 4°C. It is a dimensionless quantity. For example, the relative density of mercury is 13.6, meaning mercury is 13.6 times denser than water. Density plays a key role in determining whether an object will float or sink in a fluid. Objects with a density less than the fluid will float, while those with a higher density will sink.

Surface Tension

Surface tension is the property of a liquid surface to behave like a stretched elastic membrane. It arises due to the cohesive forces between liquid molecules. The surface tension (T) of a liquid is defined as the force per unit length acting perpendicular to an imaginary line drawn on the liquid surface. The SI unit of surface tension is N/m. Surface tension is responsible for phenomena such as the formation of droplets, capillary rise, and the ability of insects to walk on water. The excess pressure inside a liquid drop or bubble is given by ΔP = 2T/r for a drop and ΔP = 4T/r for a bubble, where r is the radius.

Capillarity

Capillarity is the rise or fall of a liquid in a narrow tube due to the forces of cohesion and adhesion. When the adhesive forces between the liquid and the tube's walls are stronger than the cohesive forces within the liquid, the liquid rises in the tube (e.g., water in a glass tube). Conversely, if cohesive forces dominate, the liquid falls (e.g., mercury in a glass tube). The height (h) to which a liquid rises or falls in a capillary tube is given by Jurin's Law: h = (2T cosθ)/(ρgr), where T is the surface tension, θ is the contact angle, ρ is the density of the liquid, g is the acceleration due to gravity, and r is the radius of the tube.

Viscosity

Viscosity is the measure of a fluid's resistance to flow. It arises due to the internal friction between layers of the fluid as they slide past one another. The coefficient of viscosity (η) is defined as the ratio of the shearing stress to the velocity gradient. The SI unit of viscosity is Pascal-second (Pa·s) or Poiseuille (Pl). For example, honey has a higher viscosity than water. Viscosity plays a crucial role in fluid dynamics, particularly in the study of laminar and turbulent flow. Stokes' Law describes the viscous drag force on a spherical object moving through a fluid: F = 6πηrv, where r is the radius of the sphere and v is its velocity.

Streamline and Turbulent Flow

Fluid flow can be classified as streamline (or laminar) or turbulent. In streamline flow, the fluid moves in parallel layers with no disruption between the layers. The velocity of the fluid at any point remains constant over time. In contrast, turbulent flow is characterized by chaotic changes in pressure and flow velocity. The Reynolds number (Re) is a dimensionless quantity used to predict the flow pattern in a fluid. It is given by Re = (ρvd)/η, where ρ is the density of the fluid, v is the velocity, d is the characteristic length (e.g., diameter of a pipe), and η is the viscosity. For Re < 2000, the flow is typically streamline, while for Re > 4000, the flow is turbulent.

Bernoulli's Principle

Bernoulli's Principle states that for an incompressible, non-viscous fluid in streamline flow, the sum of the pressure, kinetic energy per unit volume, and potential energy per unit volume remains constant along a streamline. Mathematically, it is expressed as P + (1/2)ρv² + ρgh = constant, where P is the pressure, ρ is the density, v is the velocity, g is the acceleration due to gravity, and h is the height. This principle explains phenomena such as the lift on an airplane wing and the operation of a Venturi meter. It is essential for solving problems involving fluid dynamics and energy conservation in fluids.

Important facts

  • Pressure in a fluid increases with depth and is given by P = P₀ + ρgh.
  • Pascal's Law is the principle behind hydraulic systems, where pressure applied to a fluid is transmitted undiminished.
  • Archimedes' Principle states that the buoyant force on an object is equal to the weight of the fluid displaced by the object.
  • An object floats if its density is less than the density of the fluid it is immersed in.
  • Surface tension is responsible for the spherical shape of liquid droplets and the rise of water in capillary tubes.
  • The height of liquid rise in a capillary tube is given by Jurin's Law: h = (2T cosθ)/(ρgr).
  • Viscosity is a measure of a fluid's resistance to flow and is described by Stokes' Law for spherical objects.
  • The Reynolds number determines whether fluid flow is streamline or turbulent.
  • Bernoulli's Principle explains the relationship between pressure, velocity, and elevation in fluid flow.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing gauge pressure with absolute pressure. Gauge pressure is the pressure relative to atmospheric pressure, while absolute pressure includes atmospheric pressure.
  • Assuming that the buoyant force depends on the density of the object rather than the density of the fluid displaced.
  • Forgetting that surface tension acts along the surface of a liquid and not perpendicular to it.
  • Misapplying Jurin's Law by not considering the contact angle (θ) between the liquid and the tube.
  • Ignoring the effect of viscosity in problems involving fluid flow, especially in small tubes or at low velocities.
  • Assuming that Bernoulli's Principle applies to all fluids, including viscous or compressible fluids, without considering its limitations.
  • Mixing up the formulas for excess pressure in a liquid drop (ΔP = 2T/r) and a liquid bubble (ΔP = 4T/r).
  • Overlooking the role of atmospheric pressure (P₀) in calculations involving fluid pressure at depth.
