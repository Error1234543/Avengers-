Definitions

  • {'term': 'Nucleus', 'definition': 'The central core of an atom, containing protons and neutrons, which constitutes nearly all the atomic mass. It is positively charged due to the presence of protons.'}
  • {'term': 'Atomic Number (Z)', 'definition': "The number of protons in the nucleus of an atom, which determines the element's identity and its position in the periodic table."}
  • {'term': 'Mass Number (A)', 'definition': 'The total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus of an atom. It is approximately equal to the atomic mass of the atom in atomic mass units (u).'}
  • {'term': 'Isotopes', 'definition': 'Atoms of the same element (same atomic number Z) but with different mass numbers (A) due to varying numbers of neutrons in the nucleus.'}
  • {'term': 'Isobars', 'definition': 'Atoms of different elements that have the same mass number (A) but different atomic numbers (Z).'}
  • {'term': 'Isotones', 'definition': 'Atoms of different elements that have the same number of neutrons but different atomic numbers (Z) and mass numbers (A).'}
  • {'term': 'Nuclear Force', 'definition': 'The strong attractive force that binds protons and neutrons together in the nucleus, overcoming the electrostatic repulsion between protons. It is short-range and charge-independent.'}
  • {'term': 'Radioactivity', 'definition': 'The spontaneous disintegration of an unstable nucleus, accompanied by the emission of alpha particles, beta particles, or gamma rays, leading to the formation of a more stable nucleus.'}
  • {'term': 'Half-Life (t₁/₂)', 'definition': 'The time required for half of the radioactive nuclei present in a sample to decay. It is a constant for a given radioactive isotope and is independent of the initial amount.'}
  • {'term': 'Binding Energy', 'definition': 'The energy required to disassemble a nucleus into its constituent protons and neutrons. It is a measure of the stability of the nucleus and is related to the mass defect.'}

Key concepts

  • {'concept': 'Composition of the Nucleus', 'content': 'The nucleus consists of protons (positively charged) and neutrons (neutral). The number of protons (Z) defines the element, while the total number of nucleons (A = Z + N, where N is the number of neutrons) defines the isotope. The size of the nucleus is of the order of 10⁻¹⁵ m (1 femtometer or 1 fermi), and its density is extremely high (~10¹⁷ kg/m³).'}
  • {'concept': 'Mass Defect and Binding Energy', 'content': "The mass of a nucleus is always less than the sum of the masses of its constituent protons and neutrons. This difference is called the mass defect (Δm). The binding energy (E) of the nucleus is given by Einstein's mass-energy equivalence: E = Δm × c², where c is the speed of light. Binding energy per nucleon is a key indicator of nuclear stability; higher values indicate greater stability. The binding energy curve peaks around iron (A ≈ 56), explaining why lighter nuclei undergo fusion and heavier nuclei undergo fission to achieve stability."}
  • {'concept': 'Radioactive Decay Law', 'content': 'Radioactive decay follows an exponential law: N = N₀ e⁻λt, where N is the number of undecayed nuclei at time t, N₀ is the initial number of nuclei, and λ is the decay constant. The half-life (t₁/₂) is related to the decay constant by t₁/₂ = ln(2)/λ. The activity (A) of a radioactive sample, measured in becquerels (Bq), is the rate of decay: A = λN. The average life (τ) of a radioactive nucleus is τ = 1/λ = t₁/₂ / ln(2).'}
  • {'concept': 'Types of Radioactive Decay', 'content': '1. **Alpha Decay (α-decay)**: Emission of an alpha particle (⁴₂He nucleus), reducing the atomic number by 2 and the mass number by 4. Example: ²³⁸₉₂U → ²³⁴₉₀Th + ⁴₂He. Alpha decay occurs in heavy nuclei (A > 200).\n2. **Beta Decay (β⁻-decay)**: Emission of a beta particle (electron, e⁻) and an antineutrino (ν̅), converting a neutron into a proton. The atomic number increases by 1, while the mass number remains unchanged. Example: ¹⁴₆C → ¹⁴₇N + e⁻ + ν̅.\n3. **Positron Emission (β⁺-decay)**: Emission of a positron (e⁺) and a neutrino (ν), converting a proton into a neutron. The atomic number decreases by 1, while the mass number remains unchanged. Example: ²²₁₁Na → ²²₁₀Ne + e⁺ + ν.\n4. **Electron Capture**: A nucleus captures an orbital electron, converting a proton into a neutron and emitting a neutrino. The atomic number decreases by 1, while the mass number remains unchanged. Example: ⁷₄Be + e⁻ → ⁷₃Li + ν.\n5. **Gamma Decay (γ-decay)**: Emission of high-energy gamma photons from an excited nucleus, typically following alpha or beta decay. The atomic and mass numbers remain unchanged.'}
  • {'concept': 'Nuclear Reactions', 'content': 'Nuclear reactions involve changes in the nucleus due to bombardment by particles (e.g., neutrons, protons, alpha particles) or photons. They are represented as: X + a → Y + b, where X is the target nucleus, a is the incident particle, Y is the product nucleus, and b is the emitted particle. Key types include:\n1. **Nuclear Fission**: A heavy nucleus (e.g., ²³⁵U) splits into two lighter nuclei upon neutron bombardment, releasing energy and additional neutrons. Example: ²³⁵₉₂U + ¹₀n → ¹⁴¹₅₆Ba + ⁹²₃₆Kr + 3¹₀n + energy.\n2. **Nuclear Fusion**: Two light nuclei combine to form a heavier nucleus, releasing energy. Example: ²₁H + ³₁H → ⁴₂He + ¹₀n + energy (occurs in stars).\nIn nuclear reactions, the total number of nucleons (A) and the total charge (Z) are conserved.'}
  • {'concept': 'Radioactive Equilibrium', 'content': 'In a decay chain, if the parent nucleus has a much longer half-life than the daughter nucleus, a state of secular equilibrium is reached where the activity of the daughter equals the activity of the parent. For transient equilibrium, the parent half-life is longer but not vastly so, and the daughter activity exceeds the parent activity before equilibrium is reached. These concepts are crucial in understanding decay series like the uranium or thorium series.'}
