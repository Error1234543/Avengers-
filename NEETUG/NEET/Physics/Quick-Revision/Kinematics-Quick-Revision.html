NEET Physics Quick Revision: Kinematics

NEETEnglishQuick Revision

Definitions

  • Kinematics: The branch of mechanics that describes the motion of objects without considering the forces causing the motion.
  • Displacement: The shortest straight-line distance between the initial and final positions of an object, including direction. It is a vector quantity.
  • Velocity: The rate of change of displacement with respect to time. It is a vector quantity, measured in meters per second (m/s).
  • Speed: The rate of change of distance with respect to time. It is a scalar quantity, measured in meters per second (m/s).
  • Acceleration: The rate of change of velocity with respect to time. It is a vector quantity, measured in meters per second squared (m/s²).
  • Uniform Motion: Motion in which an object covers equal distances in equal intervals of time, regardless of the duration of the interval.
  • Non-Uniform Motion: Motion in which an object covers unequal distances in equal intervals of time.
  • Projectile Motion: The motion of an object thrown or projected into the air, subject only to the acceleration due to gravity (ignoring air resistance).

Important facts

  • Displacement is a vector quantity, while distance is a scalar quantity. Displacement can be zero even if distance is not (e.g., a round trip).
  • Average velocity is the total displacement divided by total time, while average speed is the total distance divided by total time. Average speed is always greater than or equal to the magnitude of average velocity.
  • Acceleration can be positive (speeding up) or negative (slowing down, also called deceleration). Negative acceleration does not necessarily mean the object is moving backward.
  • In projectile motion, the horizontal and vertical motions are independent of each other. The horizontal motion has constant velocity, while the vertical motion has constant acceleration due to gravity.
  • The maximum range of a projectile is achieved when it is launched at an angle of 45° (ignoring air resistance). For angles θ and (90° - θ), the range is the same.
  • In circular motion, centripetal acceleration is always directed toward the center of the circle. It is not a new type of acceleration but a specific case of acceleration due to changing velocity direction.
  • The slope of a velocity-time graph gives the acceleration, and the area under the graph gives the displacement. For non-uniform acceleration, the area can be calculated using integration.

Exam tips

  • For numerical problems, always draw a diagram to visualize the scenario. Label all given quantities and identify what needs to be found.
  • Memorize the three equations of motion and the conditions under which they apply. NEET often tests your ability to choose the correct equation for a given problem.
  • In projectile motion, break the problem into horizontal and vertical components. Solve each component separately using the appropriate equations of motion.

Common traps

  • Confusing speed and velocity: Speed is a scalar (only magnitude), while velocity is a vector (magnitude and direction). An object can have constant speed but changing velocity (e.g., circular motion).
  • Assuming acceleration is always in the direction of motion: Acceleration is in the direction of the net force. If an object is slowing down, acceleration is opposite to the direction of motion.
  • Misapplying equations of motion: These equations are valid only for uniformly accelerated motion. For non-uniform acceleration, calculus (integration/differentiation) is required.
  • Ignoring vector components in projectile motion: Always resolve velocity into horizontal (u cosθ) and vertical (u sinθ) components. The horizontal component remains constant, while the vertical component changes due to gravity.
  • Forgetting that displacement can be zero: If an object returns to its starting point, displacement is zero, but distance traveled is not. This affects calculations of average velocity (which would be zero) but not average speed.
  • Assuming the maximum height and range formulas apply universally: These formulas assume the projectile lands at the same vertical level from which it was launched. For uneven ground, the equations must be adjusted.
  • Confusing centripetal and centrifugal forces: Centripetal force is the real force acting toward the center (e.g., tension in a string). Centrifugal force is a fictitious force that appears to act outward in a rotating reference frame.
