NEET Physics: Quick Revision - Work, Energy and Power

NEETEnglishQuick Revision

Definitions

  • Work (W): Work is done by a force when the point of application of the force moves in the direction of the force. Mathematically, W = F · s = F s cosθ, where F is the force, s is the displacement, and θ is the angle between the force and displacement. Unit: joule (J).
  • Energy: Energy is the capacity to do work. It exists in various forms such as kinetic energy, potential energy, thermal energy, etc. Unit: joule (J).
  • Kinetic Energy (KE): The energy possessed by a body due to its motion. KE = (1/2)mv², where m is the mass and v is the velocity of the body.
  • Potential Energy (PE): The energy possessed by a body due to its position or configuration. Gravitational potential energy near Earth's surface: PE = mgh, where m is mass, g is acceleration due to gravity, and h is height.
  • Power (P): The rate of doing work or the rate of transfer of energy. P = W/t, where W is work done and t is time. Unit: watt (W). Also, P = F · v for constant force and velocity.

Important facts

  • Work done by a force is zero if the displacement is perpendicular to the force (θ = 90°).
  • The work done by a variable force can be calculated using the area under the force-displacement graph.
  • Potential energy is a relative quantity and depends on the reference level chosen. Only changes in potential energy are physically meaningful.
  • In a conservative force field, the sum of kinetic and potential energy remains constant. This is the basis for solving problems involving pendulums, springs, and free-fall.
  • For a body moving in a circular path, the centripetal force does no work because it is perpendicular to the displacement at every point.
  • The work done by friction is always negative because the frictional force opposes the motion.
  • In an elastic collision between two bodies of equal mass, the bodies exchange their velocities after the collision.
  • The efficiency of a machine can never be 100% due to unavoidable energy losses like friction and heat.

Memory aids

  • Work = Force × Displacement × cosθ: Remember 'WFD cosθ' to recall the formula for work done by a constant force.
  • Kinetic Energy: 'Half m v squared' — think of a car (mass m) moving at speed v; its energy is proportional to the square of its speed.
  • Potential Energy: 'mgh' — think of 'mgh' as 'mass × gravity × height,' like lifting a mass to a height h.
  • Power: 'Work over Time' or 'Force × Velocity' — remember that power is how quickly work is done or how much force is applied at a certain speed.
  • Conservation of Energy: 'Energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transformed' — visualize a roller coaster converting potential energy to kinetic energy and back.

Exam tips

  • For numerical problems, always draw a free-body diagram to identify forces and displacements clearly.
  • When using the work-energy theorem, ensure you account for all forces acting on the body, including friction and normal forces.
  • For problems involving springs, remember that the potential energy stored in a spring is (1/2)kx², where k is the spring constant and x is the displacement from the equilibrium position.
  • In collision problems, always apply conservation of momentum first, then use the coefficient of restitution or conservation of kinetic energy (if elastic) to find final velocities.
  • For problems involving power, check if the force and velocity are constant. If not, use the average power formula P_avg = W_total / t_total.
  • When calculating efficiency, ensure you identify the useful output power and the total input power correctly. Do not confuse input and output.
  • In multi-step problems, track the energy transformations carefully. For example, in a pendulum, energy converts between kinetic and potential at different points in the swing.

Common traps

  • Confusing work done by a force with the force itself. Work is done only if there is displacement in the direction of the force.
  • Assuming all forces are conservative. Frictional and air resistance forces are non-conservative and dissipate energy as heat.
  • Forgetting to account for the angle θ in the work formula. Work is not simply F × s unless the force and displacement are in the same direction.
  • Misapplying the work-energy theorem. It relates net work to change in kinetic energy, not potential energy.
  • Assuming kinetic energy is conserved in all collisions. Kinetic energy is conserved only in elastic collisions.
  • Using the wrong reference level for potential energy. Always define the reference level clearly before calculating potential energy.
  • Ignoring the vector nature of velocity in collision problems. Directions matter when applying conservation of momentum and the coefficient of restitution.
  • Assuming power is the same as energy. Power is the rate of energy transfer, not the energy itself.
