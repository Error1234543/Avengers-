NEET Physics Quick Revision: Atoms

NEETEnglishQuick Revision

Definitions

  • Atom: The smallest unit of an element that retains its chemical properties, consisting of a nucleus (protons and neutrons) surrounded by electrons.
  • Bohr Model: A model of the atom proposed by Niels Bohr, where electrons revolve around the nucleus in fixed orbits (stationary states) without radiating energy. Energy is emitted or absorbed only when electrons transition between orbits.
  • Ground State: The lowest energy state of an atom, where electrons occupy the closest possible orbits to the nucleus.
  • Excited State: A higher energy state of an atom, where one or more electrons occupy orbits farther from the nucleus than in the ground state.
  • Ionization Energy: The minimum energy required to remove an electron from the ground state of an atom to infinity (i.e., to ionize the atom).
  • Rydberg Constant (R): A physical constant (R ≈ 1.097 × 10^7 m⁻¹) used in the Rydberg formula to describe the wavelengths of spectral lines in hydrogen-like atoms.
  • Spectral Lines: Discrete wavelengths of light emitted or absorbed by atoms during electronic transitions between energy levels.
  • Quantum Numbers: A set of values (principal, azimuthal, magnetic, and spin) that describe the state of an electron in an atom, including its energy, orbital shape, orientation, and spin.

Important facts

  • The radius of the first Bohr orbit (n=1) for hydrogen is 0.529 Å (Bohr radius, a₀).
  • The energy of the ground state (n=1) of hydrogen is -13.6 eV. The negative sign indicates the electron is bound to the nucleus.
  • The ionization energy of hydrogen is 13.6 eV, which is the energy required to move the electron from n=1 to n=∞.
  • The Balmer series is the only series of hydrogen spectral lines that lies in the visible region of the electromagnetic spectrum.
  • The Rydberg constant (R) is approximately 1.097 × 10^7 m⁻¹ and is used to calculate the wavelengths of spectral lines in hydrogen-like atoms.
  • Bohr’s model successfully explains the stability of the hydrogen atom by postulating that electrons do not radiate energy while in stationary orbits.
  • The quantum mechanical model of the atom introduces the concept of orbitals, which are regions of space where the probability of finding an electron is high (described by |ψ|²).
  • The Pauli Exclusion Principle states that no two electrons in an atom can have the same set of four quantum numbers, which explains the electronic configuration of multi-electron atoms.
