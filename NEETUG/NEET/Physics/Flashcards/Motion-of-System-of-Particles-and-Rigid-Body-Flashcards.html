NEET Physics: Motion of System of Particles and Rigid Body - Flashcards

NEETEnglishFlashcards

Card 1

Define the term 'Centre of Mass' (COM) for a system of particles.

The Centre of Mass (COM) of a system of particles is a point where the entire mass of the system can be considered to be concentrated for the purpose of analysing translational motion. For a system of n particles with masses m₁, m₂, ..., mₙ located at position vectors r₁, r₂, ..., rₙ, the position vector of the COM (R) is given by: R = (m₁r₁ + m₂r₂ + ... + mₙrₙ) / (m₁ + m₂ + ... + mₙ). The COM moves as if all external forces act at this point, and its motion is independent of internal forces between particles.

Card 2

What is the condition for the centre of mass of a system to remain at rest?

The centre of mass of a system remains at rest if the net external force acting on the system is zero. This is derived from Newton's second law for a system of particles: F_net = M * a_com, where F_net is the net external force, M is the total mass of the system, and a_com is the acceleration of the centre of mass. If F_net = 0, then a_com = 0, implying the COM remains at rest or moves with constant velocity.

Card 3

Derive the expression for the velocity of the centre of mass of a system of particles.

The velocity of the centre of mass (v_com) is the time derivative of its position vector (R). Starting from R = (Σ m_i r_i) / M, where M is the total mass, differentiating both sides with respect to time gives: v_com = dR/dt = (Σ m_i dr_i/dt) / M = (Σ m_i v_i) / M. Thus, v_com = (m₁v₁ + m₂v₂ + ... + mₙvₙ) / (m₁ + m₂ + ... + mₙ). This shows the velocity of the COM is the mass-weighted average of the velocities of all particles in the system.

Card 4

Explain the difference between centre of mass and centre of gravity.

The centre of mass (COM) is a point where the entire mass of a system can be considered concentrated for translational motion, independent of any gravitational field. The centre of gravity (COG) is the point where the resultant gravitational force (weight) acts on the system. In a uniform gravitational field (e.g., near Earth's surface), the COM and COG coincide. However, in a non-uniform gravitational field, they may differ because the COG depends on the variation of gravitational acceleration across the system.

Card 5

State the law of conservation of linear momentum for a system of particles. When is it applicable?

The law of conservation of linear momentum states that the total linear momentum of a system of particles remains constant if the net external force acting on the system is zero. Mathematically, if F_net = 0, then P_total = Σ m_i v_i = constant. This law is applicable in isolated systems (no external forces) or when the net external force is zero, such as during collisions or explosions where internal forces dominate.

Card 6

Define 'moment of inertia' (I) for a rigid body. What are its SI units?

The moment of inertia (I) of a rigid body about an axis is a measure of its rotational inertia, i.e., its resistance to rotational motion about that axis. For a point mass m at a perpendicular distance r from the axis, I = m r². For a system of particles or a continuous mass distribution, I = Σ m_i r_i² or I = ∫ r² dm, respectively. The SI unit of moment of inertia is kilogram-metre squared (kg·m²).

Card 7

State the parallel axis theorem and its significance.

The parallel axis theorem states that the moment of inertia (I) of a rigid body about any axis parallel to an axis passing through its centre of mass (I_com) is given by: I = I_com + M d², where M is the total mass of the body and d is the perpendicular distance between the two parallel axes. This theorem is significant because it allows calculation of the moment of inertia about any axis if the moment of inertia about a parallel axis through the COM is known, simplifying complex rotational dynamics problems.

Card 8

What is the perpendicular axis theorem? For which type of bodies is it applicable?

The perpendicular axis theorem states that for a planar (two-dimensional) rigid body, the moment of inertia about an axis perpendicular to the plane of the body (I_z) is equal to the sum of the moments of inertia about two mutually perpendicular axes (I_x and I_y) lying in the plane of the body and intersecting at the same point: I_z = I_x + I_y. This theorem is applicable only to planar (laminar) bodies, such as thin rods, discs, or plates, where the mass distribution lies entirely in a plane.

Card 9

Derive the expression for the moment of inertia of a thin uniform rod of mass M and length L about an axis perpendicular to the rod and passing through its centre.

Consider a thin uniform rod of mass M and length L. Let the axis be perpendicular to the rod and pass through its centre. Divide the rod into infinitesimal elements of mass dm and length dx at a distance x from the axis. The mass per unit length (λ) is M/L, so dm = λ dx = (M/L) dx. The moment of inertia (I) is: I = ∫ x² dm = ∫_{-L/2}^{L/2} x² (M/L) dx = (M/L) ∫_{-L/2}^{L/2} x² dx = (M/L) [x³/3]_{-L/2}^{L/2} = (M/L) [(L³/24) - (-L³/24)] = (M/L)(L³/12) = M L² / 12. Thus, I = M L² / 12.

Card 10

State the expression for the kinetic energy of a rotating rigid body. How is it analogous to translational kinetic energy?

The kinetic energy (K) of a rigid body rotating about a fixed axis with angular velocity ω is given by: K = (1/2) I ω², where I is the moment of inertia about the axis of rotation. This is analogous to the translational kinetic energy K = (1/2) m v², where m is mass and v is linear velocity. In rotational motion, moment of inertia (I) replaces mass (m), and angular velocity (ω) replaces linear velocity (v).

Card 11

Define 'torque' (τ) and state its relation with angular momentum (L).

Torque (τ) is the rotational equivalent of force and is defined as the cross product of the position vector (r) and the force (F): τ = r × F. Its magnitude is τ = r F sinθ, where θ is the angle between r and F. Torque is related to angular momentum (L) by the equation: τ_net = dL/dt, where τ_net is the net external torque acting on the system. This is the rotational analogue of Newton's second law (F_net = dp/dt).

Card 12

State the law of conservation of angular momentum. When is it applicable?

The law of conservation of angular momentum states that the total angular momentum (L) of a system remains constant if the net external torque (τ_net) acting on the system is zero. Mathematically, if τ_net = 0, then L = constant. This law is applicable in isolated systems (no external torques) or when the net external torque is zero, such as in planetary motion, spinning ice skaters, or collisions where internal torques dominate.

Card 13

Explain the concept of 'angular momentum' (L) for a point mass and a rigid body.

For a point mass m moving with velocity v at a position vector r relative to a point, the angular momentum (L) is given by the cross product: L = r × p = r × (m v), where p is the linear momentum. Its magnitude is L = m v r sinθ, where θ is the angle between r and v. For a rigid body rotating about a fixed axis with angular velocity ω, the angular momentum is L = I ω, where I is the moment of inertia about the axis. Angular momentum is a vector quantity, with direction given by the right-hand rule.

Card 14

What is the condition for rolling without slipping? Derive the relation between linear and angular velocity for such motion.

Rolling without slipping occurs when there is no relative motion (no slipping) at the point of contact between the rolling body and the surface. For a body of radius R rolling without slipping, the linear velocity (v) of the centre of mass and the angular velocity (ω) are related by: v = R ω. This is derived from the condition that the instantaneous velocity of the point of contact is zero: v - R ω = 0, hence v = R ω.
