Card 1

Define a semiconductor and classify it based on electrical conductivity.

A semiconductor is a material with electrical conductivity between that of conductors (metals) and insulators (non-metals). Its conductivity can be controlled by doping, temperature, or external fields. Semiconductors are classified as: 1. Intrinsic (pure, e.g., silicon, germanium) – conductivity due to thermally generated electron-hole pairs. 2. Extrinsic (doped) – conductivity enhanced by adding impurities (donors or acceptors). Extrinsic semiconductors are further divided into n-type (electron majority carriers) and p-type (hole majority carriers).

Card 2

Explain the concept of doping in semiconductors with an example.

Doping is the intentional addition of impurity atoms to a pure semiconductor to modify its electrical properties. - **n-type doping**: Adding pentavalent impurities (e.g., phosphorus, arsenic) to silicon. Four valence electrons form covalent bonds with silicon, while the fifth electron becomes a free electron, increasing conductivity. - **p-type doping**: Adding trivalent impurities (e.g., boron, aluminum) to silicon. Three valence electrons form covalent bonds, creating a 'hole' (electron deficiency) that acts as a positive charge carrier. Example: Adding phosphorus to silicon creates an n-type semiconductor.

Card 3

What is the difference between drift current and diffusion current in a semiconductor?

1. **Drift Current**: Caused by the movement of charge carriers (electrons/holes) under an external electric field. Magnitude depends on carrier mobility and field strength. - Formula: I_drift = n * q * μ * E * A (n = carrier concentration, q = charge, μ = mobility, E = electric field, A = cross-sectional area). 2. **Diffusion Current**: Caused by the movement of charge carriers from regions of high concentration to low concentration (due to concentration gradient). - Formula: I_diff = -q * D * (dN/dx) * A (D = diffusion coefficient, dN/dx = concentration gradient). Key difference: Drift current requires an electric field, while diffusion current arises from a concentration gradient.

Card 4

Describe the working principle of a p-n junction diode under forward and reverse bias.

**Forward Bias (p-side positive, n-side negative)**: - Reduces the potential barrier at the junction, allowing majority carriers (holes in p-side, electrons in n-side) to diffuse across the junction. - Current flows easily (low resistance). - Minority carrier injection occurs, leading to recombination and current flow. **Reverse Bias (p-side negative, n-side positive)**: - Increases the potential barrier, preventing majority carriers from crossing the junction. - Only a small reverse saturation current flows due to minority carriers (thermally generated). - High resistance; diode acts as an insulator. - If reverse voltage exceeds breakdown voltage, avalanche or Zener breakdown occurs, leading to a large reverse current.

Card 5

Derive the expression for the current through a p-n junction diode under forward bias.

The current through a p-n junction diode is given by the **Shockley diode equation**: I = I₀ * (e^(qV/kT) - 1) where: - I = diode current, - I₀ = reverse saturation current (due to minority carriers), - q = electron charge (1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C), - V = applied voltage (positive for forward bias), - k = Boltzmann constant (1.38 × 10⁻²³ J/K), - T = absolute temperature (K). **Derivation**: 1. Under forward bias, the potential barrier is reduced, allowing diffusion of majority carriers. 2. The excess carrier concentration at the junction edges leads to a diffusion current. 3. The exponential term (e^(qV/kT)) dominates for forward bias (V > 0), making the current increase exponentially with voltage. 4. For reverse bias (V < 0), the term e^(qV/kT) becomes negligible, and I ≈ -I₀ (reverse saturation current).

Card 6

What is the depletion region in a p-n junction? How does its width change with bias?

The **depletion region** is the space-charge region around the p-n junction where mobile charge carriers (electrons and holes) are depleted, leaving behind ionized donors (positive) and acceptors (negative). - **Formation**: When p-type and n-type semiconductors are joined, electrons diffuse from n-side to p-side, and holes diffuse from p-side to n-side. This creates a region of immobile ions (depletion region) and a built-in electric field. - **Width under bias**: - **Forward Bias**: Width decreases as the applied voltage reduces the potential barrier, allowing more carriers to cross the junction. - **Reverse Bias**: Width increases as the applied voltage enhances the potential barrier, pushing carriers away from the junction.

Card 7

Explain the working of a Zener diode and its applications.

**Working**: - A Zener diode is a heavily doped p-n junction designed to operate in the **reverse breakdown region** without damage. - Under reverse bias, when the voltage reaches the **Zener breakdown voltage (V_Z)**, the electric field is strong enough to pull electrons from their covalent bonds, creating a large reverse current. - The voltage across the diode remains nearly constant (V_Z) over a wide range of currents, making it useful for voltage regulation. **Applications**: 1. **Voltage Regulation**: Maintains a constant output voltage despite variations in input voltage or load current. 2. **Overvoltage Protection**: Clamps voltage spikes to protect sensitive circuits. 3. **Reference Voltage**: Provides a stable reference voltage in power supplies and measurement circuits. 4. **Waveform Clipping**: Used in clipping circuits to shape waveforms.

Card 8

Describe the construction and working of a bipolar junction transistor (BJT).

**Construction**: - A BJT is a three-layer semiconductor device with two p-n junctions. It has three regions: 1. **Emitter (E)**: Heavily doped, supplies majority carriers. 2. **Base (B)**: Thin and lightly doped, controls the flow of carriers. 3. **Collector (C)**: Moderately doped, collects majority carriers from the emitter. - Two types: NPN (current flows from collector to emitter) and PNP (current flows from emitter to collector). **Working (NPN example)**: 1. **Forward Bias (Base-Emitter Junction)**: When a small current (I_B) is applied to the base-emitter junction, electrons from the emitter diffuse into the base. 2. **Reverse Bias (Base-Collector Junction)**: The base-collector junction is reverse-biased, creating a strong electric field that sweeps most electrons from the base into the collector. 3. **Current Amplification**: A small base current (I_B) controls a larger collector current (I_C). The current gain (β) is given by β = I_C / I_B, typically ranging from 50 to 200.

Card 9

What is the difference between common-emitter, common-base, and common-collector configurations of a BJT?

The three configurations differ in which terminal is common to both input and output: 1. **Common-Emitter (CE)**: - **Input**: Base-Emitter. - **Output**: Collector-Emitter. - **Characteristics**: High current and voltage gain, moderate input/output impedance. - **Applications**: Amplifiers, switching circuits. 2. **Common-Base (CB)**: - **Input**: Emitter-Base. - **Output**: Collector-Base. - **Characteristics**: High voltage gain, low current gain (α = I_C / I_E < 1), low input impedance, high output impedance. - **Applications**: High-frequency amplifiers, current buffers. 3. **Common-Collector (CC) or Emitter Follower**: - **Input**: Base-Collector. - **Output**: Emitter-Collector. - **Characteristics**: High current gain, voltage gain ≈ 1, high input impedance, low output impedance. - **Applications**: Impedance matching, buffer circuits.
