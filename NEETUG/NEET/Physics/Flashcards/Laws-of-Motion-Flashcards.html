NEET Physics - Laws of Motion: Exam-Focused Flashcards

NEETEnglishFlashcards

Card 1

State Newton's First Law of Motion and explain its significance in terms of inertia.

Newton's First Law of Motion states that an object at rest stays at rest, and an object in motion continues in motion with the same speed and in the same direction unless acted upon by an unbalanced external force. This law is also known as the Law of Inertia. Inertia is the resistance of an object to any change in its state of motion, including changes to its speed or direction. The significance of this law lies in its introduction of the concept of inertia, which explains why objects do not spontaneously start or stop moving. It forms the foundation for understanding the behavior of objects under the influence of forces.

Card 2

Define force and its SI unit. How is force related to acceleration according to Newton's Second Law?

Force is a vector quantity that tends to produce an acceleration of a body in the direction of its application. The SI unit of force is the newton (N), where 1 N = 1 kg·m/s². According to Newton's Second Law of Motion, the force acting on an object is equal to the mass of the object multiplied by its acceleration: **F = m·a**. This law quantifies the effect of force on the motion of an object, showing that acceleration is directly proportional to the net force and inversely proportional to the mass of the object.

Card 3

A 5 kg block is pushed with a force of 20 N on a frictionless surface. Calculate the acceleration of the block.

Using Newton's Second Law, **F = m·a**, where F = 20 N and m = 5 kg. Rearranging the formula to solve for acceleration: **a = F/m = 20 N / 5 kg = 4 m/s²**. The acceleration of the block is **4 m/s²** in the direction of the applied force.

Card 4

Explain Newton's Third Law of Motion with an example. Why do the action and reaction forces not cancel each other out?

Newton's Third Law of Motion states that for every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction. This means that forces always occur in pairs: if object A exerts a force on object B, then object B simultaneously exerts a force of equal magnitude but in the opposite direction on object A. Example: When you push a wall with your hand (action), the wall pushes back on your hand with an equal and opposite force (reaction). The action and reaction forces do not cancel each other out because they act on **different objects**. In the example, the action force acts on the wall, while the reaction force acts on your hand.

Card 5

What is the normal force? How is it calculated for a block of mass *m* resting on a horizontal surface?

The normal force is the perpendicular force exerted by a surface on an object in contact with it. It balances the component of the object's weight perpendicular to the surface, preventing the object from falling through the surface. For a block of mass *m* resting on a horizontal surface, the normal force (**N**) is equal in magnitude to the weight of the block but acts in the opposite direction. Thus, **N = m·g**, where *g* is the acceleration due to gravity (approximately 9.8 m/s²).

Card 6

A 10 kg block is placed on an inclined plane with an angle of 30° to the horizontal. Calculate the component of the weight parallel to the plane and the normal force acting on the block. (Assume *g* = 9.8 m/s²)

The weight of the block (**W**) is **W = m·g = 10 kg × 9.8 m/s² = 98 N**. The component of the weight parallel to the inclined plane (**W_parallel**) is given by **W_parallel = W·sin(θ) = 98 N × sin(30°) = 98 N × 0.5 = 49 N**. The normal force (**N**) is the component of the weight perpendicular to the plane: **N = W·cos(θ) = 98 N × cos(30°) = 98 N × (√3/2) ≈ 84.87 N**. Thus, the parallel component is **49 N**, and the normal force is **84.87 N**.

Card 7

Define friction. What is the difference between static friction and kinetic friction?

Friction is the force that resists the relative motion or tendency of such motion of two surfaces in contact. It acts parallel to the surfaces and opposes the applied force. **Static friction** is the frictional force that prevents two surfaces from sliding past each other. It adjusts in magnitude to match the applied force up to a maximum value (**f_s(max) = μ_s·N**), where *μ_s* is the coefficient of static friction and *N* is the normal force. **Kinetic friction** (or dynamic friction) acts when the surfaces are in relative motion and is given by **f_k = μ_k·N**, where *μ_k* is the coefficient of kinetic friction. Static friction is generally greater than kinetic friction for the same pair of surfaces.

Card 8

A 2 kg block is placed on a horizontal surface with a coefficient of static friction *μ_s* = 0.4. What is the minimum force required to start moving the block? (Assume *g* = 9.8 m/s²)

The maximum static friction force is given by **f_s(max) = μ_s·N**, where *N* is the normal force. For a block on a horizontal surface, **N = m·g = 2 kg × 9.8 m/s² = 19.6 N**. Thus, **f_s(max) = 0.4 × 19.6 N = 7.84 N**. The minimum force required to start moving the block is equal to the maximum static friction force, which is **7.84 N**.

Card 9

Explain the concept of tension in a string. How does tension vary in a massless string passing over a frictionless pulley with two masses *m₁* and *m₂* attached to its ends?

Tension is the force transmitted through a string, rope, cable, or wire when it is pulled tight by forces acting from opposite ends. In a **massless string** (ideal case), the tension is the same throughout the string. For a frictionless pulley system with two masses *m₁* and *m₂* attached to the ends of the string, the tension in the string is uniform. If *m₁ > m₂*, the system accelerates with *m₁* moving downward and *m₂* moving upward. The tension (**T**) can be calculated using Newton's Second Law for each mass: **T - m₂·g = m₂·a** and **m₁·g - T = m₁·a**. Solving these equations gives the tension and acceleration of the system.

Card 10

Derive the expression for the acceleration of a system of two blocks connected by a massless string over a frictionless pulley, where *m₁* > *m₂*.

Consider two blocks of masses *m₁* and *m₂* (*m₁ > m₂*) connected by a massless string over a frictionless pulley. Let *a* be the acceleration of the system, and *T* be the tension in the string. For *m₁* (moving downward): **m₁·g - T = m₁·a** (Equation 1). For *m₂* (moving upward): **T - m₂·g = m₂·a** (Equation 2). Adding Equation 1 and Equation 2: **m₁·g - m₂·g = (m₁ + m₂)·a**. Solving for *a*: **a = (m₁ - m₂)·g / (m₁ + m₂)**. This is the acceleration of the system.

Card 11

What is the apparent weight of a person in an elevator accelerating upward with acceleration *a*? How does it differ from the actual weight?

The apparent weight of a person in an elevator is the normal force (**N**) exerted by the elevator floor on the person. When the elevator accelerates upward with acceleration *a*, the net force acting on the person is **N - m·g = m·a**, where *m* is the mass of the person and *g* is the acceleration due to gravity. Solving for *N*: **N = m·g + m·a = m(g + a)**. The apparent weight (**N**) is greater than the actual weight (**m·g**) by a factor of *m·a*. If the elevator accelerates downward, the apparent weight decreases: **N = m(g - a)**.
