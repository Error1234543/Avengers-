NEET Physics - Nuclei (Exam-Focused Flashcards)

NEETEnglishFlashcards

Card 1

Define the term 'nucleus' in the context of atomic structure.

The nucleus is the central, dense region of an atom that contains protons and neutrons (collectively called nucleons). It accounts for nearly all the mass of the atom but occupies a very small fraction of its volume. The nucleus is positively charged due to the presence of protons and is held together by the strong nuclear force, which overcomes the electrostatic repulsion between protons.

Card 2

What is the atomic mass number (A) and how is it related to the nucleus?

The atomic mass number (A) is the total number of protons and neutrons in the nucleus of an atom. It is given by A = Z + N, where Z is the atomic number (number of protons) and N is the neutron number. The atomic mass number determines the isotope of an element and is crucial for calculating nuclear binding energy and stability.

Card 3

Explain the concept of nuclear binding energy with an example.

Nuclear binding energy is the energy required to disassemble a nucleus into its individual protons and neutrons. It arises from the mass defect, where the mass of the nucleus is slightly less than the sum of the masses of its nucleons. This mass difference (Δm) is converted into energy using Einstein's equation E = Δm·c². For example, the binding energy of a helium-4 nucleus (2 protons + 2 neutrons) is approximately 28.3 MeV, which is why it is highly stable.

Card 4

What is the mass defect, and how is it calculated?

The mass defect is the difference between the mass of a nucleus and the sum of the masses of its individual nucleons. It is calculated as: Δm = (Z·mₚ + N·mₙ) - M, where Z is the number of protons, mₚ is the mass of a proton, N is the number of neutrons, mₙ is the mass of a neutron, and M is the actual mass of the nucleus. The mass defect is directly related to the nuclear binding energy via E = Δm·c².

Card 5

Describe the process of radioactive decay and list its three main types.

Radioactive decay is the spontaneous transformation of an unstable nucleus into a more stable one, accompanied by the emission of particles or energy. The three main types are: 1) Alpha decay: Emission of an alpha particle (²₄He nucleus), reducing the atomic number by 2 and mass number by 4. 2) Beta decay: Emission of a beta particle (electron or positron), converting a neutron into a proton or vice versa. 3) Gamma decay: Emission of high-energy gamma photons, often following alpha or beta decay to release excess energy without changing the nucleus's composition.

Card 6

Write the general equation for alpha decay and provide an example.

The general equation for alpha decay is: ᴬ_ZX → ᴬ⁻⁴_Z₋₂Y + ⁴₂He, where X is the parent nucleus, Y is the daughter nucleus, and ⁴₂He is the alpha particle. Example: Uranium-238 decays into Thorium-234 via alpha decay: ²³⁸₉₂U → ²³⁴₉₀Th + ⁴₂He.

Card 7

What is beta-minus (β⁻) decay? Provide its general equation and an example.

Beta-minus (β⁻) decay occurs when a neutron in the nucleus converts into a proton, emitting an electron (β⁻ particle) and an antineutrino. The general equation is: ᴬ_ZX → ᴬ_Z₊₁Y + e⁻ + ν̅. Example: Carbon-14 decays into Nitrogen-14: ¹⁴₆C → ¹⁴₇N + e⁻ + ν̅.

Card 8

Explain the concept of half-life in radioactive decay.

The half-life (t₁/₂) of a radioactive substance is the time required for half of the radioactive nuclei present in a sample to decay. It is a constant for each isotope and is related to the decay constant (λ) by the equation t₁/₂ = ln(2)/λ. The half-life is independent of the initial quantity of the substance and is used to determine the age of archaeological samples (e.g., carbon dating) or the stability of isotopes.

Card 9

Derive the relationship between the decay constant (λ) and half-life (t₁/₂).

The decay constant (λ) represents the probability of decay per unit time. The number of undecayed nuclei (N) at time t is given by N = N₀·e⁻λᵗ, where N₀ is the initial number of nuclei. At t = t₁/₂, N = N₀/2. Substituting: N₀/2 = N₀·e⁻λ·t₁/₂ → 1/2 = e⁻λ·t₁/₂ → ln(1/2) = -λ·t₁/₂ → t₁/₂ = ln(2)/λ.

Card 10

What is nuclear fission? Provide an example of a fission reaction.

Nuclear fission is the process in which a heavy nucleus splits into two smaller nuclei of roughly equal mass, accompanied by the release of energy and neutrons. Example: Uranium-235 absorbs a neutron and splits into Barium-141 and Krypton-92, releasing 3 neutrons and energy: ²³⁵₉₂U + ¹₀n → ¹⁴¹₅₆Ba + ⁹²₃₆Kr + 3¹₀n + Energy.

Card 11

Explain the principle of nuclear fusion with an example.

Nuclear fusion is the process in which two light nuclei combine to form a heavier nucleus, releasing a large amount of energy. This occurs at extremely high temperatures (e.g., in stars) where nuclei overcome electrostatic repulsion. Example: Fusion of deuterium and tritium to form helium and a neutron: ²₁H + ³₁H → ⁴₂He + ¹₀n + 17.6 MeV.

Card 12

What is the Q-value of a nuclear reaction? How is it calculated?

The Q-value of a nuclear reaction is the net energy released or absorbed during the reaction. It is calculated as: Q = (Mass of reactants - Mass of products) · c². If Q > 0, the reaction is exothermic (releases energy); if Q < 0, the reaction is endothermic (absorbs energy). For example, in alpha decay, Q = (M_parent - M_daughter - M_alpha) · c².

Card 13

Describe the concept of nuclear stability and the role of the neutron-to-proton ratio (N/Z).

Nuclear stability refers to the resistance of a nucleus to undergo radioactive decay. The neutron-to-proton ratio (N/Z) is a key factor in stability: 1) For lighter nuclei (Z ≤ 20), stability occurs when N/Z ≈ 1. 2) For heavier nuclei (Z > 20), stability requires N/Z > 1 due to increased electrostatic repulsion between protons. Nuclei with N/Z ratios outside the stability belt undergo decay to achieve stability.

Card 14

What is the significance of the binding energy per nucleon curve?

The binding energy per nucleon curve plots the average binding energy per nucleon against the mass number (A). It shows that: 1) Nuclei with A ≈ 56 (e.g., iron) have the highest binding energy per nucleon and are the most stable. 2) Lighter nuclei (A < 56) release energy via fusion. 3) Heavier nuclei (A > 56) release energy via fission. The curve explains why fusion and fission are energy-releasing processes.

Card 15

Explain the difference between nuclear force and electrostatic force in the nucleus.

Nuclear force (strong force) is the attractive force between nucleons (protons and neutrons) that holds the nucleus together. It is short-range (≈ 1-2 fm), charge-independent, and much stronger than the electrostatic force. Electrostatic force is the repulsive force between protons due to their positive charges. While the electrostatic force acts over larger distances, the nuclear force dominates at short ranges, ensuring nuclear stability.

Card 16

What is a nuclear reactor, and what are its main components?

A nuclear reactor is a device that initiates and controls a sustained nuclear fission chain reaction to generate energy. Its main components are: 1) Fuel: Typically uranium-235 or plutonium-239. 2) Moderator: Slows down neutrons (e.g., water, graphite). 3) Control rods: Absorb neutrons to regulate the reaction (e.g., cadmium, boron). 4) Coolant: Removes heat (e.g., water, liquid sodium). 5) Shielding: Protects against radiation (e.g., concrete, lead).
