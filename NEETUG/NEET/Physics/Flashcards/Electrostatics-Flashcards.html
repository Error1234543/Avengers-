Card 1

Define electric charge. What are its SI unit and fundamental properties?

Electric charge is a fundamental property of matter that causes it to experience a force when placed in an electromagnetic field. The SI unit of electric charge is the coulomb (C). Fundamental properties include: (1) Quantization: Charge exists in discrete packets (e.g., electron charge = -1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C). (2) Conservation: Total charge in an isolated system remains constant. (3) Additivity: Total charge is the algebraic sum of individual charges. (4) Two types: Positive and negative charges attract, while like charges repel.

Card 2

State Coulomb's Law for the force between two point charges. Include the formula, variables, and conditions for validity.

Coulomb's Law states that the electrostatic force (F) between two point charges (q₁ and q₂) separated by distance (r) is directly proportional to the product of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. Formula: F = k(q₁q₂)/r², where k = 1/(4πε₀) ≈ 9 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻² (ε₀ = permittivity of free space). Conditions: (1) Charges must be point charges or spherically symmetric. (2) Medium must be homogeneous and isotropic (e.g., vacuum or air). (3) Distance (r) must be much larger than the size of the charges.

Card 3

What is the principle of superposition in electrostatics? How is it applied to calculate the net force on a charge due to multiple charges?

The principle of superposition states that the net electrostatic force on a charge due to multiple other charges is the vector sum of the individual forces exerted by each charge separately. Application: (1) Calculate the force on the target charge due to each source charge using Coulomb's Law. (2) Resolve each force into its components (e.g., x and y axes). (3) Sum the components algebraically to find the net force components. (4) Combine components using the Pythagorean theorem to find the magnitude and direction of the net force.

Card 4

Define electric field. How is it related to the electrostatic force on a test charge? Include the formula and SI unit.

The electric field (E) at a point is defined as the electrostatic force (F) experienced per unit positive test charge (q₀) placed at that point. Formula: E = F/q₀. The SI unit is newton per coulomb (N C⁻¹) or volt per meter (V m⁻¹). The electric field is a vector quantity, with direction the same as the force on a positive test charge. For a point charge Q, E = kQ/r² (radially outward for positive Q, inward for negative Q).

Card 5

What are electric field lines? State their properties and sketch the field lines for (a) a positive point charge, (b) a negative point charge, and (c) an electric dipole.

Electric field lines are imaginary curves used to visualize the electric field. Properties: (1) Start on positive charges and end on negative charges (or extend to infinity). (2) Never intersect. (3) Density of lines ∝ field strength. (4) Tangent to the line at any point gives the direction of E. Sketches: (a) Positive point charge: Radial lines outward. (b) Negative point charge: Radial lines inward. (c) Electric dipole: Lines start on +Q, curve around, and end on -Q, with no lines crossing the perpendicular bisector.

Card 6

Define electric flux. State Gauss's Law and explain its significance in electrostatics.

Electric flux (Φ) through a surface is the dot product of the electric field (E) and the area vector (A): Φ = E·A = EA cosθ. Gauss's Law states that the total electric flux through a closed surface is equal to the net charge enclosed (Qₑₙc) divided by the permittivity of free space (ε₀): Φ = Qₑₙc/ε₀. Significance: (1) Simplifies calculation of electric fields for symmetric charge distributions (e.g., spherical, cylindrical, planar). (2) Relates field to charge distribution. (3) Applies to any closed surface, regardless of shape.

Card 7

Derive the expression for the electric field due to an infinitely long uniformly charged straight wire using Gauss's Law.

Steps: (1) Symmetry: Field is radial and uniform along the wire. (2) Gaussian surface: Choose a cylindrical surface of radius r and length L coaxial with the wire. (3) Flux calculation: Φ = E × (2πrL) (only curved surface contributes; ends contribute zero due to perpendicular E and A). (4) Charge enclosed: Qₑₙc = λL (λ = linear charge density). (5) Apply Gauss's Law: E × (2πrL) = λL/ε₀. (6) Solve for E: E = λ/(2πε₀r). Direction: Radially outward for positive λ.

Card 8

What is electric potential? How is it related to electric potential energy? Include the formula and SI unit.

Electric potential (V) at a point is the electric potential energy (U) per unit charge (q) placed at that point: V = U/q. The SI unit is volt (V), where 1 V = 1 J C⁻¹. Electric potential is a scalar quantity. For a point charge Q, V = kQ/r. The potential difference (ΔV) between two points is the work done per unit charge to move a test charge between them: ΔV = W/q.

Card 9

Derive the expression for the electric potential due to a point charge. How does it differ from the electric field?

Derivation: (1) Work done to bring a test charge q₀ from infinity to distance r from Q: W = ∫ F·dr = ∫ (kQq₀/r²) dr (limits: ∞ to r). (2) W = kQq₀ [ -1/r ]∞ʳ = kQq₀/r. (3) Potential V = W/q₀ = kQ/r. Differences from electric field: (1) Potential is scalar; field is vector. (2) Potential depends on 1/r; field depends on 1/r². (3) Potential is easier to superpose (algebraic sum) than field (vector sum).

Card 10

Define equipotential surface. What are its properties? Give examples for (a) a point charge and (b) a uniform electric field.

An equipotential surface is a surface where the electric potential is the same at every point. Properties: (1) No work is done to move a charge along an equipotential surface. (2) Electric field lines are perpendicular to equipotential surfaces. (3) Equipotential surfaces for different potentials never intersect. Examples: (a) Point charge: Concentric spherical surfaces. (b) Uniform electric field: Parallel planes perpendicular to the field lines.

Card 11

What is the relationship between electric field and electric potential? Derive the formula for the electric field in terms of potential.

The electric field is the negative gradient of the electric potential: E = -∇V. In one dimension: E = -dV/dr. Derivation: (1) Work done to move a charge q₀ by distance dr: dW = -q₀E dr (negative sign because work is done against the field). (2) Change in potential energy: dU = dW = -q₀E dr. (3) Change in potential: dV = dU/q₀ = -E dr. (4) Rearrange: E = -dV/dr. For three dimensions, E = - (∂V/∂x i + ∂V/∂y j + ∂V/∂z k).

Card 12

Define capacitance. Derive the expression for the capacitance of a parallel-plate capacitor. Include the formula and SI unit.

Capacitance (C) is the ability of a capacitor to store charge per unit potential difference: C = Q/V. The SI unit is farad (F), where 1 F = 1 C V⁻¹. Derivation for parallel-plate capacitor: (1) Electric field between plates: E = σ/ε₀ = Q/(ε₀A) (σ = surface charge density, A = plate area). (2) Potential difference: V = Ed = Qd/(ε₀A) (d = plate separation). (3) Capacitance: C = Q/V = ε₀A/d. The capacitance depends only on the geometry (A, d) and the dielectric (ε₀).
