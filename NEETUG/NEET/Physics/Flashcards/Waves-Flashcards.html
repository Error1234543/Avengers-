NEET Physics - Waves: Exam-Focused Flashcards

NEETEnglishFlashcards

Card 1

Define a wave and classify it based on the medium requirement.

A wave is a disturbance that transfers energy from one point to another without transferring matter. Based on the medium requirement, waves are classified into: 1. Mechanical waves: Require a material medium (e.g., sound waves, water waves). 2. Electromagnetic waves: Do not require a medium and can propagate through a vacuum (e.g., light waves, radio waves).

Card 2

What is the difference between transverse and longitudinal waves? Give one example of each.

Transverse waves: The particles of the medium vibrate perpendicular to the direction of wave propagation. Example: Light waves (electromagnetic waves). Longitudinal waves: The particles of the medium vibrate parallel to the direction of wave propagation. Example: Sound waves in air.

Card 3

Write the equation for a progressive harmonic wave traveling along the positive x-axis and explain each term.

Equation: y(x, t) = A sin(kx - ωt + φ) Where: - y(x, t): Displacement of the particle at position x and time t. - A: Amplitude (maximum displacement from equilibrium). - k: Angular wave number (k = 2π/λ, where λ is the wavelength). - ω: Angular frequency (ω = 2πf, where f is the frequency). - φ: Phase constant (initial phase angle). - x: Position along the wave. - t: Time.

Card 4

Derive the relationship between wave velocity (v), frequency (f), and wavelength (λ).

Wave velocity (v) is the distance traveled by the wave per unit time. Since one complete wave (wavelength λ) passes a point in one time period (T), we have: v = λ / T As frequency (f) is the reciprocal of the time period (f = 1/T), substituting gives: v = fλ This is the fundamental relationship between wave velocity, frequency, and wavelength.

Card 5

What is the principle of superposition of waves? How does it explain interference?

The principle of superposition states that when two or more waves meet at a point, the resultant displacement at that point is the algebraic sum of the displacements of the individual waves. This principle explains interference: 1. Constructive interference: Occurs when two waves in phase (crest meets crest or trough meets trough) superpose, resulting in a wave of larger amplitude. 2. Destructive interference: Occurs when two waves out of phase (crest meets trough) superpose, resulting in a wave of reduced or zero amplitude.

Card 6

Explain the formation of stationary waves and state the conditions for their formation.

Stationary (standing) waves are formed when two progressive waves of the same frequency, amplitude, and speed traveling in opposite directions superpose. Conditions for formation: 1. Two waves must have the same frequency, amplitude, and speed. 2. They must travel in opposite directions. 3. The medium must be bounded (e.g., a string fixed at both ends or a pipe with closed ends). Nodes (points of zero displacement) and antinodes (points of maximum displacement) are formed in stationary waves.

Card 7

What are the fundamental frequency and harmonics in a stretched string fixed at both ends?

For a stretched string fixed at both ends: - Fundamental frequency (first harmonic): The lowest frequency at which the string vibrates, producing a single loop. Frequency = v / (2L), where v is the wave velocity and L is the length of the string. - Harmonics: Integer multiples of the fundamental frequency. The nth harmonic has a frequency = n * (v / (2L)) and produces n loops (n antinodes).

Card 8

Describe the phenomenon of beats. How is the beat frequency calculated?

Beats occur when two sound waves of slightly different frequencies superpose, resulting in a periodic variation in amplitude (loudness). The beat frequency is the difference between the frequencies of the two waves: Beat frequency (f_b) = |f₁ - f₂| Where f₁ and f₂ are the frequencies of the two waves. Beats are used to tune musical instruments.

Card 9

What is the Doppler effect? Write the formula for the apparent frequency when the source and observer are moving.

The Doppler effect is the change in the observed frequency of a wave due to the relative motion of the source and the observer. The formula for the apparent frequency (f') when both the source and observer are moving is: f' = f * (v ± v₀) / (v ∓ vₛ) Where: - f: Actual frequency of the source. - v: Velocity of the wave in the medium. - v₀: Velocity of the observer (positive if moving toward the source, negative if moving away). - vₛ: Velocity of the source (positive if moving away from the observer, negative if moving toward).

Card 10

A sound wave travels from air into water. How do its frequency, wavelength, and velocity change?

When a sound wave travels from air into water: - Frequency (f): Remains unchanged because it is determined by the source. - Velocity (v): Increases because the speed of sound in water is higher than in air. - Wavelength (λ): Increases because λ = v / f, and v increases while f remains constant.

Card 11

What is the difference between the intensity and loudness of a sound wave?

Intensity: A physical quantity representing the power per unit area carried by a sound wave. It is measured in watts per square meter (W/m²) and is proportional to the square of the amplitude of the wave. Loudness: A physiological perception of the intensity of sound. It depends on the intensity of the sound wave and the sensitivity of the human ear. Loudness is measured in decibels (dB) and is a logarithmic scale.

Card 12

Explain the concept of resonance with an example.

Resonance occurs when a system is driven at its natural frequency, resulting in a large amplitude of vibration. Example: A tuning fork vibrating at its natural frequency can cause another identical tuning fork to vibrate with large amplitude if placed nearby. This happens because the second fork absorbs energy efficiently when the driving frequency matches its natural frequency.

Card 13

What is the difference between echo and reverberation?

Echo: A distinct repetition of sound caused by the reflection of sound waves from a distant surface. It occurs when the time gap between the original sound and the reflected sound is at least 0.1 seconds (distance of reflection ≥ 17 meters in air). Reverberation: The persistence of sound in a closed space due to multiple reflections from walls, ceiling, and floor. It occurs when the reflected sounds merge with the original sound, creating a prolonged effect.

Card 14

State the factors affecting the velocity of sound in a gas.

The velocity of sound in a gas depends on: 1. Temperature: Velocity increases with temperature (v ∝ √T, where T is the absolute temperature). 2. Nature of the gas: Velocity is inversely proportional to the square root of the molar mass of the gas (v ∝ 1/√M). 3. Humidity: Velocity increases with humidity because water vapor has a lower molar mass than dry air. 4. Density: For a given gas, velocity decreases with an increase in density (v ∝ 1/√ρ). Note: Velocity does not depend on pressure if the temperature is constant.

Card 15

What is the difference between phase velocity and group velocity?

Phase velocity (vₚ): The velocity at which the phase of a single frequency wave propagates. It is given by vₚ = ω / k, where ω is the angular frequency and k is the wave number. Group velocity (v_g): The velocity at which the envelope (or group) of waves propagates. It represents the velocity of energy or information transfer and is given by v_g = dω / dk. For non-dispersive media, vₚ = v_g, but in dispersive media, they differ.
