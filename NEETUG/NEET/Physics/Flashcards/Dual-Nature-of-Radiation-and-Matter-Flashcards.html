Card 1

What is the **photoelectric effect**? Define the key observations that classical wave theory failed to explain.

The photoelectric effect is the phenomenon where electrons are emitted from a metal surface when it is exposed to electromagnetic radiation of sufficient frequency. Key observations that classical wave theory could not explain: 1. **Threshold Frequency**: Emission occurs only if the incident radiation frequency exceeds a certain minimum value (threshold frequency, ν₀), regardless of intensity. 2. **Instantaneous Emission**: Electrons are emitted almost instantly, even at low light intensities, contradicting the wave theory's prediction of a time delay. 3. **Kinetic Energy Independence from Intensity**: The maximum kinetic energy of emitted electrons depends on the frequency of the incident radiation, not its intensity. 4. **Intensity-Dependent Electron Count**: The number of emitted electrons (photoelectric current) is proportional to the intensity of the incident radiation, but their kinetic energy is not.

Card 2

State **Einstein’s photoelectric equation** and explain each term. What is its significance?

Einstein’s photoelectric equation: **Kₘₐₓ = hν - φ** - **Kₘₐₓ**: Maximum kinetic energy of the emitted photoelectrons (in joules or eV). - **h**: Planck’s constant (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J s). - **ν**: Frequency of the incident radiation (in Hz). - **φ**: Work function of the metal (minimum energy required to remove an electron from the metal surface, in joules or eV). **Significance**: - Explains the photoelectric effect by treating light as discrete packets of energy (photons) rather than continuous waves. - Resolves the failures of classical wave theory by showing that electron emission depends on photon energy (hν), not wave intensity. - Introduces the concept of **quantization of energy**, a cornerstone of quantum mechanics.

Card 3

Define **work function (φ)** and **threshold frequency (ν₀)**. How are they related?

1. **Work Function (φ)**: The minimum energy required to remove an electron from the surface of a metal. It is a characteristic property of the metal and is typically expressed in electron volts (eV). 2. **Threshold Frequency (ν₀)**: The minimum frequency of incident radiation required to cause photoelectric emission from a metal surface. Below this frequency, no electrons are emitted, regardless of intensity. **Relationship**: - The work function and threshold frequency are related by the equation: **φ = hν₀** where **h** is Planck’s constant. - For photoelectric emission to occur, the energy of the incident photon (hν) must be at least equal to the work function (hν ≥ φ).

Card 4

What is the **de Broglie wavelength**? Derive its formula and state its significance.

**De Broglie Wavelength (λ)**: The wavelength associated with a moving particle, proposed by Louis de Broglie in 1924. It unifies the wave-particle duality of matter. **Derivation**: - From Einstein’s mass-energy equivalence: **E = mc²**. - For a photon, energy is also given by: **E = hν = hc/λ**. - Equating the two: **mc² = hc/λ ⇒ λ = h/mc**. - For a particle moving with velocity **v**, replace **c** with **v**: **λ = h/mv = h/p**, where **p** is the momentum of the particle. **Significance**: - Shows that all matter (not just light) exhibits wave-like properties. - Explains the quantization of electron orbits in Bohr’s atomic model (standing wave condition). - Forms the basis for electron microscopy and other particle-wave experiments (e.g., Davisson-Germer experiment).

Card 5

Calculate the de Broglie wavelength of an electron accelerated through a potential difference of 100 V. (Given: mass of electron = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg, charge of electron = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J s).

**Step 1**: Calculate the kinetic energy (K) of the electron. - K = eV = (1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C) × (100 V) = 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁷ J. **Step 2**: Relate kinetic energy to momentum (p). - For non-relativistic speeds: **K = p²/2m ⇒ p = √(2mK)**. - p = √(2 × 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg × 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁷ J) = √(2.915 × 10⁻⁴⁷) ≈ 5.4 × 10⁻²⁴ kg m/s. **Step 3**: Calculate the de Broglie wavelength (λ). - λ = h/p = (6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J s) / (5.4 × 10⁻²⁴ kg m/s) ≈ 1.23 × 10⁻¹⁰ m = 0.123 nm. **Answer**: The de Broglie wavelength of the electron is **0.123 nm**.

Card 6

Explain the **Davisson-Germer experiment**. What did it prove, and how?

The Davisson-Germer experiment (1927) provided experimental evidence for the wave nature of electrons, confirming de Broglie’s hypothesis. **Procedure**: - A beam of electrons was accelerated through a potential difference and directed at a nickel crystal. - The intensity of the scattered electrons was measured at various angles. **Observations**: - The scattered electrons exhibited a diffraction pattern, similar to X-rays (which are electromagnetic waves). - The angle of maximum intensity matched the prediction of Bragg’s law for wave interference. **Significance**: - Proved that electrons (particles) exhibit wave-like properties, as their diffraction pattern could only be explained by wave interference. - Validated de Broglie’s equation **λ = h/p** for matter waves. - Laid the foundation for electron microscopy and quantum mechanics.

Card 7

What is the **Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle**? State its mathematical form and explain its implications.

**Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle**: It is impossible to simultaneously determine the exact position and momentum of a particle with absolute precision. The more accurately one quantity is measured, the less accurately the other can be known. **Mathematical Form**: - **Δx · Δp ≥ h/4π** where: - **Δx**: Uncertainty in position. - **Δp**: Uncertainty in momentum. - **h**: Planck’s constant. **Implications**: - Challenges the classical notion of deterministic trajectories (e.g., Bohr’s orbits). - Introduces the concept of probability distributions in quantum mechanics (e.g., electron clouds in atoms). - Limits the precision of measurements at the quantum scale, making it impossible to predict exact outcomes for individual particles. - Applies to other conjugate pairs, such as energy and time (**ΔE · Δt ≥ h/4π**).

Card 8

How does the **photoelectric effect** demonstrate the particle nature of light? Contrast this with the wave nature of light.

**Particle Nature (Photoelectric Effect)**: - Light behaves as discrete packets of energy (photons), each with energy **E = hν**. - A single photon interacts with a single electron, transferring all its energy instantaneously. - The kinetic energy of emitted electrons depends on photon frequency, not intensity, supporting the particle model. **Wave Nature of Light**: - Exhibited in phenomena like interference, diffraction, and polarization. - Light waves have amplitude (intensity) and frequency, with energy spread continuously over the wavefront. **Contrast**: - The photoelectric effect cannot be explained by wave theory (e.g., no threshold frequency, no instantaneous emission). - Wave theory fails to account for the particle-like behavior of light in this context, necessitating the photon model.
