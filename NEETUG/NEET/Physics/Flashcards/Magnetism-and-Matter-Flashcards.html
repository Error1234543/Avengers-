NEET Physics: Magnetism and Matter - Exam-Focused Flashcards

NEETEnglishFlashcards

Card 1

Define magnetic dipole moment. State its SI unit and dimensional formula.

The magnetic dipole moment (μ) is a vector quantity that represents the magnetic strength and orientation of a magnet or other object that produces a magnetic field. It is defined as the maximum torque (τ) experienced per unit magnetic field (B) when the dipole is perpendicular to the field: μ = τ/B. SI unit: ampere-square meter (A·m²). Dimensional formula: [M⁰L²T⁰A¹].

Card 2

What is the expression for the magnetic field on the axial line of a bar magnet? Derive it for a point at distance 'd' from the center of the magnet of length '2l' and pole strength 'm'.

The magnetic field (B) on the axial line of a bar magnet is given by: B = (μ₀/4π) * [2M/(d² - l²)²], where M = m * 2l (magnetic dipole moment). Derivation: The field due to the North pole (B₁) and South pole (B₂) are calculated using the inverse square law, then added vectorially. For d >> l, it simplifies to B = (μ₀/4π) * (2M/d³).

Card 3

Explain the term 'magnetic susceptibility' (χ). How is it related to relative magnetic permeability (μᵣ)?

Magnetic susceptibility (χ) is a dimensionless proportionality constant that indicates how much a material will become magnetized in an applied magnetic field. It is defined as χ = I/H, where I is the intensity of magnetization and H is the magnetizing field. Relation to relative permeability: μᵣ = 1 + χ.

Card 4

Classify materials based on their magnetic properties. Give one example of each.

1. Diamagnetic: Weakly repelled by a magnetic field (e.g., copper, water). χ < 0, μᵣ < 1. 2. Paramagnetic: Weakly attracted by a magnetic field (e.g., aluminum, oxygen). χ > 0, μᵣ > 1. 3. Ferromagnetic: Strongly attracted by a magnetic field and can retain magnetization (e.g., iron, nickel). χ >> 0, μᵣ >> 1.

Card 5

What is Curie's law? State its mathematical form and the condition under which it is valid.

Curie's law states that the magnetic susceptibility (χ) of a paramagnetic material is inversely proportional to its absolute temperature (T): χ = C/T, where C is the Curie constant. It is valid only for high temperatures and weak magnetic fields, where the material does not reach magnetic saturation.

Card 6

Describe the hysteresis loop for a ferromagnetic material. Label the key points and explain their significance.

A hysteresis loop is a plot of magnetic flux density (B) vs. magnetizing field (H) for a ferromagnetic material. Key points: 1. Saturation (Bₛ): Maximum magnetization. 2. Remanence (Bᵣ): Residual magnetization when H = 0. 3. Coercivity (H꜀): Reverse field required to demagnetize the material (B = 0). The loop area represents energy lost as heat during one cycle of magnetization.

Card 7

What is the magnetic field inside a toroidal solenoid? Derive the expression for a toroid of N turns carrying current I and mean radius R.

The magnetic field (B) inside a toroidal solenoid is uniform and given by: B = (μ₀NI)/(2πR). Derivation: Apply Ampere’s circuital law to a circular path of radius R inside the toroid. The line integral of B around the path equals μ₀ times the total current enclosed (NI). Thus, B * 2πR = μ₀NI, leading to B = (μ₀NI)/(2πR).

Card 8

Explain the difference between magnetic intensity (H) and magnetic field (B). State their SI units.

Magnetic intensity (H) is a measure of the magnetizing field due to external currents, independent of the medium. Magnetic field (B) is the total field inside a material, including the contribution from the material’s magnetization. Relation: B = μ₀(H + I), where I is the intensity of magnetization. SI units: H (A/m), B (tesla, T).

Card 9

What is the magnetic moment of an electron due to its orbital motion? How does it contribute to the magnetic properties of an atom?

The magnetic moment of an electron due to its orbital motion is given by μₗ = (e/2m) * L, where L is the orbital angular momentum. It contributes to the paramagnetic or diamagnetic properties of an atom. In atoms with unpaired electrons, the net orbital magnetic moment may align with an external field (paramagnetism). In filled shells, the net moment is zero, but induced moments oppose the field (diamagnetism).

Card 10

A bar magnet of magnetic moment 5 A·m² is placed in a uniform magnetic field of 0.2 T. Calculate the torque experienced when the magnet is perpendicular to the field.

Torque (τ) on a magnetic dipole is given by τ = μ × B. When μ is perpendicular to B, τ = μB. Substituting values: τ = 5 A·m² * 0.2 T = 1 N·m. The torque acts to align the magnet with the field.

Card 11

What is the expression for the magnetic field at the equatorial line of a bar magnet? Derive it for a point at distance 'd' from the center of the magnet of length '2l' and pole strength 'm'.

The magnetic field (B) on the equatorial line of a bar magnet is given by: B = (μ₀/4π) * [M/(d² + l²)^(3/2)], where M = m * 2l. Derivation: The fields due to the North and South poles are equal in magnitude but opposite in direction along the equatorial line. The resultant field is the vector difference, simplifying to B = (μ₀/4π) * (M/d³) for d >> l.

Card 12

Explain the term 'magnetic domain'. How do domains explain the behavior of ferromagnetic materials?

A magnetic domain is a region within a ferromagnetic material where the atomic magnetic moments are aligned in the same direction. In an unmagnetized sample, domains are randomly oriented, resulting in zero net magnetization. When an external field is applied, domains aligned with the field grow at the expense of others, leading to net magnetization. This explains why ferromagnetic materials can be strongly magnetized and retain magnetization.

Card 13

What is the Curie temperature? State its significance for ferromagnetic materials.

The Curie temperature (T꜀) is the temperature above which a ferromagnetic material loses its ferromagnetic properties and becomes paramagnetic. Below T꜀, thermal energy is insufficient to disrupt the alignment of magnetic domains. Above T꜀, thermal agitation overcomes the exchange interaction, destroying the domain structure. Example: Iron’s Curie temperature is 770°C.

Card 14

A solenoid of 500 turns and length 0.5 m carries a current of 2 A. Calculate the magnetic field inside the solenoid. What happens to the field if an iron core of relative permeability 1000 is inserted?

Magnetic field inside a solenoid: B = μ₀nI, where n = N/L. Substituting values: n = 500/0.5 = 1000 turns/m, B = (4π × 10⁻⁷ T·m/A) * 1000 * 2 = 2.51 × 10⁻³ T. With an iron core: B = μᵣμ₀nI = 1000 * 2.51 × 10⁻³ = 2.51 T. The field increases by a factor of μᵣ.

Card 15

What is the difference between hard and soft ferromagnetic materials? Give one example of each and their applications.

Hard ferromagnetic materials: High coercivity, retain magnetization (e.g., steel, Alnico). Used in permanent magnets (e.g., loudspeakers, electric meters). Soft ferromagnetic materials: Low coercivity, easily magnetized/demagnetized (e.g., soft iron, permalloy). Used in transformer cores, electromagnets, and magnetic shielding.

Card 16

Explain the origin of diamagnetism. Why is it a universal property of all materials?

Diamagnetism arises from the orbital motion of electrons in atoms. When an external magnetic field is applied, the orbital motion changes to produce a magnetic moment opposing the field (Lenz’s law). This effect is present in all materials but is often masked by stronger paramagnetic or ferromagnetic effects. Diamagnetism is temperature-independent and weak (χ ≈ -10⁻⁵).
