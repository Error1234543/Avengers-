NEETEnglishFlashcards

Card 1

Define photosynthesis and write its overall balanced chemical equation.

Photosynthesis is the biological process by which green plants, algae, and some bacteria convert light energy into chemical energy stored in glucose or other organic compounds. The overall balanced chemical equation for oxygenic photosynthesis is: 6CO₂ + 12H₂O + light energy → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ + 6H₂O. This process occurs in the chloroplasts, primarily in the mesophyll cells of leaves, and involves two main stages: the light-dependent reactions and the Calvin cycle (light-independent reactions).

Card 2

Name the two primary pigments involved in photosynthesis and state their roles.

The two primary pigments involved in photosynthesis are **chlorophyll a** and **chlorophyll b**. Chlorophyll a is the **primary photosynthetic pigment** that directly participates in the light-dependent reactions by absorbing light energy and converting it into chemical energy (ATP and NADPH). Chlorophyll b is an **accessory pigment** that broadens the spectrum of light absorbed and transfers the captured energy to chlorophyll a. Both are located in the thylakoid membranes of chloroplasts.

Card 3

What is the Z-scheme of non-cyclic photophosphorylation? Explain its significance.

The **Z-scheme** (or Z-pathway) is a model representing the flow of electrons during non-cyclic photophosphorylation in the light-dependent reactions of photosynthesis. It involves two photosystems: **Photosystem II (PSII)** and **Photosystem I (PSI)**. Electrons are excited by light in PSII, travel through the electron transport chain (ETC) via plastoquinone, cytochrome b₆f complex, and plastocyanin, and are re-excited in PSI before being used to reduce NADP⁺ to NADPH. The significance of the Z-scheme includes: (1) **ATP and NADPH production** for the Calvin cycle, (2) **photolysis of water** (splitting of H₂O to release O₂, protons, and electrons), and (3) establishment of a **proton gradient** across the thylakoid membrane, driving ATP synthesis via ATP synthase.

Card 4

Describe the three phases of the Calvin cycle and their key enzymes.

The Calvin cycle (C₃ cycle) occurs in the stroma of chloroplasts and consists of three phases: 1. **Carboxylation Phase**: CO₂ is fixed into a 5-carbon sugar, ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate (RuBP), by the enzyme **RuBP carboxylase/oxygenase (Rubisco)**, forming an unstable 6-carbon compound that immediately splits into two molecules of 3-phosphoglycerate (3-PGA). 2. **Reduction Phase**: 3-PGA is phosphorylated by ATP and reduced by NADPH to form glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (G3P), a 3-carbon sugar. The enzyme **G3P dehydrogenase** catalyzes this step. 3. **Regeneration Phase**: Most G3P molecules (5 out of 6) are used to regenerate RuBP, requiring ATP. The enzyme **transketolase** and **aldolase** play key roles in rearranging carbon skeletons. The net output is one molecule of G3P per 3 turns of the cycle, which is used to synthesize glucose and other carbohydrates.

Card 5

What is photorespiration? Why is it considered a wasteful process?

Photorespiration is a metabolic pathway that occurs when the enzyme **Rubisco** oxygenates RuBP instead of carboxylating it, leading to the formation of **phosphoglycolate** (a 2-carbon compound) instead of 3-PGA. This happens under conditions of **high O₂ and low CO₂ concentrations**, such as in hot, dry environments. Photorespiration is considered wasteful because: - It **consumes ATP and NADPH** without producing sugars. - It releases **previously fixed CO₂**, reducing photosynthetic efficiency. - The salvage pathway (involving peroxisomes and mitochondria) generates **hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂)**, a toxic byproduct. - It competes with the Calvin cycle, lowering the overall rate of carbon fixation.

Card 6

Compare C₃, C₄, and CAM plants in terms of anatomy, CO₂ fixation pathway, and adaptive advantages.

**C₃ Plants**: - **Anatomy**: Mesophyll cells contain chloroplasts; no Kranz anatomy. - **CO₂ Fixation**: CO₂ is directly fixed into 3-PGA via Rubisco in the Calvin cycle. - **Adaptive Advantage**: Efficient in temperate climates with moderate light and water availability. - **Disadvantage**: Prone to photorespiration in hot, dry conditions. **C₄ Plants**: - **Anatomy**: Exhibit **Kranz anatomy** (bundle-sheath cells surrounded by mesophyll cells). - **CO₂ Fixation**: CO₂ is first fixed into **oxaloacetate (4-carbon compound)** in mesophyll cells by **PEP carboxylase**, then transported to bundle-sheath cells where it is decarboxylated and refixed by Rubisco. - **Adaptive Advantage**: Minimizes photorespiration by concentrating CO₂ around Rubisco; thrives in hot, dry climates (e.g., maize, sugarcane). **CAM Plants**: - **Anatomy**: No Kranz anatomy; large vacuoles in mesophyll cells. - **CO₂ Fixation**: **Temporal separation** of CO₂ fixation and Calvin cycle. CO₂ is fixed into malate at **night** (stomata open) by PEP carboxylase and stored in vacuoles. During the **day**, malate is decarboxylated, and CO₂ is refixed by Rubisco (stomata closed). - **Adaptive Advantage**: Conserves water in arid environments (e.g., cacti, pineapples).

Card 7

Explain the role of stomata in plant physiology. What factors regulate their opening and closing?

Stomata are microscopic pores on the epidermis of leaves, primarily on the lower surface, that regulate **gas exchange (CO₂ uptake and O₂ release)** and **transpiration (water loss)**. Each stoma is flanked by two **guard cells** that control its opening and closing. **Mechanism of Opening/Closing**: - **Opening**: Guard cells absorb water via osmosis (due to K⁺ ion uptake and malate synthesis), becoming turgid and bowing outward, which opens the stoma. - **Closing**: Guard cells lose water (due to K⁺ ion efflux), becoming flaccid and closing the stoma. **Regulatory Factors**: 1. **Light**: Stomata open in response to **blue light** (via phototropins) and **photosynthetically active radiation (PAR)**. 2. **CO₂ Concentration**: Low internal CO₂ levels promote stomatal opening; high levels induce closing. 3. **Water Availability**: **Abscisic acid (ABA)**, a plant hormone, triggers stomatal closure during drought stress. 4. **Temperature**: High temperatures may induce closure to reduce water loss. 5. **Humidity**: Low humidity increases transpiration, leading to stomatal closure. 6. **Circadian Rhythms**: Stomata often follow a diurnal pattern, opening during the day and closing at night.

Card 8

What is the pressure-flow hypothesis? Describe the steps involved in phloem translocation.

The **pressure-flow hypothesis** (or mass flow hypothesis) explains the translocation of organic nutrients (primarily sucrose) through the **phloem** from **source** (e.g., leaves) to **sink** (e.g., roots, fruits). The steps are: 1. **Loading at the Source**: Sucrose is actively transported into **companion cells** and then into **sieve tube elements** of the phloem, creating a **high solute concentration**. This lowers the water potential, causing water to enter the phloem from the xylem via osmosis. 2. **Generation of Hydrostatic Pressure**: The influx of water increases the **turgor pressure** in the sieve tubes at the source, creating a pressure gradient. 3. **Mass Flow**: The high turgor pressure drives the phloem sap (containing sucrose, amino acids, and other solutes) through the sieve tubes toward the sink. 4. **Unloading at the Sink**: Sucrose is actively or passively transported out of the phloem into sink cells, where it is used or stored. The removal of solutes increases the water potential, causing water to exit the phloem and return to the xylem. 5. **Recycling of Water**: Water is recycled back to the source via the xylem, maintaining the pressure gradient. **Key Points**: - Requires **ATP** for active loading/unloading. - **Sieve plates** in sieve tubes allow bulk flow. - **Companion cells** assist in loading/unloading via plasmodesmata.
