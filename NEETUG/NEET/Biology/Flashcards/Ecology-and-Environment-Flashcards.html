NEET Biology: Ecology and Environment - Exam-Focused Flashcards

NEETEnglishFlashcards

Card 1

Define 'ecology' and distinguish it from 'environmental science'.

Ecology is the scientific study of interactions among organisms and between organisms and their physical environment. It focuses on relationships, energy flow, and nutrient cycling within ecosystems. Environmental science, in contrast, is an interdisciplinary field that integrates physical, biological, and information sciences to study the environment and solve environmental problems, often including human impacts and policy dimensions. Ecology is a core component of environmental science but does not typically address engineering or policy solutions.

Card 2

What is an 'ecosystem'? Name its two main components and provide one example of each.

An ecosystem is a functional unit of nature where living organisms interact with each other and their physical environment. Its two main components are: 1. Biotic components: Living organisms such as plants, animals, and microorganisms. Example: A population of deer in a forest. 2. Abiotic components: Non-living physical and chemical factors like temperature, light, water, and soil. Example: Sunlight providing energy for photosynthesis in a pond ecosystem.

Card 3

Explain the concept of 'trophic levels' in an ecosystem. What is the role of producers?

Trophic levels represent the feeding positions in a food chain, showing the flow of energy through an ecosystem. Producers (autotrophs) occupy the first trophic level and are organisms like green plants and algae that synthesize their own food through photosynthesis using sunlight. They convert solar energy into chemical energy, forming the base of the food chain and providing energy for all other trophic levels (herbivores, carnivores, and decomposers).

Card 4

Differentiate between 'food chain' and 'food web' with examples.

A food chain is a linear sequence of organisms where each is eaten by the next member in the chain, showing a single path of energy flow. Example: Grass → Grasshopper → Frog → Snake → Hawk. A food web is a complex network of interconnected food chains in an ecosystem, representing multiple feeding relationships and energy pathways. Example: In a forest, grass may be eaten by deer and rabbits, deer may be eaten by tigers, rabbits by foxes, and both deer and rabbits by vultures after death, creating multiple intersecting chains.

Card 5

What is 'ecological succession'? Distinguish between primary and secondary succession.

Ecological succession is the gradual, predictable process of change in the species composition of an ecological community over time. It occurs in stages called seral stages, leading to a stable climax community. Primary succession begins in lifeless areas where no soil exists, such as bare rock or newly formed volcanic islands. Example: Lichens colonizing bare rock, followed by mosses, grasses, and eventually trees. Secondary succession occurs in areas where an existing community has been disturbed or destroyed but soil remains intact. Example: Regrowth of vegetation after a forest fire or agricultural abandonment.

Card 6

Define 'biodiversity'. Name and briefly describe its three main levels.

Biodiversity refers to the variety of life at all levels of biological organization. Its three main levels are: 1. Genetic diversity: The variety of genes within a species, allowing populations to adapt to changing environments. Example: Different breeds of dogs or varieties of rice. 2. Species diversity: The variety of species within a region or ecosystem. Example: The number of different bird species in a tropical rainforest. 3. Ecosystem diversity: The variety of ecosystems in a given area, including different habitats and ecological processes. Example: Deserts, forests, wetlands, and grasslands within a country.

Card 7

What are 'biotic interactions'? Name and define three types of interspecific interactions with examples.

Biotic interactions are relationships between organisms of the same or different species within an ecosystem. Three types of interspecific interactions are: 1. Mutualism: Both species benefit. Example: Bees and flowering plants (bees get nectar, plants get pollinated). 2. Parasitism: One species (parasite) benefits at the expense of the other (host). Example: Tapeworms living in the intestines of humans. 3. Competition: Both species are harmed due to the struggle for limited resources. Example: Lions and hyenas competing for the same prey in the savanna.

Card 8

Explain the '10% law' of energy transfer in ecosystems. Why is it significant?

The 10% law, proposed by Raymond Lindeman, states that only about 10% of the energy available at one trophic level is transferred to the next trophic level. The remaining 90% is lost as heat due to metabolic processes, respiration, and waste. This law is significant because: - It explains why food chains rarely have more than 4-5 trophic levels (energy becomes insufficient to support higher levels). - It highlights the inefficiency of energy transfer, emphasizing the importance of producers in supporting ecosystems. - It underscores the need for large populations of producers to sustain higher trophic levels.

Card 9

What is 'biomagnification'? Provide an example and explain its ecological impact.

Biomagnification is the process by which the concentration of toxic substances increases at each successive trophic level in a food chain. This occurs because these substances are persistent (not easily broken down) and fat-soluble, accumulating in the tissues of organisms. Example: DDT (a pesticide) enters water bodies and is absorbed by phytoplankton. Small fish eat the phytoplankton, larger fish eat the small fish, and birds of prey eat the larger fish. The concentration of DDT increases at each level, reaching harmful levels in top predators. Ecological impact: Biomagnification can lead to reproductive failure, birth defects, and death in top predators, disrupting ecosystem balance. It also poses risks to human health through contaminated food sources.

Card 10

Define 'ecological niche'. How does it differ from 'habitat'?

An ecological niche refers to the role and position a species has in its environment, including all its interactions with biotic and abiotic factors. It encompasses the species' habitat, food sources, behavior, and adaptations. Example: The niche of an earthworm includes decomposing organic matter, aerating soil, and serving as prey for birds. A habitat, in contrast, is the physical place or environment where a species lives. Example: The habitat of a fish may be a freshwater lake. While habitat is the 'address' of a species, niche is its 'profession' or functional role in the ecosystem.

Card 11

What are 'keystone species'? Provide an example and explain their importance in ecosystems.

Keystone species are species that have a disproportionately large impact on their environment relative to their abundance. Their presence is crucial for maintaining the structure and function of an ecosystem. Example: Sea otters in kelp forest ecosystems. They prey on sea urchins, which feed on kelp. Without sea otters, sea urchin populations explode, leading to overgrazing of kelp and the collapse of the kelp forest ecosystem, which supports numerous other species. Importance: Keystone species help maintain biodiversity, control populations of other species, and preserve ecosystem stability. Their removal can lead to cascading effects and ecosystem collapse.

Card 12

Explain the 'carbon cycle'. Name two human activities that disrupt this cycle and their consequences.

The carbon cycle is the biogeochemical cycle by which carbon is exchanged among the biosphere, geosphere, hydrosphere, and atmosphere. Key processes include photosynthesis (carbon fixation), respiration (carbon release), decomposition, and combustion. Carbon is stored in reservoirs like the atmosphere (CO₂), oceans, fossil fuels, and living organisms. Human activities disrupting the cycle: 1. Burning of fossil fuels: Releases large amounts of stored carbon (CO₂) into the atmosphere, increasing greenhouse gas concentrations and contributing to global warming and climate change. 2. Deforestation: Reduces the number of trees available to absorb CO₂ through photosynthesis, leading to higher atmospheric CO₂ levels and loss of carbon sinks. It also releases stored carbon when trees are burned or decomposed.

Card 13

What is 'eutrophication'? Describe its causes, process, and ecological effects.

Eutrophication is the process by which a body of water becomes overly enriched with nutrients (primarily nitrogen and phosphorus), leading to excessive growth of algae and other aquatic plants. Causes: Runoff from agricultural fields (fertilizers), sewage discharge, and industrial waste. Process: 1. Nutrients enter water bodies, stimulating algal blooms. 2. Algae grow rapidly, forming dense mats on the water surface. 3. Algae die and decompose, consuming dissolved oxygen in the water. 4. Oxygen levels drop, leading to hypoxia (low oxygen) or anoxia (no oxygen), killing fish and other aquatic organisms. Ecological effects: Loss of biodiversity, disruption of food chains, creation of dead zones, and deterioration of water quality for human use.
