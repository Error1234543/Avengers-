NEET Biology: Genetics and Evolution - Exam-Focused Flashcards

NEETEnglishFlashcards

Card 1

Define 'gene' and explain its role in heredity.

A gene is a segment of DNA that contains the information required to produce a functional product, typically a protein or RNA molecule. It serves as the basic unit of heredity, carrying genetic instructions from parents to offspring. Genes determine traits by encoding specific proteins that influence cellular processes, development, and physiology. Variations in genes (alleles) lead to genetic diversity within a population.

Card 2

What is the difference between genotype and phenotype? Provide an example.

Genotype refers to the genetic makeup of an organism, specifically the alleles present for a particular gene (e.g., BB, Bb, or bb for eye color). Phenotype refers to the observable physical or biochemical characteristics of an organism, resulting from the interaction of its genotype with the environment (e.g., brown eyes or blue eyes). Example: In pea plants, the genotype 'Tt' for height may result in the phenotype of a tall plant, where 'T' (tall) is dominant over 't' (short).

Card 3

State Mendel's Law of Segregation and explain its significance.

Mendel's Law of Segregation states that during the formation of gametes, the two alleles for a gene separate from each other so that each gamete carries only one allele for each gene. This ensures genetic variation in offspring. Significance: It explains how traits are inherited independently of one another and forms the basis for predicting the outcomes of genetic crosses (e.g., monohybrid crosses).

Card 4

What is incomplete dominance? Give an example.

Incomplete dominance is a genetic phenomenon where neither allele of a gene is completely dominant over the other, resulting in a blended or intermediate phenotype in heterozygous individuals. Example: In snapdragon flowers, crossing a red-flowered plant (RR) with a white-flowered plant (rr) produces offspring with pink flowers (Rr), an intermediate color between red and white.

Card 5

Explain the concept of multiple alleles with reference to human blood groups.

Multiple alleles refer to the presence of more than two alternative forms of a gene (alleles) that can occupy the same locus on a chromosome. Human ABO blood groups are determined by three alleles: I^A, I^B, and i. I^A and I^B are codominant, while i is recessive. The combinations result in four blood groups: A (I^A I^A or I^A i), B (I^B I^B or I^B i), AB (I^A I^B), and O (ii).

Card 6

What is a sex-linked trait? Provide an example in humans.

A sex-linked trait is a characteristic determined by genes located on sex chromosomes (X or Y). Most sex-linked traits are X-linked because the X chromosome is larger and carries more genes. Example: Hemophilia is an X-linked recessive disorder where the blood fails to clot properly. Males (XY) with the defective allele on the X chromosome exhibit the disorder, while females (XX) are typically carriers if heterozygous.

Card 7

Describe the structure of DNA as proposed by Watson and Crick.

Watson and Crick proposed the double-helix model of DNA, which consists of two antiparallel polynucleotide strands coiled around a common axis. Key features: 1) The strands are composed of nucleotides (sugar-phosphate backbone with nitrogenous bases). 2) The bases pair specifically: adenine (A) with thymine (T) via two hydrogen bonds, and guanine (G) with cytosine (C) via three hydrogen bonds. 3) The helix has a uniform diameter of 2 nm, with a complete turn every 10 base pairs (3.4 nm).

Card 8

What is the central dogma of molecular biology?

The central dogma describes the flow of genetic information in biological systems: DNA → RNA → Protein. It involves three processes: 1) Replication: DNA copies itself. 2) Transcription: DNA is used as a template to synthesize RNA (mRNA). 3) Translation: mRNA is decoded by ribosomes to synthesize proteins. Exceptions include reverse transcription (RNA → DNA) in retroviruses.

Card 9

Explain the process of transcription in prokaryotes.

Transcription in prokaryotes is the synthesis of RNA from a DNA template, occurring in the cytoplasm. Steps: 1) Initiation: RNA polymerase binds to the promoter region of DNA, unwinding the double helix. 2) Elongation: RNA polymerase moves along the template strand, synthesizing a complementary RNA strand in the 5'→3' direction. 3) Termination: Transcription stops when RNA polymerase reaches a terminator sequence, releasing the newly synthesized RNA (mRNA, tRNA, or rRNA).

Card 10

What is a mutation? Differentiate between point mutations and frameshift mutations.

A mutation is a permanent change in the DNA sequence of an organism. Types: 1) Point mutations: Changes in a single nucleotide base (e.g., substitution). Example: Sickle cell anemia results from a substitution in the hemoglobin gene (GAG → GTG). 2) Frameshift mutations: Insertions or deletions of nucleotides that shift the reading frame of the genetic code, often leading to nonfunctional proteins. Example: Tay-Sachs disease can result from a frameshift mutation in the HEXA gene.

Card 11

Define genetic drift and explain its role in evolution.

Genetic drift is the random fluctuation in the frequency of alleles in a population due to chance events, particularly in small populations. It can lead to the loss or fixation of alleles over time, reducing genetic diversity. Role in evolution: Genetic drift can cause significant evolutionary changes, especially in isolated populations (e.g., founder effect or bottleneck effect), and may contribute to speciation by altering allele frequencies independently of natural selection.

Card 12

What is natural selection? Provide an example.

Natural selection is the process by which organisms better adapted to their environment tend to survive and produce more offspring, leading to the perpetuation of advantageous traits. Example: Peppered moths in industrial England. Before industrialization, light-colored moths were more common (camouflaged on lichen-covered trees). After industrialization, dark-colored moths became more prevalent due to pollution darkening tree bark, demonstrating how environmental changes drive natural selection.

Card 13

Explain the Hardy-Weinberg principle and its assumptions.

The Hardy-Weinberg principle states that allele and genotype frequencies in a population will remain constant from generation to generation in the absence of evolutionary influences. The principle is expressed as: p² + 2pq + q² = 1, where p and q are the frequencies of two alleles. Assumptions: 1) No mutations. 2) No gene flow (migration). 3) Large population size. 4) No genetic drift. 5) Random mating. 6) No natural selection. Deviations from these assumptions indicate evolutionary forces at work.

Card 14

What is speciation? Differentiate between allopatric and sympatric speciation.

Speciation is the process by which new species evolve from existing ones. Types: 1) Allopatric speciation: Occurs when populations of the same species become geographically isolated, leading to genetic divergence and reproductive isolation. Example: Darwin's finches on the Galápagos Islands. 2) Sympatric speciation: Occurs without geographic isolation, often due to ecological or behavioral differences within the same habitat. Example: Polyploidy in plants, where chromosome duplication leads to reproductive isolation from the parent species.

Card 15

Describe the evidence for evolution from comparative anatomy.

Comparative anatomy provides evidence for evolution through: 1) Homologous structures: Organs with similar anatomical features but different functions, indicating common ancestry (e.g., forelimbs of humans, bats, and whales). 2) Analogous structures: Organs with similar functions but different origins, indicating convergent evolution (e.g., wings of birds and insects). 3) Vestigial organs: Remnants of structures that were functional in ancestors but are reduced or nonfunctional in modern species (e.g., human appendix or coccyx).

Card 16

What is adaptive radiation? Provide an example.

Adaptive radiation is the rapid evolution of diversely adapted species from a common ancestor to exploit different ecological niches. Example: The finches observed by Darwin on the Galápagos Islands. A single ancestral species diversified into multiple species with distinct beak shapes and sizes, each adapted to different food sources (e.g., seeds, insects, or cactus flowers).

Card 17

Explain the concept of molecular homology in evolution.

Molecular homology refers to similarities at the molecular level (e.g., DNA, RNA, or protein sequences) that indicate common ancestry. Example: Cytochrome c, a protein involved in cellular respiration, shows highly conserved sequences across diverse species, supporting evolutionary relationships. The more similar the sequences, the more recent the common ancestor. Molecular homology is used to construct phylogenetic trees and study evolutionary relationships.
