Definitions

  • {'term': 'Ecology', 'definition': 'The branch of biology that studies the interactions among organisms and their biophysical environment, including both biotic (living) and abiotic (non-living) components. It encompasses individual organisms, populations, communities, ecosystems, biomes, and the biosphere.'}
  • {'term': 'Ecosystem', 'definition': 'A functional unit of nature where living organisms (biotic components) interact with each other and their physical environment (abiotic components) to form a stable system. Examples include forests, ponds, grasslands, and deserts.'}
  • {'term': 'Biodiversity', 'definition': 'The variety of life at genetic, species, and ecosystem levels. It includes species diversity (number of species), genetic diversity (variation within species), and ecosystem diversity (variety of habitats).'}
  • {'term': 'Food Chain', 'definition': 'A linear sequence of organisms where each is eaten by the next member in the chain. It represents the flow of energy from producers (autotrophs) to consumers (heterotrophs) and decomposers.'}
  • {'term': 'Food Web', 'definition': 'A complex network of interconnected food chains in an ecosystem, showing multiple feeding relationships and energy pathways. It provides stability to the ecosystem by offering alternative food sources.'}
  • {'term': 'Trophic Level', 'definition': 'The position an organism occupies in a food chain. Producers (e.g., plants) occupy the first trophic level, primary consumers (herbivores) the second, secondary consumers (carnivores) the third, and so on.'}
  • {'term': 'Ecological Pyramid', 'definition': 'A graphical representation of the relationship between different trophic levels in an ecosystem, based on parameters like number of organisms, biomass, or energy. Types include pyramid of number, pyramid of biomass, and pyramid of energy.'}
  • {'term': 'Biogeochemical Cycle', 'definition': 'The cyclic movement of essential elements (e.g., carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, water) through biotic and abiotic components of an ecosystem. These cycles maintain the balance of nutrients in the environment.'}
  • {'term': 'Pollution', 'definition': 'The introduction of harmful substances or products into the environment, causing adverse changes. Pollutants can be natural or anthropogenic (human-made) and affect air, water, soil, and living organisms.'}
  • {'term': 'Biodiversity Hotspot', 'definition': 'A biogeographic region with significant levels of biodiversity that is threatened by human activities. To qualify, a region must contain at least 1,500 species of vascular plants as endemics and have lost at least 70% of its original habitat.'}

Key concepts

  • {'concept': 'Levels of Ecological Organization', 'content': 'Ecology is studied at multiple levels: (1) **Organism**: An individual living being (e.g., a single deer). (2) **Population**: A group of individuals of the same species living in a specific area (e.g., a herd of deer). (3) **Community**: All populations of different species living and interacting in an area (e.g., deer, grass, wolves, and trees in a forest). (4) **Ecosystem**: A community interacting with its abiotic environment (e.g., a forest ecosystem with soil, water, and climate). (5) **Biome**: A large-scale community of organisms characterized by the dominant forms of plant life and climate (e.g., tropical rainforest, desert). (6) **Biosphere**: The global sum of all ecosystems, where life exists on Earth.'}
  • {'concept': 'Energy Flow in Ecosystems', 'content': 'Energy enters ecosystems via sunlight, which is captured by producers (e.g., plants, algae) through photosynthesis. This energy is transferred to consumers (herbivores, carnivores) and decomposers (bacteria, fungi) through feeding relationships. Energy flow is unidirectional and follows the **10% Law**: only about 10% of the energy at one trophic level is transferred to the next, with the rest lost as heat. This limits the number of trophic levels in a food chain to 4-5.'}
  • {'concept': 'Ecological Pyramids', 'content': 'Ecological pyramids represent the structure of an ecosystem in terms of energy, biomass, or number of organisms. (1) **Pyramid of Number**: Shows the number of organisms at each trophic level. It can be upright (e.g., grassland) or inverted (e.g., tree ecosystem where one tree supports many insects). (2) **Pyramid of Biomass**: Represents the total biomass at each trophic level. It is usually upright (e.g., forest) but can be inverted in aquatic ecosystems (e.g., phytoplankton supporting larger zooplankton). (3) **Pyramid of Energy**: Always upright, as energy decreases at higher trophic levels due to the 10% Law. It is the most accurate representation of energy flow.'}
  • {'concept': 'Biogeochemical Cycles', 'content': 'These cycles describe the movement of essential elements through the environment. Key cycles include: (1) **Carbon Cycle**: Carbon is fixed by photosynthesis, passed through food chains, and returned to the atmosphere via respiration, decomposition, and combustion. Human activities (e.g., burning fossil fuels) disrupt this cycle, increasing atmospheric CO₂. (2) **Nitrogen Cycle**: Nitrogen is fixed by bacteria (e.g., Rhizobium in legumes) into usable forms (ammonia, nitrates), absorbed by plants, and cycled through food chains. Denitrifying bacteria return nitrogen to the atmosphere. (3) **Water Cycle**: Involves evaporation, condensation, precipitation, and runoff. Human activities (e.g., deforestation, groundwater extraction) alter this cycle. (4) **Phosphorus Cycle**: Phosphorus moves from rocks to soil to organisms and back to soil. Unlike other cycles, it lacks a gaseous phase and is slow-moving.'}
  • {'concept': 'Ecological Succession', 'content': 'The gradual process by which the species composition of a community changes over time in an ecosystem. (1) **Primary Succession**: Occurs in lifeless areas (e.g., bare rock, newly formed volcanic islands) where soil is absent. Pioneer species (e.g., lichens, mosses) colonize first, followed by grasses, shrubs, and trees. (2) **Secondary Succession**: Occurs in areas where an existing community has been disturbed (e.g., abandoned farmland, forest after a fire). Soil is already present, so succession proceeds faster. The final stable community is called the **climax community**.'}
  • {'concept': 'Biodiversity and Its Conservation', 'content': 'Biodiversity is crucial for ecosystem stability, resilience, and human well-being. Threats to biodiversity include: (1) **Habitat Loss**: Due to deforestation, urbanization, and agriculture. (2) **Pollution**: Air, water, and soil pollution harm species. (3) **Overexploitation**: Hunting, fishing, and harvesting beyond sustainable levels. (4) **Invasive Species**: Non-native species outcompete or prey on native species. (5) **Climate Change**: Alters habitats and disrupts species distributions. Conservation strategies include: (1) **In-Situ Conservation**: Protecting species in their natural habitats (e.g., national parks, wildlife sanctuaries). (2) **Ex-Situ Conservation**: Protecting species outside their natural habitats (e.g., zoos, botanical gardens, seed banks). (3) **Biodiversity Hotspots**: Prioritizing regions with high endemism and threat levels (e.g., Western Ghats, Himalayas). (4) **Legal Frameworks**: Laws like the Wildlife Protection Act (1972) and Biodiversity Act (2002) in India.'}
  • {'concept': 'Environmental Pollution', 'content': 'Pollution is a major environmental issue with severe ecological and health impacts. Types include: (1) **Air Pollution**: Caused by pollutants like CO₂, SO₂, NOₓ, and particulate matter. Sources include vehicles, industries, and burning fossil fuels. Effects include respiratory diseases, acid rain, and global warming. (2) **Water Pollution**: Contamination of water bodies by sewage, industrial waste, and agricultural runoff. Leads to eutrophication, loss of aquatic life, and waterborne diseases. (3) **Soil Pollution**: Caused by pesticides, heavy metals, and industrial waste. Reduces soil fertility and contaminates food chains. (4) **Noise Pollution**: Excessive sound from industries, vehicles, and construction. Causes hearing loss, stress, and wildlife disruption. (5) **Plastic Pollution**: Non-biodegradable plastics accumulate in ecosystems, harming marine and terrestrial life. Mitigation strategies include reducing emissions, treating waste, and adopting sustainable practices.'}
