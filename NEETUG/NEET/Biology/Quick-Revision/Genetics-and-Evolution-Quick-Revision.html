Definitions

  • {'term': 'Genetics', 'definition': 'The branch of biology that studies heredity, genes, and variation in living organisms. It explains how traits are passed from parents to offspring through genetic material (DNA).'}
  • {'term': 'Heredity', 'definition': 'The transmission of genetic characters from parents to offspring, ensuring continuity of traits across generations.'}
  • {'term': 'Gene', 'definition': 'A segment of DNA that codes for a specific protein or RNA molecule, determining a particular trait or function in an organism.'}
  • {'term': 'Allele', 'definition': 'Different versions of the same gene that occupy the same locus on homologous chromosomes. Alleles can be dominant or recessive.'}
  • {'term': 'Genotype', 'definition': 'The genetic constitution of an organism, representing the alleles present for a particular trait (e.g., TT, Tt, tt).'}
  • {'term': 'Phenotype', 'definition': 'The observable physical or biochemical characteristics of an organism, determined by its genotype and environmental influences.'}
  • {'term': 'Evolution', 'definition': 'The gradual process by which species change over time through genetic variation, natural selection, and other mechanisms, leading to the diversity of life on Earth.'}
  • {'term': 'Natural Selection', 'definition': 'A mechanism of evolution proposed by Charles Darwin, where individuals with advantageous traits are more likely to survive and reproduce, passing those traits to the next generation.'}
  • {'term': 'Mutation', 'definition': 'A permanent change in the DNA sequence of a gene or chromosome, which can lead to new alleles and genetic variation. Mutations can be neutral, beneficial, or harmful.'}
  • {'term': 'Genetic Drift', 'definition': 'Random fluctuations in the frequency of alleles in a population, often occurring in small populations and leading to loss of genetic diversity.'}

Key concepts

  • {'concept': 'Mendelian Inheritance', 'content': "Gregor Mendel's laws form the foundation of genetics. The **Law of Segregation** states that alleles for a trait separate during gamete formation, ensuring each gamete carries only one allele. The **Law of Independent Assortment** states that alleles of different genes assort independently during gamete formation (applies to genes on different chromosomes or far apart on the same chromosome). The **Law of Dominance** states that in a heterozygous pair, the dominant allele masks the recessive allele. Mendel's experiments with pea plants (e.g., tall vs. dwarf, yellow vs. green seeds) demonstrated these principles."}
  • {'concept': 'Chromosomal Theory of Inheritance', 'content': "Proposed by Sutton and Boveri, this theory states that genes are located on chromosomes, which are the physical carriers of genetic material. Chromosomes segregate and assort independently during meiosis, explaining Mendel's laws at the cellular level. Key evidence includes the behavior of sex chromosomes (e.g., XX in females, XY in males) and the discovery of linked genes (genes on the same chromosome that do not assort independently)."}
  • {'concept': 'Linkage and Recombination', 'content': 'Genes located close to each other on the same chromosome are **linked** and tend to be inherited together. **Recombination** (crossing over during meiosis) can separate linked genes, producing new combinations of alleles. The frequency of recombination between two genes is proportional to the distance between them on the chromosome, which is used to construct **genetic maps**. For example, if two genes recombine 10% of the time, they are 10 map units apart.'}
  • {'concept': 'Sex Determination', 'content': 'The mechanism by which the sex of an organism is established. In humans, the **XY system** determines sex: females have two X chromosomes (XX), while males have one X and one Y chromosome (XY). The SRY gene on the Y chromosome triggers male development. Other systems include the **ZW system** (birds, some reptiles: ZW for females, ZZ for males) and the **haplodiploid system** (bees, ants: males are haploid, females are diploid). Environmental factors (e.g., temperature) can also determine sex in some species.'}
  • {'concept': 'Molecular Basis of Inheritance', 'content': "DNA is the hereditary material, as proven by experiments like the **Hershey-Chase experiment** (using bacteriophages) and **Griffith's transformation experiment** (using *Streptococcus pneumoniae*). DNA structure, proposed by Watson and Crick, is a double helix with complementary base pairing (A-T, C-G). **DNA replication** is semi-conservative, where each new DNA molecule consists of one old strand and one newly synthesized strand. The **central dogma** of molecular biology describes the flow of genetic information: DNA → RNA → Protein (transcription and translation)."}
  • {'concept': 'Mutations and Genetic Disorders', 'content': "Mutations can be **point mutations** (substitution of a single nucleotide, e.g., sickle cell anemia caused by a substitution in the hemoglobin gene) or **chromosomal mutations** (deletions, duplications, inversions, or translocations, e.g., Down syndrome caused by trisomy 21). **Pedigree analysis** is used to study the inheritance of genetic disorders, such as autosomal dominant (e.g., Huntington's disease), autosomal recessive (e.g., cystic fibrosis), and X-linked disorders (e.g., hemophilia, color blindness)."}
  • {'concept': 'Evolutionary Mechanisms', 'content': "Evolution occurs through multiple mechanisms: **Natural selection** (differential survival and reproduction), **genetic drift** (random changes in allele frequencies, significant in small populations), **gene flow** (migration of individuals between populations), and **mutations** (source of new alleles). **Adaptive radiation** is the diversification of a species into different forms to adapt to various ecological niches (e.g., Darwin's finches). **Convergent evolution** occurs when unrelated species evolve similar traits due to similar environmental pressures (e.g., wings in birds and bats)."}
  • {'concept': 'Evidence for Evolution', 'content': 'Multiple lines of evidence support evolution: **Fossil records** show transitional forms (e.g., *Archaeopteryx* linking reptiles and birds). **Comparative anatomy** reveals homologous structures (same origin, different functions, e.g., forelimbs of humans, bats, and whales) and analogous structures (different origin, same function, e.g., wings of insects and birds). **Embryology** shows similarities in early developmental stages (e.g., gill slits in vertebrate embryos). **Molecular biology** demonstrates genetic similarities (e.g., DNA sequences, protein structures) among species. **Biogeography** explains the distribution of species (e.g., unique species on islands like the Galápagos).'}
  • {'concept': 'Hardy-Weinberg Principle', 'content': 'A mathematical model describing the genetic equilibrium in a population. It states that allele frequencies remain constant across generations if the following conditions are met: no mutations, no gene flow, large population size, no genetic drift, random mating, and no natural selection. The equation is **p² + 2pq + q² = 1**, where **p** and **q** are the frequencies of two alleles, **p²** is the frequency of homozygous dominant individuals, **2pq** is the frequency of heterozygotes, and **q²** is the frequency of homozygous recessive individuals. Deviations from this equilibrium indicate evolutionary forces at work.'}
  • {'concept': 'Origin of Life', 'content': "The **Oparin-Haldane hypothesis** proposes that life originated from simple organic molecules formed under early Earth's reducing atmosphere (rich in methane, ammonia, water vapor, and hydrogen). The **Miller-Urey experiment** demonstrated that amino acids could form under these conditions. **Chemical evolution** led to the formation of complex molecules like proteins and nucleic acids. The **RNA world hypothesis** suggests that RNA was the first genetic material, capable of both storing information and catalyzing reactions. The first cells were likely **prokaryotic**, anaerobic, and heterotrophic, evolving into photosynthetic cyanobacteria that produced oxygen, leading to the **Great Oxygenation Event**."}

Important facts

  • Mendel's work was rediscovered in 1900 by de Vries, Correns, and von Tschermak, marking the birth of modern genetics.
  • The human genome contains approximately 20,000-25,000 protein-coding genes, but these genes make up only about 1-2% of the total DNA.
  • Incomplete dominance occurs when the heterozygous phenotype is intermediate between the two homozygous phenotypes (e.g., pink flowers in snapdragons from red and white parents).
  • Co-dominance occurs when both alleles in a heterozygous pair are fully expressed (e.g., AB blood group in humans).
  • Polygenic inheritance involves multiple genes controlling a single trait (e.g., skin color, height), resulting in a range of phenotypes.
  • Pleiotropy occurs when one gene influences multiple phenotypic traits (e.g., sickle cell anemia affects hemoglobin shape and malaria resistance).
  • Epistasis is the interaction between genes where one gene masks or modifies the expression of another (e.g., coat color in Labrador retrievers).
  • The **Human Genome Project** (completed in 2003) mapped all the genes in the human genome, revolutionizing genetics and medicine.
  • Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) is inherited exclusively from the mother and is used in tracing maternal lineages and studying human evolution.
