NEET Quick Revision: Structural Organisation in Animals and Plants

Definitions

  • Structural organisation refers to the hierarchical arrangement of cells, tissues, organs, and organ systems that collectively form a multicellular organism, enabling specialised functions and efficient survival.
  • Tissue: A group of similar cells that perform a specific function, derived from the same embryonic origin and working together as a unit.
  • Organ: A structure composed of two or more tissue types that work together to perform a specific physiological function.
  • Organ system: A group of organs that coordinate to perform complex functions essential for the survival of the organism.
  • Meristematic tissue: Plant tissue composed of undifferentiated, actively dividing cells responsible for growth in length (apical meristem) and girth (lateral meristem).
  • Permanent tissue: Plant tissue derived from meristematic cells that have lost the ability to divide and are specialised for functions such as protection, support, and transport.
  • Epithelial tissue: Animal tissue that covers body surfaces, lines cavities, and forms glands, providing protection, secretion, absorption, and sensation.
  • Connective tissue: Animal tissue that supports, binds, and protects other tissues and organs, characterised by an extracellular matrix and diverse cell types (e.g., bone, blood, cartilage, adipose).
  • Muscle tissue: Animal tissue responsible for movement and locomotion, classified into skeletal (voluntary, striated), smooth (involuntary, non-striated), and cardiac (involuntary, striated).
  • Nervous tissue: Animal tissue composed of neurons and glial cells, specialised for transmitting electrical impulses and coordinating body functions.

Meristematic tissues are found in regions of active growth (root tips, shoot tips, cambium) and consist of small, thin-walled cells with dense cytoplasm and prominent nuclei. Permanent tissues are derived from meristematic cells and are classified into simple (parenchyma, collenchyma, sclerenchyma) and complex (xylem, phloem) tissues. Parenchyma cells are living, thin-walled, and perform functions such as storage, photosynthesis, and secretion. Collenchyma provides mechanical support to growing regions (e.g., leaf petioles) with unevenly thickened cell walls. Sclerenchyma consists of dead, lignified cells (fibres and sclereids) that provide rigid support. Xylem transports water and minerals from roots to aerial parts and consists of tracheids, vessels, xylem fibres, and xylem parenchyma. Phloem transports organic nutrients (sucrose) from leaves to other parts and consists of sieve tubes, companion cells, phloem fibres, and phloem parenchyma.'}
  • {'concept': 'Animal Tissues', 'content': 'Animal tissues are classified into four primary types: epithelial, connective, muscle, and nervous. Epithelial tissues are avascular, tightly packed cells with minimal intercellular matrix, classified by shape (squamous, cuboidal, columnar) and layers (simple, stratified). Simple squamous epithelium (e.g., alveoli, blood vessels) facilitates diffusion, while stratified squamous epithelium (e.g., skin, oesophagus) provides protection. Connective tissues are characterised by a prominent extracellular matrix and include loose (areolar, adipose), dense (tendons, ligaments), specialised (bone, cartilage, blood), and reticular types. Bone tissue consists of osteocytes embedded in a calcified matrix, providing structural support and mineral storage. Blood is a fluid connective tissue with plasma, erythrocytes (oxygen transport), leukocytes (immunity), and platelets (clotting). Muscle tissues are contractile and classified into skeletal (voluntary, multinucleated, striated), smooth (involuntary, spindle-shaped, non-striated), and cardiac (involuntary, branched, striated with intercalated discs). Nervous tissue consists of neurons (transmit impulses) and glial cells (support neurons).'}

Important facts

  • Collenchyma cells are living and provide mechanical support to growing regions of the plant, such as leaf petioles and young stems, due to their unevenly thickened cell walls.
  • Sclerenchyma cells are dead at maturity and provide rigid support to mature plant parts, with heavily lignified secondary walls (e.g., fibres in stems, sclereids in seed coats).
  • Xylem vessels are more efficient in water conduction than tracheids due to their wider lumens and perforated end walls, but they are absent in gymnosperms (e.g., pines).
  • Phloem sieve tubes lack nuclei at maturity and rely on companion cells for metabolic functions, connected via plasmodesmata.
