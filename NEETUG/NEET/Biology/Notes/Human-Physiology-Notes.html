Introduction to Human Physiology

Human physiology is the scientific study of the functions and mechanisms occurring within the human body. It encompasses various systems such as the digestive, circulatory, respiratory, excretory, nervous, endocrine, skeletal, muscular, and reproductive systems. For NEET, understanding the integration and regulation of these systems is crucial, as questions often test the interdependence of physiological processes. The human body maintains homeostasis, a dynamic equilibrium that ensures optimal conditions for cellular functions, through feedback mechanisms like negative and positive feedback loops. For example, the regulation of body temperature, blood glucose levels, and pH balance are classic examples of homeostasis.

Digestive System

The digestive system is responsible for the breakdown of food into absorbable units, which are then assimilated into the body. It consists of the alimentary canal (mouth, esophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine, rectum, and anus) and accessory organs (salivary glands, liver, pancreas, and gallbladder). Key processes include: - **Ingestion**: Intake of food via the mouth. - **Mechanical Digestion**: Chewing (mastication) and churning in the stomach. - **Chemical Digestion**: Enzymatic breakdown of macromolecules (e.g., amylase for carbohydrates, pepsin for proteins, lipase for fats). - **Absorption**: Nutrients are absorbed primarily in the small intestine (villi and microvilli increase surface area). - **Egestion**: Elimination of undigested waste. The liver produces bile, which emulsifies fats, while the pancreas secretes digestive enzymes and bicarbonate to neutralize stomach acid. Common NEET questions focus on the role of enzymes, hormonal regulation (e.g., gastrin, secretin, cholecystokinin), and disorders like peptic ulcers or malabsorption syndromes.

Circulatory System

The circulatory system, or cardiovascular system, transports oxygen, nutrients, hormones, and waste products throughout the body. It consists of the heart, blood vessels (arteries, veins, capillaries), and blood. Key components and processes include: - **Heart**: A four-chambered muscular organ (two atria and two ventricles) that pumps blood. The right side pumps deoxygenated blood to the lungs (pulmonary circulation), while the left side pumps oxygenated blood to the body (systemic circulation). - **Blood Vessels**: Arteries carry blood away from the heart, veins carry blood toward the heart, and capillaries facilitate exchange between blood and tissues. - **Blood**: Composed of plasma (55%), red blood cells (erythrocytes), white blood cells (leukocytes), and platelets (thrombocytes). Hemoglobin in RBCs binds oxygen. - **Cardiac Cycle**: Includes systole (contraction) and diastole (relaxation). The SA node (pacemaker) initiates the heartbeat. - **Blood Pressure**: Regulated by cardiac output, blood volume, and peripheral resistance. Hypertension and hypotension are common disorders. NEET often tests knowledge of blood groups (ABO and Rh systems), ECG interpretation, and disorders like atherosclerosis or heart failure.

Respiratory System

The respiratory system facilitates gas exchange (oxygen and carbon dioxide) between the body and the environment. It includes the nasal cavity, pharynx, larynx, trachea, bronchi, bronchioles, and alveoli. Key processes are: - **Ventilation**: Movement of air into (inhalation) and out of (exhalation) the lungs, driven by diaphragm and intercostal muscles. - **Gas Exchange**: Occurs in alveoli via diffusion. Oxygen diffuses into blood, and carbon dioxide diffuses out. - **Transport of Gases**: Oxygen binds to hemoglobin (oxyhemoglobin), while carbon dioxide is transported as bicarbonate ions (HCO₃⁻), carbaminohemoglobin, or dissolved in plasma. - **Regulation**: The respiratory center in the medulla oblongata controls breathing rate based on CO₂ levels (chemoreceptors). Common NEET topics include lung volumes (tidal volume, vital capacity), disorders like asthma or emphysema, and the role of surfactants in preventing alveolar collapse.

Excretory System

The excretory system removes metabolic waste products and maintains osmotic balance. The primary organ is the kidney, which filters blood to produce urine. Key components and processes: - **Kidney Structure**: Nephrons are the functional units, consisting of the glomerulus, Bowman’s capsule, proximal convoluted tubule (PCT), loop of Henle, distal convoluted tubule (DCT), and collecting duct. - **Urine Formation**: Involves filtration (glomerulus), reabsorption (PCT, loop of Henle, DCT), and secretion (DCT). - **Regulation**: Hormones like ADH (antidiuretic hormone) regulate water reabsorption, and aldosterone regulates sodium and potassium balance. - **Other Organs**: Skin (sweat), lungs (CO₂), and liver (urea formation) also contribute to excretion. NEET questions often focus on the counter-current mechanism in the loop of Henle, disorders like kidney stones or renal failure, and the role of dialysis.

Nervous System

The nervous system coordinates voluntary and involuntary actions and transmits signals between different body parts. It is divided into: - **Central Nervous System (CNS)**: Brain and spinal cord. The brain includes the cerebrum (higher functions), cerebellum (balance), and brainstem (vital functions). - **Peripheral Nervous System (PNS)**: Cranial and spinal nerves. Subdivided into somatic (voluntary) and autonomic (involuntary) systems. The autonomic system further divides into sympathetic (fight-or-flight) and parasympathetic (rest-and-digest) systems. Key processes: - **Neurons**: Functional units of the nervous system. Structure includes dendrites, cell body, axon, and synaptic terminals. Transmission occurs via action potentials (electrical) and neurotransmitters (chemical). - **Synapse**: Junction between neurons where neurotransmitters (e.g., acetylcholine, dopamine) are released. - **Reflex Action**: Rapid, involuntary response to stimuli (e.g., knee-jerk reflex). NEET often tests neurotransmitter functions, brain regions, and disorders like Parkinson’s disease or Alzheimer’s.

Endocrine System

The endocrine system regulates physiological processes via hormones, which are chemical messengers secreted by endocrine glands. Key glands and hormones include: - **Pituitary Gland**: Master gland; secretes growth hormone (GH), ACTH, TSH, FSH, LH, prolactin, and oxytocin. - **Thyroid Gland**: Produces thyroxine (T₄) and triiodothyronine (T₃) for metabolism regulation. Iodine deficiency causes goiter. - **Adrenal Glands**: Secrete adrenaline (epinephrine) and cortisol (stress response). - **Pancreas**: Produces insulin (lowers blood glucose) and glucagon (raises blood glucose). Diabetes mellitus results from insulin deficiency or resistance. - **Gonads**: Testes (testosterone) and ovaries (estrogen, progesterone). Hormonal regulation often involves feedback loops (e.g., hypothalamic-pituitary-thyroid axis). NEET questions focus on hormone functions, disorders (e.g., gigantism, hypothyroidism), and feedback mechanisms.

Muscular and Skeletal Systems

The musculoskeletal system provides structural support, enables movement, and protects organs. - **Skeletal System**: Composed of bones, cartilage, and ligaments. Functions include support, protection, mineral storage (calcium, phosphorus), and blood cell production (bone marrow). Types of bones include long (femur), short (carpals), flat (skull), and irregular (vertebrae). - **Muscular System**: Three types of muscles: - **Skeletal Muscle**: Voluntary, striated, attached to bones (e.g., biceps). Contraction is via sliding filament mechanism (actin and myosin). - **Smooth Muscle**: Involuntary, non-striated, found in organs (e.g., stomach, blood vessels). - **Cardiac Muscle**: Involuntary, striated, found in the heart (intercalated discs facilitate synchronized contraction). NEET topics include muscle contraction physiology, bone disorders (e.g., osteoporosis), and joint types (e.g., ball-and-socket, hinge).

Reproductive System

The reproductive system ensures the production of gametes and the continuation of the species. It differs in males and females: - **Male Reproductive System**: Includes testes (produce sperm and testosterone), epididymis, vas deferens, seminal vesicles, prostate gland, and urethra. Spermatogenesis occurs in seminiferous tubules. - **Female Reproductive System**: Includes ovaries (produce ova and estrogen/progesterone), fallopian tubes, uterus, cervix, and vagina. Oogenesis occurs in follicles. The menstrual cycle (28 days) involves follicular phase, ovulation, luteal phase, and menstruation. Fertilization occurs in the fallopian tube, and implantation occurs in the uterus. NEET questions often focus on gametogenesis, hormonal regulation (e.g., FSH, LH, estrogen, progesterone), and reproductive disorders (e.g., PCOS, infertility).

Integration and Regulation of Systems

Human physiology relies on the integration of multiple systems to maintain homeostasis. Examples include: - **Exercise**: Increases heart rate (circulatory), breathing rate (respiratory), and muscle activity (muscular). The nervous system coordinates these responses. - **Stress Response**: The hypothalamus activates the sympathetic nervous system and adrenal glands (adrenaline and cortisol release). - **Blood Glucose Regulation**: Insulin (pancreas) lowers blood glucose, while glucagon raises it. The liver stores and releases glucose as needed. NEET often tests these integrative concepts, requiring aspirants to understand how systems interact to respond to stimuli or maintain balance.

Important facts

  • Homeostasis is maintained through negative feedback loops (e.g., thermoregulation, blood glucose levels).
  • The small intestine is the primary site for nutrient absorption due to its villi and microvilli.
  • Hemoglobin in red blood cells binds oxygen reversibly, forming oxyhemoglobin.
  • The SA node in the heart acts as the natural pacemaker, initiating the cardiac cycle.
  • Alveoli are the sites of gas exchange in the lungs, with a large surface area for diffusion.
  • Nephrons are the functional units of the kidney, responsible for filtration, reabsorption, and secretion.
  • The hypothalamus links the nervous and endocrine systems via the pituitary gland.
  • Skeletal muscles contract via the sliding filament mechanism involving actin and myosin.
  • The menstrual cycle is regulated by hormones like FSH, LH, estrogen, and progesterone.
  • The sympathetic nervous system triggers the 'fight-or-flight' response, while the parasympathetic system promotes 'rest-and-digest'.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing pulmonary circulation (heart to lungs) with systemic circulation (heart to body).
  • Misidentifying the roles of insulin and glucagon in blood glucose regulation (insulin lowers, glucagon raises).
  • Overlooking the difference between mechanical and chemical digestion (e.g., chewing vs. enzymatic breakdown).
  • Assuming all hormones are proteins (e.g., steroid hormones like estrogen and testosterone are lipids).
  • Forgetting that the loop of Henle is crucial for water reabsorption and urine concentration.
  • Mixing up the functions of the cerebrum (higher brain functions) and cerebellum (balance and coordination).
  • Ignoring the role of surfactants in preventing alveolar collapse in the lungs.
  • Misunderstanding the difference between voluntary (skeletal) and involuntary (smooth, cardiac) muscles.
  • Confusing spermatogenesis (male) with oogenesis (female) in terms of gamete production.
  • Overlooking the role of feedback loops in hormonal regulation (e.g., thyroid hormone regulation).
