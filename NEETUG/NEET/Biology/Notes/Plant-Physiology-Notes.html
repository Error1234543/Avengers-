Plant Physiology - NEET Biology Notes

NEETEnglishNotes

Introduction to Plant Physiology

Plant physiology is the study of how plants function, including the processes that enable them to grow, develop, reproduce, and respond to their environment. It encompasses various sub-disciplines such as photosynthesis, respiration, mineral nutrition, transport in plants, plant growth and development, and plant hormones. For NEET, understanding these processes at a molecular, cellular, and organismal level is crucial, as questions often test conceptual clarity and application rather than rote memorization. Plant physiology is a high-weightage topic in NEET, typically contributing 8-10 questions annually, with a focus on mechanisms, comparisons, and experimental interpretations.

Photosynthesis: The Process and Its Significance

Photosynthesis is the biological process by which green plants, algae, and some bacteria convert light energy into chemical energy stored in glucose or other organic compounds. It occurs in the chloroplasts of plant cells, primarily in the mesophyll cells of leaves. The overall equation for photosynthesis is: 6CO₂ + 12H₂O + Light Energy → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ + 6H₂O. The process is divided into two stages: the light-dependent reactions (occurring in the thylakoid membranes) and the Calvin cycle (occurring in the stroma). Key components include chlorophyll (the primary pigment), photosystems I and II, electron transport chain, ATP synthase, and the enzyme RuBisCO (Ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase). NEET often tests the differences between C₃, C₄, and CAM pathways, the role of RuBisCO in photorespiration, and the factors affecting the rate of photosynthesis (light intensity, CO₂ concentration, temperature, and water availability).

Respiration in Plants: Aerobic and Anaerobic Pathways

Respiration is the process by which plants oxidize glucose and other organic compounds to release energy in the form of ATP. It occurs in the mitochondria of plant cells and involves three main stages: glycolysis (cytoplasm), the Krebs cycle (mitochondrial matrix), and the electron transport chain (inner mitochondrial membrane). The overall equation for aerobic respiration is: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + Energy (36-38 ATP). Anaerobic respiration, or fermentation, occurs in the absence of oxygen and results in the production of ethanol and CO₂ in plants (alcoholic fermentation) or lactic acid in some bacteria. Key differences between photosynthesis and respiration are frequently tested in NEET, such as their occurrence in different organelles, energy transformation (light to chemical vs. chemical to ATP), and their roles in the carbon cycle. The Pasteur effect, respiratory quotient (RQ), and factors affecting respiration (temperature, oxygen concentration, and substrate availability) are also important.

Mineral Nutrition in Plants

Plants require essential minerals for growth, development, and reproduction. These minerals are classified into macronutrients (required in larger quantities, e.g., nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium, magnesium, and sulfur) and micronutrients (required in trace amounts, e.g., iron, manganese, zinc, copper, boron, molybdenum, and chlorine). Deficiency symptoms of these nutrients are often tested in NEET, such as chlorosis (yellowing of leaves due to nitrogen or magnesium deficiency), necrosis (death of tissue due to calcium or potassium deficiency), and stunted growth (due to phosphorus deficiency). The role of nitrogen-fixing bacteria (e.g., Rhizobium in legumes) and mycorrhizal associations in enhancing nutrient uptake is also significant. Hydroponics and aeroponics are experimental techniques used to study mineral nutrition, and their principles are occasionally tested.

Transport in Plants: Water, Minerals, and Food

Transport in plants involves the movement of water, minerals, and food (sucrose) through specialized tissues. Water and minerals are absorbed by root hairs and transported upward through the xylem via the transpiration-cohesion-tension mechanism. Key concepts include root pressure, guttation, transpiration (its types, factors affecting it, and its significance), and the ascent of sap. Food is transported through the phloem via the pressure-flow hypothesis, involving source (e.g., leaves) and sink (e.g., roots, fruits) tissues. NEET questions often focus on the differences between xylem and phloem, the role of stomata in transpiration, and the experimental demonstration of transport pathways (e.g., ringing experiments). The apoplast and symplast pathways for water movement are also important.

Plant Growth and Development

Plant growth is the irreversible increase in size and mass, while development refers to the progression of a plant through its life cycle, including seed germination, vegetative growth, flowering, fruiting, and senescence. Growth is measured in terms of increase in length (primary growth) or girth (secondary growth). The phases of growth include the lag phase, log phase (exponential growth), and stationary phase. Growth is influenced by genetic factors and environmental conditions such as light, temperature, water, and nutrients. NEET often tests the differences between determinate and indeterminate growth, the role of meristems (apical, lateral, and intercalary), and the concept of plasticity in plant development.

Plant Hormones: Types and Functions

Plant hormones (or phytohormones) are chemical messengers that regulate growth, development, and responses to environmental stimuli. The five major classes of plant hormones are: 1. **Auxins**: Promote cell elongation, root initiation, and apical dominance. Indole-3-acetic acid (IAA) is the most common auxin. 2. **Gibberellins**: Stimulate stem elongation, seed germination, and flowering. They are used commercially to increase fruit size (e.g., in grapes). 3. **Cytokinins**: Promote cell division, delay senescence, and are involved in shoot initiation. 4. **Abscisic Acid (ABA)**: Inhibits growth, promotes seed dormancy, and helps plants respond to stress (e.g., drought). 5. **Ethylene**: A gaseous hormone that promotes fruit ripening, senescence, and abscission (leaf fall). NEET questions often focus on the practical applications of these hormones (e.g., use of auxins in rooting powders, gibberellins in brewing, and ethylene in fruit ripening), their sites of synthesis, and their interactions (e.g., auxin-cytokinin ratio in tissue culture).

Photoperiodism and Vernalization

Photoperiodism is the response of plants to the relative lengths of daylight and darkness, which regulates flowering. Plants are classified into three groups based on their photoperiodic requirements: 1. **Short-day plants (SDP)**: Flower when the day length is shorter than a critical period (e.g., chrysanthemums, rice). 2. **Long-day plants (LDP)**: Flower when the day length exceeds a critical period (e.g., spinach, wheat). 3. **Day-neutral plants (DNP)**: Flower regardless of day length (e.g., tomato, cucumber). Vernalization is the process by which exposure to prolonged cold temperatures promotes flowering in certain plants (e.g., winter wheat). The hormone florigen is hypothesized to be involved in photoperiodic induction of flowering. NEET questions often test the differences between SDP, LDP, and DNP, the role of phytochrome in photoperiodism, and the practical applications of vernalization in agriculture.

Plant Movements: Tropisms and Nastic Movements

Plant movements are responses to external stimuli and are classified into tropisms (directional movements) and nastic movements (non-directional movements). Tropisms include: 1. **Phototropism**: Growth toward (positive) or away from (negative) light, mediated by auxins. 2. **Geotropism/Gravitropism**: Growth in response to gravity (e.g., roots show positive geotropism, shoots show negative geotropism). 3. **Hydrotropism**: Growth toward water. 4. **Thigmotropism**: Growth in response to touch (e.g., tendrils of climbing plants). Nastic movements include: 1. **Nyctinasty**: Sleep movements of leaves (e.g., in legumes). 2. **Thigmonasty**: Rapid movements in response to touch (e.g., Mimosa pudica). 3. **Seismonasty**: Movements in response to mechanical shock (e.g., Venus flytrap). NEET questions often focus on the mechanisms of these movements, the role of hormones (e.g., auxins in phototropism), and the differences between tropisms and nastic movements.

Seed Germination and Dormancy

Seed germination is the process by which a seed develops into a new plant. It involves three main stages: imbibition (water absorption), activation of enzymes, and growth of the embryo. The conditions required for germination include water, oxygen, and a suitable temperature. Seed dormancy is a state in which seeds are unable to germinate even under favorable conditions, ensuring that germination occurs at the optimal time. Dormancy can be caused by: 1. **Physical dormancy**: Hard seed coat preventing water uptake (e.g., in legumes). 2. **Physiological dormancy**: Internal factors such as immature embryos or the presence of germination inhibitors (e.g., abscisic acid). 3. **Morphological dormancy**: Underdeveloped embryos. NEET questions often test the types of dormancy, methods to break dormancy (e.g., scarification, stratification), and the role of hormones (e.g., gibberellins in promoting germination, abscisic acid in maintaining dormancy).

Important facts

  • Photosynthesis occurs in the chloroplasts and involves two stages: light-dependent reactions (thylakoid membranes) and the Calvin cycle (stroma).
  • RuBisCO is the most abundant enzyme on Earth and plays a dual role in photosynthesis (carboxylation) and photorespiration (oxygenation).
  • The C₄ pathway minimizes photorespiration by spatially separating initial CO₂ fixation (mesophyll cells) and the Calvin cycle (bundle-sheath cells).
  • Aerobic respiration produces 36-38 ATP per glucose molecule, while anaerobic respiration produces only 2 ATP.
  • Nitrogen is a macronutrient essential for amino acid and protein synthesis; its deficiency causes chlorosis in older leaves.
  • The transpiration-cohesion-tension mechanism explains the ascent of sap in the xylem, driven by transpiration pull.
  • Auxins promote apical dominance, while cytokinins promote lateral bud growth, demonstrating their antagonistic roles.
  • Ethylene is the only gaseous plant hormone and is involved in fruit ripening and senescence.
  • Short-day plants flower when the night length exceeds a critical period, while long-day plants flower when the night length is shorter than a critical period.
  • Seed dormancy ensures germination occurs under favorable conditions and can be broken by scarification (physical dormancy) or stratification (physiological dormancy).

Common mistakes

  • Confusing the sites of light-dependent reactions (thylakoid membranes) and the Calvin cycle (stroma) in photosynthesis.
  • Assuming that oxygen released during photosynthesis comes from CO₂; it actually comes from water during photolysis.
  • Overlooking the role of RuBisCO in photorespiration, which reduces the efficiency of photosynthesis in C₃ plants.
  • Misidentifying the respiratory quotient (RQ) for different substrates (e.g., RQ = 1 for carbohydrates, RQ < 1 for fats, RQ > 1 for organic acids).
  • Forgetting that micronutrients are required in trace amounts and their deficiency symptoms often appear in younger leaves (e.g., iron deficiency causes interveinal chlorosis).
  • Confusing the apoplast pathway (through cell walls and intercellular spaces) with the symplast pathway (through plasmodesmata) for water transport in roots.
  • Assuming that all plant movements are tropisms; nastic movements are non-directional and not influenced by the direction of the stimulus.
  • Overlooking the role of abscisic acid in seed dormancy and stress responses, often focusing only on its inhibitory effects on growth.
  • Misidentifying the photoperiodic requirements of plants (e.g., assuming short-day plants flower in short days, when it is actually the length of the night that matters).
  • Forgetting that vernalization requires exposure to cold temperatures and is distinct from photoperiodism.
