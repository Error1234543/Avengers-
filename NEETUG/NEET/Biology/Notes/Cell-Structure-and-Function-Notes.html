Cell Structure and Function - NEET Biology Notes

NEETEnglishNotes

Introduction to the Cell

The cell is the fundamental structural and functional unit of all living organisms. Cells were first observed by Robert Hooke in 1665, who coined the term 'cell' after examining cork under a microscope. Later, Schleiden and Schwann proposed the Cell Theory, which states that: (1) All living organisms are composed of one or more cells, (2) The cell is the basic unit of life, and (3) All cells arise from pre-existing cells (added by Rudolf Virchow). Cells are broadly classified into two types: prokaryotic cells (lacking a defined nucleus and membrane-bound organelles, e.g., bacteria) and eukaryotic cells (possessing a true nucleus and membrane-bound organelles, e.g., plant and animal cells). NEET frequently tests the differences between these two types, including their size, genetic material organization, and presence of organelles like mitochondria and chloroplasts.

Structure of the Cell Membrane

The cell membrane, also known as the plasma membrane, is a selectively permeable phospholipid bilayer that encloses the cell. It is composed of phospholipids, proteins, cholesterol, and carbohydrates. The fluid mosaic model, proposed by Singer and Nicolson, describes the membrane as a fluid structure with proteins embedded in or attached to the lipid bilayer. Key functions of the cell membrane include: (1) Regulating the movement of substances in and out of the cell, (2) Providing structural support and protection, (3) Facilitating cell signaling and communication, and (4) Maintaining electrochemical gradients. NEET often focuses on the roles of integral and peripheral proteins, glycoproteins, and glycolipids in cell recognition and transport processes.

Nucleus and Genetic Material

The nucleus is the control center of the eukaryotic cell, housing the genetic material (DNA). It is surrounded by a double membrane called the nuclear envelope, which contains nuclear pores for the exchange of materials (e.g., RNA and proteins) between the nucleus and cytoplasm. The nucleus contains nucleoplasm, chromatin (a complex of DNA and proteins), and one or more nucleoli, which are sites of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) synthesis and ribosome assembly. Chromatin can be further classified into euchromatin (loosely packed, transcriptionally active) and heterochromatin (densely packed, transcriptionally inactive). NEET questions often test the structure of chromosomes, the cell cycle (interphase and mitotic phase), and the significance of the nucleus in heredity and protein synthesis.

Cytoplasm and Cytoskeleton

The cytoplasm is the gel-like substance enclosed within the cell membrane, excluding the nucleus. It consists of the cytosol (the liquid component) and organelles. The cytoskeleton is a network of protein filaments (microfilaments, intermediate filaments, and microtubules) that provides structural support, maintains cell shape, and facilitates cell movement and intracellular transport. Microfilaments (actin filaments) are involved in cell motility and cytokinesis, intermediate filaments provide mechanical support, and microtubules (tubulin polymers) form the spindle fibers during cell division and serve as tracks for organelle movement. NEET emphasizes the role of the cytoskeleton in processes like mitosis, meiosis, and cell motility (e.g., cilia and flagella).

Mitochondria: The Powerhouse of the Cell

Mitochondria are double-membraned organelles known as the 'powerhouses' of the cell due to their role in ATP production through cellular respiration. The outer membrane is smooth, while the inner membrane is folded into cristae, increasing surface area for enzyme attachment. The mitochondrial matrix contains enzymes for the Krebs cycle, mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), and ribosomes. Mitochondria are semi-autonomous, as they possess their own genetic material and can replicate independently of the cell. NEET frequently tests the structure of mitochondria, their role in aerobic respiration, and the significance of mtDNA in inheritance (maternal inheritance pattern).

Endoplasmic Reticulum and Golgi Apparatus

The endoplasmic reticulum (ER) is a network of membranous tubules and sacs involved in protein and lipid synthesis. It is classified into two types: (1) Rough ER (RER), studded with ribosomes and involved in protein synthesis and modification, and (2) Smooth ER (SER), lacking ribosomes and involved in lipid synthesis, detoxification, and calcium storage. The Golgi apparatus, or Golgi complex, consists of flattened membranous sacs called cisternae. It modifies, sorts, and packages proteins and lipids received from the ER for secretion or delivery to other organelles. NEET questions often focus on the pathway of protein synthesis (RER → Golgi → vesicles) and the role of the Golgi in forming lysosomes and secretory vesicles.

Lysosomes and Peroxisomes

Lysosomes are membrane-bound organelles containing hydrolytic enzymes that digest macromolecules, old organelles, and foreign substances (e.g., bacteria). They are formed by the Golgi apparatus and are involved in autophagy (self-digestion of damaged organelles) and apoptosis (programmed cell death). Peroxisomes are small organelles containing oxidative enzymes (e.g., catalase) that break down fatty acids and detoxify harmful substances like hydrogen peroxide. NEET emphasizes the role of lysosomes in intracellular digestion and the significance of peroxisomes in metabolism and detoxification.

Ribosomes and Protein Synthesis

Ribosomes are non-membranous organelles composed of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and proteins. They are the sites of protein synthesis and can be found free in the cytoplasm or attached to the rough ER. Ribosomes consist of two subunits: a large subunit and a small subunit, which come together during translation. The process of protein synthesis involves transcription (DNA → mRNA in the nucleus) and translation (mRNA → protein at the ribosome). NEET frequently tests the structure of ribosomes, their role in translation, and the differences between prokaryotic (70S) and eukaryotic (80S) ribosomes.

Chloroplasts and Photosynthesis

Chloroplasts are double-membraned organelles found in plant cells and algae, responsible for photosynthesis. They contain chlorophyll, a green pigment that captures light energy. The structure of a chloroplast includes: (1) The outer membrane, (2) The inner membrane, (3) The intermembrane space, (4) The stroma (site of the Calvin cycle), and (5) Thylakoids (flattened sacs stacked into grana, where the light-dependent reactions occur). Chloroplasts are semi-autonomous, possessing their own DNA and ribosomes. NEET questions often focus on the structure of chloroplasts, the stages of photosynthesis (light-dependent and light-independent reactions), and the significance of chlorophyll and accessory pigments.

Cell Wall and Vacuoles

The cell wall is a rigid, non-living layer found in plant cells, fungi, and some prokaryotes. In plants, it is primarily composed of cellulose and provides structural support, protection, and shape to the cell. The cell wall is permeable, allowing the passage of water and dissolved substances. Vacuoles are membrane-bound sacs found in plant and animal cells, with larger central vacuoles in plant cells. They store nutrients, waste products, and pigments, and help maintain turgor pressure in plant cells. NEET emphasizes the differences between plant and animal cells, particularly the presence of cell walls and large central vacuoles in plants.

Cell Junctions and Communication

Cell junctions are specialized structures that facilitate communication and adhesion between cells. In animal cells, three main types of junctions exist: (1) Tight junctions (prevent leakage of substances between cells), (2) Desmosomes (provide mechanical strength and anchor cells together), and (3) Gap junctions (allow direct communication between cells via channels). In plant cells, plasmodesmata are cytoplasmic channels that connect adjacent cells, enabling the transport of substances and communication. NEET questions often test the functions of these junctions and their roles in maintaining tissue integrity and coordination.

Cell Cycle and Cell Division

The cell cycle is the series of events that take place in a cell leading to its division and duplication. It consists of two main phases: interphase (G1, S, and G2 phases) and the mitotic phase (mitosis and cytokinesis). Mitosis is the process of nuclear division, resulting in two genetically identical daughter cells, and is divided into prophase, metaphase, anaphase, and telophase. Meiosis is a reductional division that produces four genetically distinct haploid cells (gametes) from a diploid parent cell. NEET frequently tests the stages of mitosis and meiosis, the significance of checkpoints in the cell cycle, and the differences between mitosis and meiosis (e.g., chromosome number, genetic variation).

Important facts

  • Prokaryotic cells lack a defined nucleus and membrane-bound organelles, while eukaryotic cells possess both.
  • The fluid mosaic model describes the cell membrane as a dynamic structure with embedded proteins.
  • Mitochondria and chloroplasts are semi-autonomous organelles with their own DNA and ribosomes.
  • The rough ER is involved in protein synthesis, while the smooth ER is involved in lipid synthesis and detoxification.
  • Lysosomes contain hydrolytic enzymes for intracellular digestion, while peroxisomes contain oxidative enzymes for detoxification.
  • Ribosomes are the sites of protein synthesis and can be free or attached to the rough ER.
  • Chloroplasts are responsible for photosynthesis and contain chlorophyll in thylakoid membranes.
  • The cell wall is present in plant cells, fungi, and some prokaryotes, providing structural support.
  • Gap junctions in animal cells and plasmodesmata in plant cells facilitate intercellular communication.
  • Mitosis results in two genetically identical diploid cells, while meiosis produces four genetically distinct haploid cells.

Common mistakes

  • Confusing prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells, particularly their size, genetic material organization, and organelles.
  • Misidentifying the roles of rough ER and smooth ER in protein and lipid synthesis.
  • Overlooking the semi-autonomous nature of mitochondria and chloroplasts, including their own DNA and ribosomes.
  • Mixing up the stages of mitosis and meiosis, especially the chromosome number and genetic variation outcomes.
  • Ignoring the differences between lysosomes and peroxisomes in terms of enzyme content and functions.
  • Forgetting that plant cells have both chloroplasts and mitochondria, while animal cells only have mitochondria.
  • Misunderstanding the fluid mosaic model of the cell membrane, particularly the roles of proteins and lipids.
  • Confusing the functions of tight junctions, desmosomes, and gap junctions in animal cells.
  • Overlooking the role of plasmodesmata in plant cell communication.
  • Failing to recognize the significance of checkpoints in the cell cycle for regulating cell division.
